# Dead Letter Office

The first letter arrived with a tooth inside it.

Ada tipped it onto the sorting desk. Small, white, a child's milk tooth, with a thread of pink caught at the root. The envelope bore an ordinary second-class stamp and a cancellation from a town that had disappeared during the Six Bells.

It was addressed to her.

Not to the office, not to her predecessor, whose name still showed through the paint on the door. To Ada Marr, in the careful block capitals her father had taught her when he could no longer trust his own hands.

Inside, on a page torn from an exercise book, someone had written:

*You said I should send proof. I am sorry about the blood. Please don't let them close the window.*

Below that was a name: Ruth.

Ada had no children.

She checked the envelope again. Across its back, beneath the flap, a date had been impressed so deeply that it had nearly cut the paper.

Seventeen years from next Tuesday.

***

The Committee had given her the dead letters because she had once worked in a library.

They had confused an acquaintance with paper for a willingness to be responsible for it. By the time Ada explained the difference, they had assigned her a desk, two keys and a stove whose chimney opened into a room nobody could find.

The office occupied the ground floor of a former building society. Above it, a family lived in three rooms and occasionally a fourth. Every Thursday they sent down a plate of potatoes as rent. Ada accepted the potatoes and never entered them in the accounts.

Since the Bells, people addressed letters to things they had lost. Husbands. Streets. The taste of coffee. A woman in the northern district wrote every week to the afternoon before her son died. Ada returned those envelopes when she could find the woman and filed them when she couldn't.

Nothing had ever written back.

By noon, nine more letters lay beside the tooth.

They came through the public posting slot one at a time. Ada put a chair against the outside door and watched. No fingers appeared. The metal flap lifted inward, paused as though someone were holding it against the wind, and dropped a blue envelope onto the mat.

Beyond the glass, a man was repairing a bicycle.

She opened the envelope.

*Dear Mum. We haven't met yet. Please don't be frightened by what the inspector tells you.*

Ada locked the door.

Then she went upstairs.

The woman there, Mrs Vale, listened without touching the letters. Her youngest boy stood behind her skirt, looking at the tooth.

"Have you answered?" Mrs Vale asked.

"No."

"Good. Keep it that way until you know whether they want feeding."

Ada had expected advice about the Committee. This was better advice, and worse.

"What if they're children?"

Mrs Vale looked towards the ceiling. Somewhere overhead, in the room that came and went, a marble rolled slowly across the boards.

"Then somebody ought to be looking after them."

***

The inspector arrived before Ada sent for him.

His name was Bellamy, which he acknowledged only once and with visible weariness. He had a leather case and the expression of a man who had been asked to count the sea using a teaspoon.

He read the letters through a sheet of clear material.

"What is that?"

"A precaution."

"Against what?"

"We'll know if it works."

By then there were thirty-one envelopes.

Bellamy arranged them by date. The earliest came from seven years ahead. The latest had no year at all, only the words *AFTER THE LAST WINTER*.

They were all from children.

Most asked for practical things. A repaired step. A red coat. Someone to remember a dog called Pigeon. One contained three pages of arithmetic with a note asking Ada to check the answers.

Bellamy stopped at Ruth's letter.

"Do you recognise the handwriting?"

"No."

"Any possibility of pregnancy?"

"No."

He nodded without embarrassment and wrote something down.

"You think this is a trick."

"I think the world has acquired ways of making a person consent before they understand the request."

He took a little brass ruler from his case. Near the posting slot, its markings blurred.

Ada watched him measure the wall.

"There's an opening," he said. "Not large. Not stable."

"To where?"

"Where is a forgiving word."

He asked to see the cellar.

The stairs ended three steps early. They had always done that. Ada lowered herself onto the packed earth and held the lamp while Bellamy examined the old parcel cage.

It was full of envelopes.

They lay in pale heaps against the wire, moving slightly, like leaves disturbed by animals underneath. A child's hand pressed through the mesh. It was made of folded paper.

Ada dropped the lamp.

Bellamy caught it by the handle.

The hand withdrew.

From inside the cage, a girl said, "You're early."

***

Her name was Ruth. She was eleven, and she knew Ada disliked coriander, slept on her left side, and had a scar beneath her chin.

Those facts proved nothing. Ada's own reflection had once told her a secret it could not possibly know, and then asked permission to come indoors.

"Who is your mother?" Bellamy asked.

"You've got her upstairs."

"Who is your father?"

Ruth was silent.

"Who sent you?"

"Nobody sent us."

The envelopes rustled. Ada could hear other voices now, whispering arguments and names. The sound was intimate enough to make her feel she had entered a dormitory after lights out.

Ruth said, "They closed the school."

"What school?"

"The one where you work."

"I work here."

"I know. Later you work at the school."

Bellamy opened his case.

Ruth's voice sharpened.

"Don't use the black tape."

He stopped.

"Why?"

"Because then I don't know you anymore."

Ada crouched by the wire.

"What happens if he uses it?"

"The letters stop."

"Is that all?"

A pause.

"That's all you'll see."

***

Bellamy wanted the room cleared.

Ada wanted him to explain himself first.

They stood upstairs while the posting flap lifted and fell, lifted and fell. He had put the letters into a biscuit tin. There were too many now to close the lid.

"Possibility has weight," he said. "Under ordinary circumstances, not here. Not yet. But the boundaries are damaged."

"She's a possibility?"

"She may be. She may be something wearing one."

"Can you tell?"

"No."

That answer made her trust him a little.

He spread a sheet across her desk. It was a diagram of the cellar, but there was another outline inside it, a narrow room with seventeen small circles.

"A nursery?"

"A holding space."

"Who made it?"

"I don't know."

"You're making a habit of that."

"I'd rather you noticed it than mistook a diagram for knowledge."

He showed her the margin. Beside the outline, numbers had been written and crossed out. Seventeen. Twenty-two. Thirty-one.

"They're becoming more definite. Someone at this end is paying attention."

"Me."

"Yes."

"What happens when they're definite?"

He looked at the ceiling.

Above them, Mrs Vale dropped a pan. A boy cried out, then stopped.

Bellamy went very still.

Ada ran upstairs.

Mrs Vale was kneeling in the corridor. Her youngest boy stood beside her. The older one, Daniel, was gone.

Where his bedroom door had been, there was a cupboard filled with paper.

***

They found him in the fourth room.

He was sitting on the floor with his knees against his chest. His face was grey. He had bitten through his lower lip.

He said the corridor had become very long.

At its far end, a woman had been calling a name he didn't know. She had sounded annoyed, not monstrous. As though supper was getting cold.

When he had turned back, his mother had been standing in the wrong doorway.

"Wrong how?" Bellamy asked.

Daniel looked at her.

Mrs Vale put a hand over his eyes.

"Enough."

Bellamy agreed.

Downstairs, he put a strip of black tape across the posting slot.

The noise in the cellar ceased.

Upstairs, somebody began crying.

It was a thin, animal sound. Ada went to the stairs and heard Mrs Vale trying to soothe her younger boy.

He was asking who Daniel was.

***

Ada sat beside the biscuit tin until dark.

Bellamy had gone to arrange a seal. He promised to return before morning. He left the brass ruler behind, perhaps accidentally.

The tin lid rose a fraction.

A folded corner pushed through.

Ada pulled the envelope free.

*He isn't gone. They have to make room. Tell him to keep saying his name.*

Below that, in a smaller hand:

*I didn't mean to.*

She went upstairs.

Daniel was asleep with his mother holding his wrist. The younger boy watched them both with solemn suspicion.

Ada told Mrs Vale what the letter said.

Mrs Vale did not ask where she had got it.

"Daniel Vale," she said.

Her sleeping son stirred.

"Daniel Vale."

The younger boy frowned.

Then, very quietly, he joined in.

***

At three in the morning, Ada peeled back one corner of the tape.

The posting flap opened.

A white finger came through.

It had a split nail, a little crescent of dirt beneath it, a healing scratch across the knuckle. Not paper now.

"Can I talk to you?" Ruth asked.

Ada sat on the floor.

"Are you really my daughter?"

"I don't know what really means from here."

"Tell me something you remember."

"You made us put our shoes on before you would look at anything. Even if it was important. You said people who ran barefoot towards a noise were how the world got into this state."

Ada shut her eyes.

"I sound difficult."

"You were sometimes."

"What happened at the school?"

"We were waiting for you to come back."

"From where?"

"Here."

Ruth pushed something through the slot.

A photograph.

Seventeen children stood on the steps of a low brick building. At one side, older and broader, Ada shaded her eyes against the sun.

On the back she recognised her own handwriting.

*First day. Nobody lost.*

She looked at the children until faces began to shift at the edges.

"Ruth. Is anybody else there? An adult?"

"Not anymore."

"What happened to them?"

"They couldn't remember whose turn it was to stay."

***

Bellamy returned at dawn carrying a roll of tape in a wooden box.

Ada gave him the photograph.

He studied it for a long time.

"This could be an inducement."

"Yes."

"It could be accurate and still be an inducement."

"I know."

He sat down.

For the first time, she noticed that his shoes had been resoled with pieces of different tyres.

She asked whether sealing the opening would kill them.

"I don't know if kill is the right word."

"Then choose a word you can defend."

He turned the photograph over.

"Prevent."

Ada thought of the woman who wrote to an afternoon. Of all the envelopes that had never asked anything back.

"There has to be another way."

"There may be. It won't necessarily be a kinder one."

She fetched the ruler.

Together they measured the photograph.

The seventeen children occupied a space smaller than a postage stamp. The brass markings writhed when they crossed Ada's figure.

Bellamy measured Ruth's letter, then the tooth.

Finally he measured the old employment form pinned behind the desk.

"What?" Ada asked.

He looked sick.

"You're the address."

***

They explained it in the cellar because Ruth insisted on hearing.

The room on the other side was borrowing certainty from the life in which Ada had become their teacher. Every letter delivered pulled that life closer. But there was no empty place for it here.

It would overwrite things.

A bedroom first. A brother next. Eventually an entire street might discover it had always been somewhere else.

Ruth listened without interrupting.

Then she said, "We could stop writing."

"You already exist enough to need somewhere," Bellamy said.

"Could we go somewhere different?"

"Not while the address is hers."

Ada held the employment form between finger and thumb.

It was only paper. Her signature ran across its bottom in the hurried script she used for obligations she expected to be temporary.

"Can we change it?"

Bellamy didn't answer quickly.

"An address can receive on behalf of someone else," he said at last.

"Care of."

"Yes."

"So give them the office."

"The office isn't a person."

"Does it need to be?"

Ruth said, "It has rooms."

The paper in the cage shifted.

For a moment Ada could smell floor polish and wet coats.

Bellamy knelt beside her.

"It needs a custodian," he said. "Someone to keep the boundary. To accept them as real without insisting that their whole past becomes ours."

"What would that cost?"

"Your place in the life they're borrowing."

Ada looked at the photograph.

The woman on the steps had a hand resting on Ruth's shoulder.

She had thought, until that moment, that she was being asked to rescue strangers.

***

Mrs Vale came down when they told her.

She brought tea, potatoes and Daniel, who refused to stay upstairs.

Bellamy wrote the terms on an unused counter receipt. He crossed out anything he could not explain in ordinary language.

Ada would keep the office.

The children would have access through it.

Their former history would not replace the present.

Ada would surrender the claim that they were hers.

"Will I remember them?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Will they remember me?"

Ruth answered from the cage.

"I think we'll remember somebody."

Mrs Vale put the tea down too hard.

"That isn't enough."

"No," Ada said.

She asked Ruth whether the children agreed.

There was an argument beyond the wire. Someone wanted to know whether there would still be breakfast. Someone else asked if the dog could come.

"Pigeon?" Ada said.

The argument stopped.

"You know him?"

"I've had a letter."

Ruth laughed, once, in surprise. It was the first sound she had made that belonged entirely to a child.

Ada signed the receipt.

Nothing happened.

Bellamy said, "You have to address it."

So she took a fresh envelope.

*The children at the school.*

*Care of the Dead Letter Office.*

She hesitated before the final line.

Then wrote:

*For collection.*

***

The cage door opened.

The first child through was carrying a saucepan. He was barefoot, and Ada almost told him off.

Behind him came a girl with a bandage over one eye, two boys arguing about a scarf, a small child asleep against an older child's shoulder.

Seventeen.

A dog limped out last.

Ruth came through holding a tooth wrapped in a handkerchief.

She looked directly at Ada.

For an instant, recognition brightened her face.

Then it passed.

"Are you the lady who runs this place?"

Ada felt Mrs Vale's hand close around her elbow.

"Yes."

"We were told to wait here."

"You can."

"Is she coming?"

Ada could not make herself answer.

Bellamy crouched beside Ruth.

"The person who was looking after you couldn't come through."

"Is she dead?"

"No," he said. "But she can't be who she was."

Ruth thought about that with a gravity Ada found almost unbearable.

"That happens," she said.

***

They put blankets in the old strongroom.

The children ate all the potatoes and most of the emergency biscuits. Pigeon slept beneath the sorting desk with his paws moving.

By noon, Daniel's bedroom door had returned. His younger brother complained about the noise and was rewarded with such fierce affection that he became suspicious again.

Bellamy stayed to help wash the cups.

"Will there be more?" Ada asked.

He looked at the posting slot.

"Possibly."

"That's going on the sign, then."

He smiled without much happiness.

Before he left, he placed the brass ruler in her hand.

"A precaution," he said.

That afternoon Ada amended the notice outside.

*Letters accepted.*

*Collection arranged where possible.*

*No guarantee of return.*

She made a second notice in larger writing.

*Please put your shoes on.*

***

Ruth found her at the desk after the others were asleep.

She had a stamped envelope and a pencil.

"Can you help me spell something?"

"Of course."

Ruth climbed onto the chair beside her.

"Custodian."

Ada spelled it.

Ruth wrote slowly. The shape of her letters was familiar, though Ada could no longer have said why.

"Is that what you are?"

"Apparently."

"Is it a job?"

"Mostly."

"Do you have to like us?"

"No."

Ruth considered this.

"That's good. Imran can be horrible before breakfast."

"So I've noticed."

The girl smiled, then bent over her page.

Ada waited.

When Ruth finished, she folded the letter and wrote on the envelope:

*To the person who came back.*

There was no address.

"Will it get there?" she asked.

Ada looked towards the cellar door. On its other side, seventeen coats hung on pegs that had not existed yesterday. Somewhere a child turned in sleep and murmured a name the world had made room to keep.

"I can try."

Ruth left the letter on the desk.

Ada waited until the girl had gone downstairs before opening it.

There were only six words.

*We got here. Nobody was lost.*

She read them once.

Then she took an ordinary stamp from the drawer and placed it carefully in the corner of the envelope.

For the first time since she had taken the job, she knew exactly where a letter belonged.
