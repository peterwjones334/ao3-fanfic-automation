The warehouse on the edge of the industrial district had gone quiet three hours before dawn. Kirishima’s right shoulder locked hard when he tried to push himself upright against the concrete wall. The sling the medics had fitted on-site cut across his chest and pinned the arm against his ribs. Bakugou crouched in front of him, gloves still smoking faintly at the knuckles, and did not offer a hand.

“Can you stand,” he said.

“Yeah.”

“Then stand.”

Kirishima stood. The motion sent a bright, narrow line of pain from the joint to the base of his neck. He kept his face still. Bakugou watched the stillness for a full second, then turned and walked toward the extraction point without looking back. Kirishima followed at a measured pace, one foot in front of the other, the sling creaking softly with each step.

The agency put him on restricted duty for ten days. The sling stayed on. Bakugou stopped appearing at the gym during the hours he had kept for years. On the second evening he appeared instead at Kirishima’s door with a paper bag that smelled of grilled chicken and rice. He did not wait for an invitation. He set the bag on the counter, washed his hands under the kitchen tap, and began unpacking with the same efficiency he used to clear a room.

“You’re early,” Kirishima said from the couch. The ice pack on his shoulder had already slid sideways and was dripping onto the cushion.

“You’re upright,” Bakugou answered. He crossed the room, lifted the pack, and settled it again with both hands. The pressure was exact, centered over the joint. “Eat. Then you can tell me you’re fine.”

They ate on the couch. Bakugou sat at the far end. Their knees did not touch. The television played a documentary about coastal rescue operations neither of them watched. When Kirishima’s chopsticks paused too long between bites, Bakugou’s eyes cut over and stayed until the next piece of chicken reached his mouth. He rinsed the containers, left them in the rack, and locked the door behind him on the way out.

On the third night he brought an extra container of steamed greens and a bottle of electrolyte solution he claimed he had found in the agency refrigerator. He sat closer. The couch dipped under his weight. Kirishima could feel the heat of him through the thin cotton of his shirt even with the space still between them. The pain in the shoulder had settled into a steady throb that matched the low volume of the television. He did not reach for the stronger pills the doctors had left on the counter. Bakugou watched him not reach.

“Take them,” he said.

“I’m managing.”

“You’re not.” Bakugou stood, filled a glass from the tap, and brought the bottle. He held it out. Their knuckles brushed when Kirishima took it. Bakugou did not pull his hand away until the transfer was finished. The contact left a small, precise warmth that lingered after the glass was set down. “I saw the hit. You stepped into it on purpose. So take the pills.”

Kirishima swallowed them. The water was cold enough to make his teeth ache. Bakugou sat again, closer than before. Their shoulders nearly touched. Neither of them moved the last few centimeters. The documentary narrator described a man who had waited three hours in open water. Neither of them looked at the screen.

On the fourth night the documentary ended before they finished eating. The street outside went quiet enough that the refrigerator’s hum filled the room. Kirishima’s head had grown heavy with the residual ache and the mild edge of the medication. It tipped, almost without permission, until it rested against Bakugou’s shoulder. Bakugou went still. Then he turned his head until his temple pressed back—a small, answering weight, deliberate enough that Kirishima felt the choice in it.

“You scared me,” he said into the dark.

Kirishima kept his eyes closed. “I know.”

“Don’t do it again.”

“Can’t promise that.”

“I know.” Bakugou’s hand found Kirishima’s good one on the cushion between them. He did not lace their fingers. He held on, thumb moving once across the knuckles in a careful line. “I’m still here.”

Kirishima turned his palm and held back. The grip was firm and unhurried, the way Bakugou held something he had already decided not to drop. Outside, a car passed. Headlights swept the ceiling in a brief arc and vanished. Inside, the lamp hummed. The ice pack had melted into lukewarm water against the sling. Neither of them moved to replace it.

They stayed like that until the television switched itself to static. Bakugou’s thumb continued its slow path across Kirishima’s knuckles. Kirishima counted the passes until the numbers blurred. When he spoke, his voice barely reached the space between them.

“You can stay,” he said. “If you want.”

Bakugou’s grip tightened once, then settled. “Yeah,” he answered. “I want.”

On the fifth night Bakugou arrived with the food and a change of clothes in a second bag. He did not explain the bag. He set it by the door and unpacked dinner. They ate. The documentary played. When Kirishima’s head tipped again, Bakugou was already there, shoulder angled to meet him. The hand found its place without either of them looking down. The static came on. Bakugou stayed until the light outside the window turned gray and the first buses started their routes.

On the sixth night Kirishima woke on the couch with a blanket over him that had not been there when he closed his eyes. Bakugou was in the kitchen, rinsing the containers from the night before. The sling had been adjusted while he slept; the straps sat flatter against his chest. The ice pack was fresh and properly placed. Bakugou did not turn around when he spoke.

“Shoulder’s less swollen,” he said. “Don’t push it.”

“I won’t.”

Bakugou dried his hands on a towel. He crossed the room and sat. The distance between them was gone. Their shoulders touched fully. Kirishima’s good hand rested on his own thigh. Bakugou covered it with his own and left it there, palm warm, fingers relaxed but present.

“I meant it,” he said. “I’m still here.”

Kirishima turned his hand under Bakugou’s and held on. The city outside stayed quiet. The lamp hummed. Neither of them moved to turn the television off. The static continued, soft and constant, while the light outside shifted from gray to pale gold.


On the seventh night the shoulder still ached, but the ache had dulled enough that Kirishima could lift a glass without the motion catching. Bakugou arrived with the usual bag and, this time, a small container of cut fruit he set on the table without comment. They ate. The documentary played. When the credits rolled, Bakugou muted the television instead of letting it run to static.

“How does it feel,” he asked.

“Better.”

“How much better.”

Kirishima considered the question. “I can sleep on my side for an hour before it wakes me.”

Bakugou nodded once. He reached for Kirishima’s good hand again. The motion was easier now, practiced. Their fingers fit without adjustment. Outside, rain began against the window, light at first, then steady. Neither of them spoke for a long stretch. The rain filled the room the way the static had.

“I keep seeing it,” Bakugou said eventually. “The hit. You moving. Me not fast enough.”

“You were fast enough. I’m here.”

“Barely.”

“Still here.”

Bakugou’s thumb moved across Kirishima’s knuckles, the same careful line as every night before. “Yeah,” he said. “You are.”

They sat with the rain and the muted television until the fruit was gone and the ice pack needed replacing. Bakugou stood, fetched a fresh one from the freezer, and settled it again with both hands. When he sat, he left no space between them. Kirishima leaned into the contact. Bakugou leaned back. The rain continued. The city stayed quiet. They stayed.


On the eighth night the rain had stopped. Bakugou brought the food and, without being asked, rewrapped the sling after dinner so the straps sat flatter. His hands were careful. Kirishima watched them work and did not look away when Bakugou finished and left one palm resting on the good shoulder for a moment longer than necessary.

“It still catches when I reach up,” Kirishima said.

“Then don’t reach up.”

“I have to eventually.”

“Eventually is not tonight.” Bakugou sat. Their shoulders touched. The hand found Kirishima’s again without either of them commenting on the habit. “You can take the time.”

“You keep coming back.”

“Yeah.”

“Why.”

Bakugou was quiet long enough that Kirishima thought he might not answer. Then he said, “Because I want to know you’re still here when I leave. And because I don’t want to leave yet.”

Kirishima turned his hand and laced their fingers properly for the first time. The fit was easy. Bakugou’s grip answered, firm and steady. Outside, the streetlights came on. Inside, the lamp stayed lit. They sat with the quiet and the contact until the ice pack warmed and the city outside settled into its night sounds. Neither of them moved to turn anything off.
