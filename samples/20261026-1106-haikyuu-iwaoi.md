The apartment above the clinic had thin walls and a view of the river. Iwaizumi kept the windows cracked even in October because the air inside smelled like antiseptic and the coffee he never finished. He was halfway through the morning’s charts when the knock came—three short raps, then a pause, then two more. Old habit.

Oikawa stood in the hallway with a duffel and a soft brace on his left knee. His hair was shorter than it had been the last time they saw each other, the ends still damp from the rain. He didn’t smile. “They said you had a room.”

“They said a lot of things,” Iwaizumi answered, but he stepped aside. The duffel went on the floor by the couch. Oikawa sat carefully, the brace making a small sound against the cushion. Outside, a delivery bike rattled past. Neither of them reached for the kettle.

They had not lived in the same city for six years. Oikawa’s contract in Argentina had been public; the meniscus tear that ended the season had been less so. Iwaizumi had seen the footage once, late, on a phone in the break room, and turned it off before the replay. He had not called. Oikawa had not either.

The first week was mostly schedules. Oikawa had appointments downstairs with the other therapist, the one who did not know him. Iwaizumi left early and came back late. They shared the kitchen without speaking much. Oikawa cooked rice and left half in the pot. Iwaizumi ate it standing up, then washed the bowl. At night the floorboards creaked when one of them moved. Iwaizumi learned the sound of Oikawa’s careful steps—weight on the right, pause, left—and stopped checking the clock.

On the eighth morning the rain stopped. Oikawa was on the floor of the living room with a resistance band, face tight, counting under his breath. The band slipped. He swore, low and precise, and stayed where he was, forehead against his forearm. Iwaizumi came out of the bathroom with a towel around his neck and watched for three seconds before he crossed the room.

“Here.” He took the band, looped it properly, set Oikawa’s foot against it. “Slow. Don’t chase the stretch.”

Oikawa’s jaw worked. He did the set. When it was finished he stayed on the floor, breathing through his nose. “You didn’t have to.”

“I know.”

That afternoon Iwaizumi canceled two late appointments and took the stairs instead of the elevator. He found Oikawa at the kitchen table with the brace off, ice pack balanced on the joint, reading the same page of a paperback for the fourth time. The page was dog-eared already. Iwaizumi put a mug down in front of him—tea, not coffee—and sat opposite.

“Tell me what the other guy said about load.”

Oikawa looked at the mug, then at him. “You could just ask him.”

“I’m asking you.”

They talked for twenty minutes. Oikawa kept his answers short until the ice started to melt and drip onto the table. Then the words came easier: the swelling that returned if he stood too long, the way the right side compensated until his hip ached, the email from the club that had used the phrase “extended absence” twice. Iwaizumi listened without writing anything down. When the ice pack was mostly water he stood, emptied it into the sink, and came back with a fresh one wrapped in a thin towel.

“Twenty minutes,” he said. “Then you walk the hallway. Not the stairs.”

Oikawa took the pack. Their fingers brushed. Neither of them moved for a moment. Outside, a boat horn sounded once on the river.

The second week Oikawa started making the rice with miso and leaving the pot on low. Iwaizumi began setting out two bowls instead of one. They still did not talk about the years. Oikawa asked once, carefully, about the clinic’s lease. Iwaizumi answered with the number of years left and the cost of the new ultrasound machine. He did not mention the nights he had spent reading papers on post-surgical protocols for elite athletes, or the folder on his desktop labeled with a single letter.

On a Thursday the power flickered and stayed off for three hours. They ate cold rice and the last of the pickled vegetables by the light of a phone propped against the wall. Oikawa’s knee was elevated on a stack of cushions. The screen cast blue across his face and the line of his collarbone where the shirt had slipped. Iwaizumi looked at the river instead—dark, with the lights of the opposite bank wavering in it—and said, “You can stay as long as you need.”

“I know.”

“I’m not—” Iwaizumi stopped. The phone dimmed. “I’m not doing this because of anything old.”

Oikawa was quiet long enough that the phone locked. In the dark he said, “Okay.”

The next morning the power was back and the kettle worked. Oikawa made tea for both of them and set Iwaizumi’s mug at the edge of the counter where he would find it on the way out. Iwaizumi drank half of it standing in the doorway of the clinic, the charts still under his arm, and went back up to rinse the mug before the first patient arrived.

They developed a rhythm that was not quite shared and not quite separate. Oikawa did his exercises in the living room while Iwaizumi finished notes at the table. Sometimes Iwaizumi corrected a form without looking up from the laptop. Sometimes Oikawa left a second portion of whatever he had cooked and went to bed before Iwaizumi came up. On the nights they were both awake past eleven, they sat on opposite ends of the couch with the television muted and the subtitles on. The river moved. The city made its small, constant noises. Neither of them filled the space with anything that had a name.

The first time Oikawa’s knee gave on the stairs, Iwaizumi was already at the bottom. He heard the sharp intake of breath and the hand hitting the rail. He took the steps two at a time, caught Oikawa under the elbow before the second step down, and kept his own body between Oikawa and the drop. They stayed like that while Oikawa’s breathing evened. The brace was on. The muscle still trembled under Iwaizumi’s palm.

“I’m fine,” Oikawa said.

“You’re not falling on my stairs.”

“They’re not your stairs.”

“They are while you’re here.”

Oikawa’s free hand closed briefly on Iwaizumi’s sleeve, then let go. They went the rest of the way down slowly. At the bottom Oikawa sat on the last step and put his head in his hands. Iwaizumi stayed standing, one hand still on the rail, until the shaking stopped. Then he went upstairs, filled a glass with water, and brought it down. Oikawa drank half of it. Neither of them mentioned the way their shoulders had pressed together on the landing.

Two days later the other therapist cleared Oikawa for light pool work. The clinic’s pool was small and chlorinated and open only in the evenings. Iwaizumi stayed late to lock up and found Oikawa already in the water, moving in slow circuits along the lane line. The brace was off. The scar on the knee was pale and neat. Iwaizumi sat on the edge with his shoes off and his trousers rolled and watched the way Oikawa’s shoulders stayed level even when the left leg lagged.

When Oikawa stopped at the wall, water ran off his arms. “You can come in,” he said.

“I’m not cleared for anything.”

“It’s a pool, Iwa-chan.”

The old name landed between them and stayed. Iwaizumi slid in up to his waist. The water was warmer than he expected. They did not touch. They moved parallel for ten minutes, then Oikawa turned and the current of his passage brushed Iwaizumi’s hip. Neither of them commented. When they got out, Iwaizumi handed Oikawa the towel he had brought down and looked at the wet footprints on the tile until Oikawa was dry enough to put the brace back on.

The mutual part of the pining was not dramatic. It lived in the second mug left on the counter, in the way Iwaizumi started leaving the clinic lights on when he knew Oikawa would be late coming up, in the way Oikawa began setting the rice cooker for two portions without asking. It lived in the nights Iwaizumi woke to the sound of careful steps and did not get up, and in the mornings Oikawa waited until he heard the shower before he started the exercises so the floor would not creak under both of them at once.

On the morning the email arrived—official, from the club’s medical staff, offering a short-term role in Japan if the clearance held—Oikawa read it at the table and then closed the laptop without answering. Iwaizumi was packing his bag for the day. He saw the screen go dark and the way Oikawa’s hands stayed flat on either side of it.

“You don’t have to decide today,” Iwaizumi said.

“I know.”

“Or this week.”

Oikawa looked up. His eyes were clear. “If I take it, I still need somewhere to sleep while the paperwork clears.”

“The room’s yours.”

“And if I don’t take it.”

Iwaizumi zipped the bag. The sound was loud in the quiet apartment. “Then the room’s still yours. The rice cooker is already set for two.”

Oikawa’s mouth moved, almost a smile, then settled. He opened the laptop again, typed three lines, and closed it. “I told them I need until Friday.”

“All right.”

They ate the rice that night with grilled fish Oikawa had bought on the way back from the pool. The fish was slightly overdone on one side. Iwaizumi ate it anyway. Afterward he washed the plates while Oikawa dried, standing close enough that their elbows bumped when one of them reached for the rack. The contact was brief and ordinary. When the last plate was put away, Oikawa stayed at the sink with the towel in his hands and said, “I used to think if I just got far enough away it would stop mattering.”

Iwaizumi dried his own hands on the same towel. “Did it?”

“No.”

Outside, the river was dark and steady. The clinic below them was closed, the lights off, the schedule for tomorrow already printed and waiting on the desk. Oikawa folded the towel and set it on the counter. Iwaizumi turned off the kitchen light. They stood for a moment in the dim from the living-room lamp, close enough that the heat of Oikawa’s arm was noticeable through the thin cotton of his shirt. Neither of them stepped back.

“Friday,” Oikawa said.

“Friday,” Iwaizumi answered.

They went to their separate rooms. The floorboards creaked once, then settled. In the morning the rice cooker would click on by itself, and the second mug would be waiting on the counter, and the river would keep moving under the windows whether either of them looked at it or not.
