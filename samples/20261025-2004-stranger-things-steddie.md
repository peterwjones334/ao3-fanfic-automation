The rain started just after the lunch rush and didn’t let up. Eddie stood at the front window of Munson’s Records with a rag in his good hand, wiping condensation from the glass in slow circles. Outside, Main Street was empty except for a single car idling at the light, wipers on high. Inside, the fluorescents buzzed. Steve was in the back room sorting the shipment that had arrived wet at the edges; Eddie could hear the soft thud of sleeves being stacked.

They’d been working the shop together for seven weeks. Steve had shown up one morning in late August with a resume folded in his back pocket and asked if Eddie needed help. The resume was from Family Video. The pocket was on a pair of jeans that had seen better years. Eddie had said yes because the boxes were heavy and because Steve didn’t flinch when the first record Eddie put on was Black Sabbath.

Now Steve knew where the filters were kept and how Eddie liked the coffee—strong, no sugar, left on the warmer until it was almost too bitter. He knew which customers wanted recommendations and which ones wanted to be left alone. He knew, lately, that Eddie’s left hand stopped cooperating around three in the afternoon on days when the barometer dropped.

Eddie finished the window and turned. Steve was leaning in the doorway to the back, arms crossed, watching him.

“Shipment’s dry enough,” Steve said. “Most of it. A couple of the jazz ones got creased.”

“We’ll discount them.”

Steve nodded. He didn’t move. Water dripped from the hem of his jacket onto the linoleum. He’d gone out to the alley to drag the last crate under the overhang and hadn’t bothered to take the jacket off when he came back in.

“Hand?” Steve asked.

Eddie flexed it once, testing. The tremor was there, low and steady. “Not bad yet.”

“Okay.”

They worked through the afternoon without much talk. A kid came in looking for a tape his older brother used to play; Steve found it in the used bin and rang it up at half price without checking with Eddie first. Eddie let it go. At four the rain eased to a fine mist. Eddie flipped the open sign to closed twenty minutes early and locked the door. Steve was still behind the counter, counting singles into the drawer.

“You can head out,” Eddie said. “I’ll finish.”

“I’m almost done.”

Eddie sat on the stool they kept behind the register and watched Steve’s hands. Steady. No hesitation when he miscounted a stack and started over. The radio played low—something with too much reverb that Eddie would have changed if he’d been alone. He left it.

When the drawer was closed Steve looked up. “You want a ride?”

Eddie usually walked or took the van if Wayne hadn’t borrowed it. Tonight the van was at the plant. “Yeah. If you’re going that way.”

They killed the lights and set the alarm. Outside the air smelled like wet asphalt and the bakery two doors down that had closed an hour ago. Steve’s car was parked at the end of the block, a beige thing with a cracked dashboard and a tape deck that only worked on the driver’s side. Eddie slid into the passenger seat. The heater rattled when Steve turned the key.

They drove without music. Streetlights slid across the windshield and over Steve’s hands on the wheel. At the trailer park Eddie pointed to the second row. Steve pulled in beside the dark trailer and left the engine running.

“Thanks,” Eddie said. He didn’t reach for the door.

Steve’s thumb tapped once against the steering wheel. “You eaten?”

“Not really.”

“There’s a diner open on the highway. Or I can drop you and go.”

Eddie looked at the trailer. The porch light was out again. Wayne’s truck wasn’t in its usual spot. “Diner’s fine.”

They didn’t talk much on the way. The diner was half empty, a handful of truckers at the counter and one couple in a booth by the window. Steve ordered coffee and a grilled cheese. Eddie ordered the same and then added fries when the waitress asked. They ate across from each other. Steve’s knee bumped Eddie’s under the table once and stayed there. Eddie didn’t move his leg.

Halfway through the fries Eddie’s hand started again. He set the fork down and rested the wrist against the edge of the table, trying to still it. Steve watched for a second, then reached over and covered Eddie’s hand with his own. Palm to the back of the knuckles. Weight enough to quiet the shake without pinning it.

“Better?” Steve asked, low.

Eddie nodded. He turned his hand under Steve’s until their fingers linked. The contact was simple. Warm. Steve’s thumb moved once across Eddie’s knuckles and stopped.

They finished the food like that. The waitress refilled their coffees and didn’t comment. Outside the mist had turned to real rain again, streaking the windows. Steve paid. Eddie didn’t argue.

In the car on the way back Eddie kept his hand on his thigh. The tremor had settled. Steve drove with both hands on the wheel until they reached the trailer, then left one resting on the gearshift between them. Eddie looked at it. Steve didn’t move it.

“Same time tomorrow?” Steve asked.

“Yeah.” Eddie opened the door. Rain blew in, cold against his face. He hesitated. “You can come in. If you want. Coffee’s better here than the shop’s.”

Steve turned the engine off. The sudden quiet made the rain louder. “Okay.”

Inside, Eddie turned on the lamp by the couch and started the kettle. The trailer was small. Steve stood near the door until Eddie nodded toward the couch, then sat. His jacket dripped onto the linoleum. Eddie tossed him a dish towel from the sink. Steve dried his hands and set the towel on his knee.

They drank the coffee on opposite ends of the couch. The space between them was maybe a foot. Eddie’s hand rested on the cushion. After a minute Steve’s settled beside it, close enough that their pinkies touched. Neither pulled away.

“You don’t have to keep covering the discounts,” Eddie said eventually.

“I know.”

“Or the boxes. Or—” He flexed the fingers that were still touching Steve’s. “This.”

Steve turned his hand so their palms met. “I want to.”

Eddie looked at their hands. The lamp threw soft light across Steve’s wrist, the faint scar there from something Eddie had never asked about. He thought about the seven weeks of early mornings and late closings, the way Steve had learned the shop without being told twice, the quiet that had stopped feeling like something they were waiting out.

He thought about the first week, when Steve had stayed late to help reorganize the metal section and hadn’t complained when Eddie played the same three songs on loop. He thought about the afternoon Steve had driven him to the clinic in the next town because the hand had locked up completely and Eddie couldn’t get the van started. Steve had sat in the waiting room for two hours and never asked what the doctor said. Eddie had told him anyway on the drive back: old injury, nerves, weather made it worse. Steve had nodded and changed the subject to the new shipment.

“Stay a while,” Eddie said.

Steve’s fingers tightened once, then relaxed. “Yeah.”

They sat with the rain on the roof and the coffee going cold between them. Eddie’s hand didn’t shake. Steve’s thumb traced a slow line across his palm and stopped at the center. Outside, a truck passed on the main road, headlights sweeping the thin curtains. Inside, neither of them moved to turn on another light.

Steve shifted closer on the couch until their shoulders touched. Eddie let his head tip sideways until it rested against Steve’s. The contact was careful. Steve’s free hand came up and settled lightly on the back of Eddie’s neck, thumb moving once along the hairline before it stilled.

“This okay?” Steve asked.

“Yeah.”

They stayed like that until the rain eased and the trailer grew cold enough that Eddie got up to find a blanket. He draped it over both of them when he sat back down. Steve’s arm settled around his shoulders without comment. Eddie closed his eyes and listened to the last of the water dripping from the roof. His hand, resting on Steve’s thigh under the blanket, stayed still.

In the morning Steve was on the curb again, two cups in a cardboard tray and a paper bag under his arm. Eddie unlocked the door. Their shoulders brushed in the narrow space. Steve set the cups on the counter and started the pot out of habit even though the coffee was already made.

“Trailer coffee’s better,” he said.

“Told you.”

Eddie picked up a marker. His hand stayed steady through the first stack of records. Steve glanced over once, saw, and went back to unlocking the register. The rain had stopped. Sun came through the front window in a thin bar across the floor. Eddie priced another sleeve, set it aside, and reached for the next one. Steve’s elbow bumped his when they both leaned for the same crate. Neither stepped back.

The quiet held. Eddie let it. Later, when the tremor started just before closing, Steve took the marker without being asked and finished the last of the pricing while Eddie locked up. On the drive to the trailer they didn’t talk about it. Steve stayed for dinner—chili from a can, bread he’d bought on the way. They ate at the small table with their knees touching underneath. When Eddie’s hand cramped halfway through the bowl, Steve covered it with his own until the muscles eased. Neither of them called it anything. They didn’t need to.

The next evening Wayne came home early. He found them on the couch again, Eddie’s head on Steve’s shoulder, the blanket pulled up to their chests, the television on low with the sound off. Steve started to sit up. Eddie didn’t move.

Wayne stood in the doorway for a second, keys in his hand. He looked at the two of them, at the empty bowls on the table, at the way Steve’s hand rested open on Eddie’s knee.

“Coffee’s on if you want it,” Wayne said. He hung his jacket and went to the kitchen without another word.

Steve exhaled. Eddie felt it against his hair.

“You good?” Steve asked, quiet.

“Yeah.” Eddie turned his face into the fabric of Steve’s shirt. “Stay.”

Steve’s arm tightened once around him. Outside, the rain had started again, light against the roof. Inside, the kettle clicked on. Eddie closed his eyes and let the tremor in his hand stay where it was—quiet, for now, under Steve’s palm.
