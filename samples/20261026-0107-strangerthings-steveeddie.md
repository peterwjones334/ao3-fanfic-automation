**The Quiet After**

After the gates closed, Hawkins settled into a quiet it had never known how to hold. The pavement cracks were filled with the same tar that peeled in summer. The new mall went up smaller, lower, without the atrium that used to catch every echo. People who had seen the underside of the town kept their hours short and their doors locked, even when the weather turned mild.

Steve Harrington took the night shifts at Family Video because the daylight made the parking lot too wide. He restocked the returns in alphabetical order, then again by genre when the first pass left him restless. Horror went back on the shelf spine-out so the covers didn’t face the door. At closing he drove the long route home, past the tree line that no longer hummed, past the quarry road where the gravel had been graded smooth.

Eddie Munson came in on a Thursday with a stack of tapes under one arm and a cigarette tucked behind his ear that he never lit. His hair was longer at the ends. The denim jacket had been patched at both elbows with fabric that didn’t quite match. He set the tapes on the counter and kept his eyes on the candy rack.

“Returns,” he said.

Steve took them. Two were three days late. He stamped the cards without mentioning the fee.

Eddie nodded once, like the silence was an answer he had expected, and left.

He returned the following week with a different stack and stayed longer in the metal aisle. The week after that he asked, without looking up, whether the store still carried the old Hellfire campaign tapes Dustin had copied onto VHS. Steve found them in the back room under a box of returns that had never been claimed. Eddie took them without paying and came back the next night to return them on time.

They spoke in pieces that fit between the register counts. Eddie talked about the garage on the edge of town where he was learning to rebuild carburetors, about the trailer park that still stood because no one had found a reason to clear it. Steve talked about the kids who stopped by after school, about Robin’s attempts to teach him the proper way to alphabetize so the shelves didn’t look like they’d been sorted by someone who still flinched at the horror section. Neither of them mentioned the spring before or the hospital corridors that had smelled like antiseptic and rain.

One night the van wouldn’t start. Eddie stood in the parking lot with the hood up and the flashlight from the glove box pointed at an engine that refused to turn over. Steve locked the store and walked over with the keys to the BMW already in his hand.

“Leave it,” Eddie said. “I’ll walk.”

“It’s three miles.”

Eddie closed the hood. The rain had started, fine and cold. They loaded the returns into the back of Steve’s car because the boxes were already stacked by the door and neither of them wanted to leave them under the awning. Eddie’s hands were wet when he passed the last tape across. He stopped with it halfway between them, looked at Steve the way he had looked at the hospital doors the night they carried him out, and leaned in.

The kiss was short. Steve tasted rain and the metal of the tape case still between their fingers. When Eddie pulled back his mouth was set in the line Steve recognized from every room where someone had asked him if he was all right and waited for the wrong answer.

“Sorry,” Eddie said.

“Don’t.” Steve set the tape in the box and reached for him again. The second kiss lasted longer. Eddie’s hands stayed at his sides until Steve’s fingers found the damp edge of his jacket and held there.

After that the hours they already kept simply stretched. Eddie arrived before close on the nights Steve worked alone and sat on the counter while the register was counted. He brought coffee from the diner that tasted the way the place smelled at two in the morning. Steve learned which of Eddie’s ribs still pulled when the weather shifted and which of the scars along his throat he covered with the collar of his shirt when customers came in. Eddie learned that Steve’s hands shook when the power flickered and that the shaking stopped if someone else was holding the flashlight.

They slept in Steve’s apartment more nights than they slept apart. The window stayed cracked even when the nights turned cold. In the morning Eddie made coffee strong enough to strip paint and left half of it in the pot because he still left before the other mechanics arrived at the garage. Steve drove him the short distance and kissed him once at the curb, quick, before the lot filled.

The kids noticed on a Saturday when Dustin arrived with a new set of character sheets and a question he had clearly practiced.

“Are you two, like, together now or what?”

Steve looked at Eddie. Eddie had one hand on the back of the couch and the other still holding the dice bag he had been about to hand over. He shrugged.

“Yeah,” he said. “Guess we are.”

Dustin grinned the way he used to grin when a roll went better than the odds and went back to sorting miniatures on the kitchen table. Robin arrived twenty minutes later with groceries and didn’t ask. She looked at the two mugs by the sink, at the denim jacket on the chair, and started putting the milk away like the apartment had always been arranged that way.

They kept the routine because it didn’t require a name. Movie nights ended with Eddie’s head on Steve’s shoulder and the credits rolling past the point where either of them was still watching. Mornings started with the same pot of coffee and the same argument about whether the radio stayed on classic rock or switched to the station that played the songs Eddie had taught himself on the guitar that still lived in the corner of the living room. Evenings filled with the sound of someone else moving through the rooms—keys on the counter, boots by the door, the low scrape of a chair pulled out at the table.

On the night that marked a year since the gates had closed, they drove to the quarry. The water was flat. The gravel lot was empty except for the BMW and a single streetlight that had been replaced since the last time either of them had stood there. Eddie kept his hands in his pockets. Steve stood close enough that their shoulders touched.

“I used to come out here when it got bad,” Eddie said. “Before any of it. Just to hear something that wasn’t the trailer.”

Steve watched the water. “Does it still get bad?”

Eddie was quiet long enough that the streetlight buzzed once and settled. “Less. Not never. Less.”

Steve took his hand out of his pocket and found Eddie’s. The fingers were warm. They stayed until the light clicked off on its timer and the dark settled properly over the water. On the drive back Steve took the long route without being asked. Eddie’s hand rested on the center console, palm up, until Steve set his own over it at the first red light.

The apartment was dark when they unlocked the door. Eddie turned on the lamp by the couch. Steve locked the deadbolt and the chain. Neither of them reached for the overhead switch. The lamp was enough. Eddie sat on the edge of the couch and unlaced his boots. Steve stood in the kitchen long enough to fill the kettle, then left it on the stove without turning the burner on. When he came back Eddie had already shifted to make room. Steve sat. Their shoulders touched again. Outside, the street was quiet in the way Hawkins had learned to be. Inside, the only sound was the low tick of the clock on the wall and the steady pull of two people breathing in the same room.

They stayed like that until the lamp grew warm against the side of Steve’s face and Eddie’s head tipped, slow, onto his shoulder. Steve didn’t move. The quiet held.
