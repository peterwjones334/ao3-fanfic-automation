The basement smelled like damp carpet and the last of the winter dust. Will stood at the bottom of the stairs with a cardboard box already half-full of old comic books, watching Mike pull a plastic bin from under the workbench. The light from the single overhead bulb made everything look a little yellow. Outside, Hawkins was doing its best impression of a normal June afternoon—lawnmowers, distant kids on bikes, Mrs. Wheeler’s radio playing something soft through the kitchen window above them.

“You don’t have to stay,” Mike said. He didn’t look up. “I told my mom I’d get it done before she starts the renovation. Most of this is just going to the thrift store anyway.”

Will shifted the box against his hip. “I said I would help.”

They had been circling this for three days. Will had been back from California for a week. The Byers house was still half-empty—Joyce had sold most of the furniture when they left, and the new place she’d rented was smaller, closer to the edge of town. Jonathan was in the city for work. Will had offered to help clear the Wheeler basement because the alternative was sitting in a quiet house with his sketchbook and the particular kind of silence that made him think too much about the years they had spent pretending the distance didn’t matter.

Mike set the bin down. The lid was cracked. When he lifted it, the smell of old paper and crayon wax rose up. Inside were the rolled maps from the last campaign they’d played before Will moved, the edges soft with handling. Mike’s handwriting was still on the nearest one in careful block letters: *The Gates of Shadow*

Will looked away first.

They worked without much talking for a while. Will sorted the books into keep and donate piles. Mike stacked the bins by the stairs. Every so often their hands brushed when they reached for the same thing, and both of them pretended it was nothing. Will had gotten better at that in California. Mike had always been good at it.

“El called last night,” Mike said eventually. He was on his knees in front of an old trunk, folding a blanket that had been used for forts. “She’s taking a summer class. Said the dorms are loud.”

Will nodded. He had spoken to her too, briefly, the day after he arrived. She had sounded tired and happy in equal measure. “She’s good at the loud parts now.”

“Yeah.” Mike smoothed the blanket. His thumb caught on a loose thread. “She asked about you. How you were settling in.”

“I’m settling.”

The words sat between them. Will could feel Mike watching the side of his face. He kept his eyes on the next comic, the cover faded to a dull green. He had spent most of high school learning how to stand in rooms with Mike and not let anything show that could be used against either of them. The habit was still there even when the threat was gone.

Mike closed the trunk. The latch clicked. “You don’t have to do that.”

“Do what?”

“Act like I’m going to—” Mike stopped. He sat back on his heels, hands on his knees. There was a streak of dust across the front of his shirt. “Like I’m still the person who didn’t know what to say when you needed me to.”

Will set the comic down. The basement felt smaller. “You did know. You just didn’t want to.”

Mike’s mouth tightened. He looked at the wall, at the old poster of a dragon that had been taped there since they were twelve. “I wanted to. I just didn’t know how without making it worse.”

They had never said it out loud in so many words. Will had come out to the group in pieces—first to Jonathan, then to the others, then, finally, to Mike in a letter that he had rewritten four times and still almost not sent. Mike had called him the same night. The conversation had been careful and full of silences. Will had hung up feeling both relieved and hollow. That had been two years ago. They had written, sometimes. They had called on birthdays. They had not talked about the letter again.

Will picked up another box. This one was lighter. He opened it and found the stack of drawings he had left behind when they moved—sketches of the party, of the woods behind the Byers house, of Mike’s hands holding a die. He had forgotten they were here.

Mike stood. He crossed the space between them in three steps and stopped just short of the box. “Can I see?”

Will handed him the top drawing without looking at it. Mike held it carefully by the edges. It was the one of the four of them at the table, Lucas laughing, Dustin mid-sentence, Mike leaning forward with his elbows on the map. Will had drawn himself smaller than the others, half-turned toward the edge of the paper.

“You always did that,” Mike said.

“Did what?”

“Drew yourself like you were already leaving.”

Will took the drawing back. His fingers brushed Mike’s. Neither of them moved away. The contact was brief and ordinary and enough to make Will’s throat tight.

“I didn’t know how else to draw it,” he said. “I kept thinking if I made myself smaller, it would hurt less when I actually left.”

Mike was quiet for a long moment. Upstairs, the radio changed songs. A car passed on the street. Will could hear Mike breathing, the small catch at the end of the exhale.

“I kept your last letter,” Mike said. “The one from March. I read it again last week when I knew you were coming back.”

Will had written that letter at the kitchen table in California, after a shift at the bookstore. He had told Mike about the class he was taking, about a painting he was working on, about the way the light looked different over the ocean. He had not written the sentence he had wanted to write: *I still miss the way you used to look at me when you thought I wasn’t paying attention.*

“What did it say that you needed to read again?” Will asked.

Mike’s ears went pink. He looked at the floor between their feet. “Nothing that wasn’t true. I just—I wanted to remember the way you sound when you’re not trying to make it easier for me.”

Will set the box down. The drawings shifted inside. He could feel the weight of the afternoon pressing against the back of his neck. He had spent the last week telling himself that coming home did not mean anything had to change. That they could be friends the way they had been before everything got complicated. That wanting more was a habit he could still break.

Mike stepped closer. Not enough to touch, but enough that Will could see the faint freckles across the bridge of his nose, the ones that only showed up in summer. “I was going to wait,” Mike said. “Until you were settled. Until I figured out how to say it without sounding like I was asking you to stay for me.”

“Say what?”

Mike’s hand lifted, then dropped. He laughed once, short and nervous. “That I spent two years trying to be the person you needed and only figuring out how once you were already gone. That every time you called I wanted to ask if you were coming back, and I didn’t, because I thought it would make you feel guilty. That I still look for you in rooms even when I know you’re not there.”

Will’s chest hurt in a way that was almost familiar. He had imagined versions of this conversation in California, late at night, when the apartment was quiet and the distance felt permanent. None of them had sounded like this. None of them had included Mike looking at him like the answer mattered more than the question.

“You could have asked,” Will said. His voice came out thinner than he wanted. “I would have told you I was thinking about it.”

“I know that now.” Mike rubbed his thumb against the side of his index finger, a nervous habit Will recognized from every campaign they had ever played. “I kept thinking if I said it wrong, you would think I only wanted you because you were leaving. Or because El and I—” He stopped. “We’re good. She knows. She’s been telling me for months that I was being an idiot.”

Will almost smiled. “She would.”

“Yeah.” Mike met his eyes. The basement light caught the brown of them and made them look warmer. “I don’t want you to stay if you don’t want to. I just want you to know that if you do, I’m not going to pretend I don’t notice when you walk into a room. I’m not going to make you smaller so it’s easier for me to let you go.”

Will looked at the drawing still in his hand. The version of himself on the paper was turned away. He folded it once, carefully, and set it on top of the box. When he looked up, Mike was still watching him, waiting without pressing.

“I didn’t come back just for the summer,” Will said. The words felt larger once they were out. “I applied to the community college here. The art program. I haven’t told my mom yet. I wanted to see if it still felt like home first.”

Mike’s breath left him in a small rush. He nodded, once, like he was confirming something to himself. “Does it?”

Will thought about the quiet house, the familiar roads, the way the basement still smelled the same even after all the years. He thought about Mike standing three feet away with dust on his shirt and his hands open at his sides.

“It’s starting to,” he said.

They finished the rest of the basement in the late afternoon light that came down the stairs. They didn’t talk about the college application or the letters or the drawings. They stacked the last of the boxes by the door and carried them up to the driveway for the thrift store run tomorrow. When they were done, Mike offered Will a glass of water in the kitchen. They stood at the counter while the ice settled. Outside, the sprinkler on the front lawn clicked on.

Mike set his glass down. His shoulder brushed Will’s when he turned. This time neither of them moved away.

“If you want,” Mike said, “we could drive out to the quarry tomorrow. Just to see it. I haven’t been since last fall.”

Will nodded. The glass was cold in his hand. “Yeah. I’d like that.”

They stayed in the kitchen until the light changed and Mrs. Wheeler called down to ask if they were staying for dinner. Will said yes. Mike smiled, small and real, and went to set another place at the table. Will watched him go. The ache in his chest had shifted into something quieter. Not gone. Just no longer the only thing he could feel.
