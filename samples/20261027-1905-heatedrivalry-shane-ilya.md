Shane’s shoulder had been aching since the third period in Boston three weeks ago. He’d told the trainers it was fine. It wasn’t.

The league had gathered the captains for a panel that felt more like a performance review. Most of the room had cleared out the moment the microphones went dark. Shane stayed behind in the hallway outside the conference room, leaning against the wall because standing upright made the edges of his vision soften. Hayden was probably already downstairs, texting Jackie. The others had left in pairs and small groups, still talking about nothing that mattered.

A door opened at the far end of the corridor. Ilya Rozanov stepped out, still in the navy suit he’d worn for the cameras, jacket unbuttoned, collar open. He looked tired in a way the cameras never caught.

Shane straightened. The movement pulled the muscle wrong and he had to bite the inside of his cheek to keep quiet.

Ilya stopped when he saw him. For a second neither of them moved. Then Ilya walked over, slow, hands in his pockets.

“Hollander.”

“Rozanov.”

“You are standing like someone who is about to fall over.”

“I’m fine.”

Ilya’s eyes tracked the way Shane’s left hand hovered near his right shoulder without touching it. “You are not.”

Shane didn’t answer. The hallway was empty. Somewhere a cart rattled, then faded.

“Sit down before you do something stupid,” Ilya said. He nodded at the bench against the wall.

Shane sat because the alternative was arguing while the room tilted. The bench was hard. His shoulder screamed when he lowered himself.

Ilya stayed standing. He looked at the closed door of the conference room, then back. “They asked me about you. On the panel. What I thought of your season.”

“What did you say?”

“That you are still the most annoying person in the league.” Ilya’s mouth twitched. “They laughed.”

“Charming.”

“I did not tell them you play hurt.”

Shane’s head came up. The motion cost him. “I’m not—”

“You are.” Ilya’s voice was flat. “I watched the Boston game. You favored the right side after the hit in the second. You have not been the same since.”

Shane looked at the carpet. It was patterned in muted blues and greys. “It’s managed.”

“By who? You?”

No answer.

Ilya exhaled through his nose. He sat on the other end of the bench, careful to leave space. The distance still felt small.

“My trainer is good,” Shane said eventually. “And the team doctor.”

“And you tell them the truth?”

Shane didn’t.

They sat. The quiet stretched. Shane’s pulse was loud in his ears. He could smell Ilya’s cologne—something clean, with a faint edge of whatever soap the hotel used. It was familiar in a way that made his chest tight. They’d spent years circling each other on the ice, in interviews, in hotel bars after games where one of them had won and the other had gone home furious. The wanting had never left. It had just gotten quieter, buried under the need to win and the need not to be the reason the other lost focus.

“I have ice,” Ilya said. “In my room. And tape. And the good painkillers, not the ones they give you that do nothing.”

“I’m not going to your room.”

“Then I will bring them here. But the hallway is not private, and you look like you will pass out if you stay upright much longer.”

Shane closed his eyes. The offer was practical. It was also the last thing he should accept.

“Room 1214,” Ilya said. “If you change your mind.”

He stood. Shane didn’t look up until the footsteps were almost gone.

“Rozanov.”

Ilya turned.

“Don’t tell anyone.”

“I never do.”

Shane made it to the room twenty minutes later. He’d gone back to his own first, stared at the ice machine on his floor, and decided he couldn’t reach it without his arm shaking. The elevator ride was short. The knock on 1214 felt louder than it was.

Ilya opened the door in a t-shirt and sweatpants. His hair was damp. He’d showered. Shane’s brain supplied the image and then refused to elaborate.

“Come in,” Ilya said. He stepped aside.

The room was a standard suite. Two chairs by the window, a small table, the bed made. Ilya had already pulled a chair closer to the desk and set a bag of ice on a towel. A roll of athletic tape sat next to a bottle of water and a blister pack of pills.

Shane sat. He shrugged out of his jacket carefully. The shirt underneath was wrinkled from the panel. When he tried to lift his arm to pull it off, the pain spiked sharp enough that he stopped, breathing hard.

Ilya watched him for a second, then held out a hand. “Let me.”

Shane hesitated. Then he nodded.

Ilya’s fingers were careful at the buttons. He worked them open one by one, not looking at Shane’s face. When the shirt was loose, he helped ease it off the bad shoulder first. Shane’s skin prickled where the air hit it. The bruising had faded to yellow-green along the collarbone. The muscle underneath still looked tight.

“Sit forward,” Ilya said.

Shane did. Ilya pressed the ice pack against the joint, wrapping the towel so it stayed. The cold was immediate and vicious. Shane’s breath caught.

“Too much?”

“No.”

Ilya’s hand stayed on the pack for a moment longer than necessary, steadying it. Then he moved to the tape. He didn’t ask if Shane wanted it. He just started, measuring the length against Shane’s arm, cutting it clean.

The first strip went over the top of the shoulder. Ilya’s palm was warm where it smoothed the tape down. Shane stared at the carpet between his shoes and tried not to lean into the touch.

“You should have said something earlier,” Ilya said. His voice was low. “After Boston.”

“To you?”

“To someone.”

“I did. They cleared me.”

“They clear everyone if the playoffs are close enough.” Ilya’s fingers worked the next strip into place, anchoring it. “You played the last four games like your arm was not attached to your body.”

Shane huffed a laugh that hurt. “We won two of them.”

“You scored in the third against us. On a broken play. I thought you were going to drop the stick.”

“I didn’t.”

“No.” Ilya finished the wrap and sat back on his heels. He looked up. Their faces were closer than they’d been in months. “You never do.”

The ice was numbing the worst of it. Shane’s shoulder still throbbed, but the edge was gone. He could think again.

“Why are you doing this?” he asked.

Ilya’s expression didn’t change. “Because you will not.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It is the only one I have.”

They stayed like that. Ilya’s hands rested on his own knees. Shane could see the scar on his left knuckle from a fight three seasons ago—Shane had been the one who’d thrown the first punch that night, after a dirty hit on Hayden. They’d both sat out the next game.

“I think about you,” Shane said. The words came out before he could stop them. “More than I should.”

Ilya went very still.

“On the ice. Off it. When I’m supposed to be watching film. When I’m home and my parents ask how the season is going and I say fine and I mean I keep checking the Boston scores like they matter more than they do.”

The room was quiet except for the low hum of the heater.

“I know,” Ilya said.

Shane looked at him. Ilya’s eyes were dark. There was no smirk, no deflection.

“You do?”

“I watch you too.” Ilya’s mouth curved, small and tired. “It is inconvenient.”

Shane’s chest ached worse than his shoulder. “Yeah.”

Ilya reached for the water and the pills. He shook two out and held them in his palm. Shane took them. Their fingers brushed. Neither of them pulled away immediately.

“Stay,” Ilya said. “Until the ice melts. Or longer. Your choice.”

Shane swallowed the pills. The water was cold. He set the bottle down.

“I’ll stay,” he said. “For a while.”

Ilya nodded. He adjusted the ice pack once more, then stood and moved to the other chair. He didn’t turn on the television. He didn’t fill the silence. He just sat, close enough that Shane could hear him breathing, and let the minutes pass.

Shane shifted, careful of the shoulder. The ice was already starting to soften at the edges. “You ever get tired of it?” he asked. “The way they talk about us. Like we’re just the story they need.”

“Every day.” Ilya’s voice was quiet. “They do not know what it costs.”

Shane thought about the nights he’d sat in hotel rooms after losses, phone in his hand, thumb hovering over Ilya’s number and never pressing. He thought about the way Ilya had looked at him across the ice in Boston, after the hit, when no one else was watching. There had been anger there, and something else Shane hadn’t let himself name.

“I almost called you,” Shane said. “After the game. When the trainers were done with me.”

Ilya’s head turned. “Why didn’t you?”

“Because I didn’t know what I would say if you answered.”

“You could have said your shoulder hurts.”

“And then what?”

Ilya was quiet for a long moment. “Then I would have told you to stop playing like an idiot. And I would have meant it.”

Shane’s mouth twitched. The ice pack slipped a little. Ilya reached over without asking and settled it back into place, his fingers warm against Shane’s skin for half a second before he pulled away.

“You can stay the night,” Ilya said. “If the pills make you tired. The couch is fine. Or the bed. I will not touch you unless you ask.”

Shane looked at him. The offer sat between them, plain and careful. Outside, the city lights blurred against the window. Somewhere below, the panelists were still talking, still performing the version of the league that didn’t include this.

“The bed,” Shane said. His voice came out rougher than he meant. “If that’s all right.”

Ilya nodded once. He stood and pulled the covers back on one side, then stepped away to give Shane room. Shane moved slowly, the taped shoulder protesting every shift. When he was under the blankets, Ilya turned off the main light, leaving only the lamp by the desk. He sat in the chair again, not close enough to touch, close enough that Shane could hear the steady rhythm of his breathing.

Shane closed his eyes. The pain was still there, but it was manageable. For the first time in weeks, he didn’t feel like he had to carry it alone.

He didn’t sleep right away. He listened to Ilya shift in the chair, to the low sound of the heater, to the city outside. At some point the ice pack had gone fully soft. Ilya must have moved it, because when Shane opened his eyes again the towel was folded on the nightstand and the room was darker.

Ilya was still there. His head was tipped back against the chair, eyes closed. He looked younger like that. Less guarded.

Shane watched him for a minute. Then he said, quietly, “Ilya.”

Ilya’s eyes opened. He didn’t startle.

“Come here,” Shane said. “If you want.”

Ilya stood. He crossed the short distance and sat on the edge of the bed, careful not to jostle the shoulder. For a moment he just looked at Shane in the low light. Then he lay down on top of the covers, on his side, facing him, one arm bent under his head.

“This is fine?” he asked.

“Yeah.”

They stayed like that. Shane’s hand found the space between them and rested there, palm up. After a while Ilya’s fingers settled against his, light, not quite holding on. The contact was small. It was enough.

In the morning the shoulder would still hurt. The season would still be long. Nothing would be solved by one night in a hotel room. But when Shane woke, the first thing he felt was the weight of Ilya’s hand still against his, and the second thing he felt was the quiet certainty that he wouldn’t have to pretend, at least not here, that he was fine on his own.
