The room was small and too warm. Bakugou lay on the narrow hospital bed with his right arm elevated, the bandages thick from elbow to fingertips. The monitors ticked. Outside the window the city kept moving; sirens rose and fell two streets over and then faded.

Midoriya sat in the chair nearest the bed. He had been there since the ambulance arrived. His jacket was folded over his knees. The blood on the sleeve had dried to a stiff edge he had not yet noticed.

“You can leave,” Bakugou said. His voice was rough. The nurse had given him something for the pain an hour ago; it had taken the sharp edge off but left the rest.

“I know.”

They watched the light change on the ceiling. Midoriya’s hands rested on the jacket. One knuckle was split. He flexed the fingers once and stopped when Bakugou’s eyes followed the motion.

“Your arm,” Midoriya said.

“It’ll heal.”

“The doctor said the nerves—”

“I heard him.” Bakugou turned his head. The pillow shifted. “You stayed for the whole debrief.”

“They asked for a statement.”

“You gave them one.” Bakugou’s mouth tightened. “You said I held the line. You didn’t say I walked into the blast on purpose.”

Midoriya looked at the floor. The linoleum had a crack near the door. “You were covering the civilians.”

“I was covering you.”

The words sat between them. Midoriya did not answer right away. He had spent the last three years learning how to hear the things Bakugou did not say. This one was plain.

“I would have moved,” Midoriya said.

“You were already moving toward the kids.” Bakugou’s free hand curled on the sheet. “I saw it. So I stayed.”

A nurse came in to check the IV. They both went quiet. When she left, the room felt smaller. Midoriya stood and poured water from the pitcher into the plastic cup. He held it out. Bakugou took it with his left hand. Their fingers touched for the length of the exchange.

“You’re shaking,” Bakugou said.

“I’m fine.”

“You’re not.” Bakugou drank. He set the cup down himself. “Sit back down.”

Midoriya sat. He kept his eyes on Bakugou’s bandaged arm. The gauze was clean. Under it the skin would be mottled and tight for weeks. He had seen Bakugou burn himself before. He had never seen him choose it.

“I keep thinking about the way the heat rolled,” Midoriya said. “You didn’t even flinch until after.”

“I felt it.” Bakugou’s voice dropped. “I just didn’t have time to do anything else.”

They stayed like that until the hallway lights dimmed for the night shift. Midoriya’s phone buzzed once. He silenced it without looking. Bakugou watched him do it.

“Your mother called earlier,” Bakugou said. “While you were talking to the police. I told her you were still here.”

“You answered my phone.”

“It was face-up on the table. She was going to keep calling.” Bakugou shifted. The movement pulled at the bandages. He breathed through it. “She said to tell you to eat something.”

Midoriya almost smiled. It did not quite reach his eyes. “I’ll get something later.”

“There’s a machine down the hall. It takes coins.”

“I know where it is.”

Neither of them moved. The monitor beeped. Somewhere in the building a door closed hard enough to echo.

“Kacchan,” Midoriya said. He had not used the name in months. It came out smaller than he meant. “When the blast came back at you, I thought—”

“Don’t.”

“I need to say it.”

Bakugou looked at him. The painkillers had left his eyes clearer than they should have been. “Then say it.”

“I thought I was going to watch you die for me again.” Midoriya’s hands tightened on the jacket. “I can’t do that. Not again. Not after everything.”

The room was quiet enough that Midoriya could hear the low hum of the air vent. Bakugou’s jaw worked. He looked at the window, then back.

“I didn’t do it for you to watch,” he said. “I did it so you wouldn’t have to.”

“That isn’t better.”

“It’s what I had.” Bakugou’s left hand lifted. He stopped it halfway across the space between the bed and the chair. The fingers hovered. “Come here.”

Midoriya stood. He moved the chair closer until the plastic arm touched the bed rail. Bakugou’s hand settled on his wrist. The grip was light. The skin of Bakugou’s palm was warm and dry except where the bandage edge brushed Midoriya’s sleeve.

“You’re still shaking,” Bakugou said again.

“I know.”

They stayed that way. Midoriya’s free hand rested on the bed near Bakugou’s hip. He did not touch him. Bakugou’s thumb moved once, slow, over the inside of Midoriya’s wrist. The pulse there jumped and then steadied.

“The doctor said I can leave tomorrow if the swelling stays down,” Bakugou said. “I don’t want to go back to the dorms yet. Too many people asking questions.”

“You can come to mine,” Midoriya said. “The apartment is quiet. All Might’s old place. No one else has the key.”

Bakugou’s fingers tightened, then relaxed. “Your mother will worry.”

“She already does.”

“Fine.” Bakugou closed his eyes for a moment. When he opened them the line between his brows had eased. “But I’m not staying on the couch. Your back is already fucked from the last fight.”

“The bed is big enough.”

“I know how big it is.” Bakugou’s mouth twitched. “I’ve been there.”

Midoriya’s ears went warm. He did not pull his wrist away. “That was different.”

“Was it.”

They did not answer the question. The nurse came back with evening medication. Bakugou swallowed the pills without complaint. Midoriya stepped into the hall while she worked. When he returned, Bakugou was half-asleep, the bandaged arm still elevated, the left hand open on the sheet where Midoriya’s wrist had been.

Midoriya sat again. He stayed until the monitors showed steady numbers and Bakugou’s breathing evened out. Then he stood, folded the jacket over his arm, and turned off the overhead light. The hallway was empty. He bought two onigiri from the machine and ate one standing by the window at the end of the corridor. The city lights looked the same as they had the night before. He finished the second rice ball on the walk to the station and kept the plastic wrapper in his pocket until he reached the apartment.

In the morning he was back before visiting hours started. Bakugou was awake and already arguing with the discharge nurse about the sling. Midoriya waited by the door until the paperwork was signed. They took a taxi. Bakugou sat on the right side so his arm would not brush the door. Midoriya paid. Neither of them spoke until the elevator in the old building.

“Your place smells like that tea All Might used to drink,” Bakugou said when the door closed behind them.

“I keep some in the cabinet.”

“Make it.”

Midoriya put the kettle on. Bakugou stood in the middle of the small living room and looked at the framed photo on the shelf—the one of the class after the final exam, all of them still in uniform, Bakugou’s arm slung around Kirishima’s shoulders, Midoriya half a step to the left. Bakugou turned away from it.

They drank the tea at the kitchen table. Bakugou’s left hand wrapped the cup. Midoriya watched the way the steam moved across his face.

“I meant what I said last night,” Midoriya said.

“I know.”

“I don’t want you doing that again.”

“I can’t promise that.” Bakugou set the cup down. “But I can promise I’ll tell you when I’m about to.”

Midoriya nodded. He reached across the table. His fingers stopped just short of Bakugou’s. Bakugou closed the distance. Their hands rested together on the wood, palms touching, the bandage a pale line between them.

“Stay,” Midoriya said.

“I’m already here.”

Outside, the morning traffic moved. Inside, the kettle clicked as it cooled. They sat until the tea went cold and the light on the table shifted from grey to ordinary yellow. Bakugou’s thumb moved once over Midoriya’s knuckles. Midoriya turned his hand and held on.
