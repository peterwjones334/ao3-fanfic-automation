The bell over the door of Melvin's Records didn't ring so much as rattle. Eddie pushed it open with his shoulder, the paper bag of coffee and two sandwiches balanced against his hip. The limp was better on dry days. Today the sky was low and gray, the kind that settled into the joints.

Steve was already behind the counter, sorting a stack of returns. He looked up, then back down, the way he always did—like he was giving Eddie the option not to be noticed.

"Coffee's from the place that doesn't burn it," Eddie said, setting the bag down. "And the sandwich with the actual meat is yours. Don't say I never did anything for you."

Steve's mouth twitched. He took the cup, fingers brushing the cardboard sleeve. "You drove twenty minutes for coffee that doesn't taste like ash."

"I'm a saint." Eddie dropped onto the stool behind the register, stretching the left leg out carefully. The scar along his ribs pulled when he breathed deep. He didn't mention it. Steve noticed anyway; his eyes flicked down and away.

They worked the afternoon shift like that most Tuesdays. Melvin was half-deaf and half-retired, content to leave the store to whoever showed up. Eddie had started coming in three months after the hospital released him, when the quiet in his trailer got too loud. Steve had followed a week later with a stack of tapes and the excuse that Family Video was cutting hours. Neither of them had explained the rest.

A customer came in looking for something loud. Eddie pointed him toward the metal section without standing. Steve rang him up, patient, the way he was with the kids when they were younger and more feral. The door rattled shut. The rain started properly then, soft against the glass.

"You eat yet?" Steve asked.

"Waiting for you." Eddie unwrapped his sandwich. The bread was still warm. He watched Steve take a bite of his own and make a small, satisfied sound. It was stupid how much that sound lived in Eddie's chest for the rest of the afternoon.

---

The nightmares were worse when the weather turned. Eddie woke at three with his heart trying to climb out of his throat and the taste of copper still on his tongue. He sat on the edge of the bed until the room stopped tilting, then pulled on a hoodie and went outside to smoke. The trailer park was dark. A dog barked once, far off, and settled.

Headlights cut across the gravel twenty minutes later. Steve's car idled for a second before the engine cut. He got out with two paper cups and didn't ask questions. They sat on the steps, shoulders almost touching. The coffee was from the all-night place by the highway. It was terrible. Eddie drank it anyway.

"You don't have to do this," he said, after the first cup was gone.

Steve shrugged. The motion brushed their arms together. "Couldn't sleep either."

They didn't talk about the Upside Down. They didn't talk about the bats or the hospital or the way Eddie still flinched at certain sounds. Steve just sat there until Eddie's hands stopped shaking around the cup, then drove back toward town with the radio low. Eddie watched the taillights until they disappeared and went inside to change the sheets.

It happened three more times that month. On the fourth, Eddie was already on the steps when Steve pulled in. He didn't bring coffee. He brought a blanket from the back seat and draped it over both of them without comment. Eddie leaned into the warmth and didn't pull away when Steve's hand settled, careful, on his knee.

---

They closed the store early one Thursday because Melvin's daughter called and said the old man had a cold. Steve locked the front door. Eddie flipped the sign. The rain had stopped but the air still felt heavy. They stood in the aisle between the rock and the new releases, neither of them moving toward the exit.

"Dustin asked about you," Steve said. His voice was quiet. "Said you haven't been by the trailer in a while."

"Been busy."

"With what?"

Eddie looked at the shelf beside him. Someone had put a copy of a Duran Duran record in the wrong place. He fixed it. "Existing. It's a full-time job these days."

Steve huffed a laugh that didn't quite land. "Yeah. I know."

The silence stretched. Eddie could hear the low hum of the cooler in the back and the drip of the roof outside. He wanted to say something that wasn't a joke. The words stuck. Steve shifted his weight. Their shoes were close on the linoleum.

"You can come over," Steve said. "After. If you want. I've got the place to myself until Sunday. Robin's at her mom's."

Eddie nodded once. "Okay."

They drove separate cars. Eddie followed the taillights through the wet streets and parked behind Steve's BMW in the driveway. The house was dark except for the porch light. Inside, Steve turned on the lamp in the living room and left the rest of the lights off. He made more coffee. They sat on opposite ends of the couch with the television on low, some late-night movie neither of them was watching.

Halfway through, Eddie's leg started to ache. He stretched it out. Steve noticed. He always noticed.

"You want ice?"

"It's fine."

Steve got up anyway. He came back with a bag of frozen peas wrapped in a dish towel and handed it over without making it a thing. Eddie pressed it against the scar on his thigh and let out a slow breath. Steve sat closer this time. Not touching, but near enough that Eddie could feel the heat of him.

"I keep thinking it's going to come back," Eddie said. The words came out rough. "Not the whole thing. Just—pieces. Like it left something in me and it's still looking for a way out."

Steve was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "I get that."

"Do you?"

"Yeah." Steve's hand rested on the cushion between them, palm up. Not reaching. Just there. "Sometimes I wake up and I can't remember if the kids are safe. Have to check the phone. Drive past the Byers place even though they're not there anymore. It doesn't stop."

Eddie looked at the hand. He set the peas aside and covered it with his own. Steve's fingers turned and laced through his, careful of the old burns on Eddie's knuckles. They sat like that until the movie ended and the screen went to static. Neither of them moved to turn it off.

---

The first kiss happened on a Sunday morning in the store, before they opened. Eddie was restocking the singles. Steve was counting the till. The light through the front windows was thin and gold. Eddie straightened and Steve was right there, close enough that Eddie could see the faint freckles across his nose that the summer had left behind.

"You've got—" Steve said, and reached up. His thumb brushed the corner of Eddie's mouth. There was nothing there. They both knew it.

Eddie caught his wrist. Held it. Steve's pulse jumped under his fingers. For a second neither of them breathed. Then Eddie leaned in and kissed him, soft and uncertain, the way you test something that might break. Steve made a small sound and kissed back, one hand coming up to rest at the side of Eddie's neck. They stayed like that until a car passed outside and the moment thinned.

They didn't talk about it for the rest of the shift. They didn't need to. When they locked up, Steve caught Eddie's hand again on the way to the cars and didn't let go until they reached the parking lot.

---

It wasn't sudden after that. It was slow the way everything between them had been—careful, built out of small things. Eddie stayed over more nights than he went home. Steve learned which side of the bed Eddie preferred and stopped asking if the lights needed to stay on. They still didn't talk much about the worst of it, but the silences got easier to sit in.

One night in early spring, Eddie woke to Steve sitting on the edge of the mattress, elbows on his knees, breathing like he'd run somewhere. Eddie sat up. The room was dark except for the streetlight through the blinds.

"Bad one?" he asked.

Steve nodded. Eddie shifted closer and put a hand between his shoulder blades. Steve leaned into it. After a minute he turned and pressed his face against Eddie's collarbone. Eddie held him there, one hand in his hair, until the shaking eased.

"I'm here," he said. It was the only thing that felt true.

Steve's arms came around his waist. "I know."

They stayed like that until the sky started to lighten. Eddie's leg ached. Steve's heart was still beating too fast against his chest. Outside, a bird started up in the tree by the driveway. Neither of them moved to get up. There would be time for coffee and the store and the rest of the day. For now, this was enough.
