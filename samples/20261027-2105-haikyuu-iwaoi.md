The gym lights had been off for twenty minutes before Iwaizumi finally locked the side door. Rain came down hard against the metal overhang, the kind that turned the street into a shallow river and soaked a hoodie in the time it took to cross one intersection. He stood there longer than necessary, bag over one shoulder, watching water sheet off the curb. The apartment was ten minutes away if he walked fast. Oikawa would already be home. He always was on Thursdays.

Iwaizumi stepped out.

The walk was the same as every other night that month. Same cracked pavement, same closed bakery, same convenience store clerk who nodded when Iwaizumi shook water off his sleeves. Ordinary. That was what he had wanted after high school: ordinary. No more national camps, no more setting drills until his shoulders locked, no more Oikawa calling his name across a court like it was the only sound that mattered. Just classes, a part-time shift at the university athletic clinic, and a two-bedroom place that still smelled like Oikawa’s shampoo and the cheap curry they ate three nights a week.

Light showed under the apartment door. Iwaizumi toed off his shoes and peeled the wet hoodie over his head. From the kitchen came the steady scrape of a spoon.

“You’re late,” Oikawa said. He didn’t turn around. He stood at the stove in an old Seijoh shirt and gray sweatpants, hair tied up with a rubber band that was losing its grip. Flour dusted one cheek. “I made the good curry. Not the packet.”

“Clinic ran long.” Iwaizumi dropped his bag by the table. “You stretch after practice?”

“Yes, Mother.”

Oikawa ladled rice, then curry, then set the bowls down. They ate. Rain hit the windows hard enough to cover the silence. Iwaizumi watched the way Oikawa’s fingers moved when he reached for the pickled ginger—the small scar across one knuckle from a middle-school block, the way he still held a spoon like he was about to serve. When Oikawa glanced up, Iwaizumi looked at his rice.

Afterward Oikawa washed while Iwaizumi dried. Their elbows bumped twice. Neither moved away.

“I have early clinic hours,” Iwaizumi said.

“Mm.”

“You should sleep. Your knee—”

“It’s fine.”

“It’s not fine. You ice it for fifteen minutes and then walk around like an idiot the rest of the night.”

Oikawa’s mouth twitched. “You counted?”

“I live here.”

The water ran. Oikawa rinsed a bowl and set it in the rack. He stayed at the sink longer than he needed to. When he finally turned, the circles under his eyes looked darker under the kitchen light.

“I don’t want to go back,” he said.

Iwaizumi waited.

“To the court. Not like this. I keep thinking if I just push harder it’ll feel the way it used to. It doesn’t. The sets are clean and nothing else is.” He rubbed his thumb over the scar. “You don’t have to stay up. I know you have work.”

“I’m not leaving you talking to the dishes.”

Oikawa huffed a laugh that didn’t reach his eyes. “Come sit, then.”

They moved to the couch. Oikawa stretched his leg out. Iwaizumi got the ice pack from the freezer, wrapped it in a clean towel, and settled it over the knee. Oikawa watched his hands.

“You don’t have to do that.”

“Shut up.”

They sat. The television stayed off. Rain filled the room. Iwaizumi kept one hand on the towel, not because the pack needed holding, but because moving it would mean shifting away. After a while Oikawa’s head tipped sideways until it rested against Iwaizumi’s shoulder. His breathing slowed. Iwaizumi stayed exactly where he was until the ice melted and the apartment went quiet around them.

---

Saturday morning the rain had stopped. Oikawa was already at the kitchen table when Iwaizumi came out, laptop open, one knee propped on a chair. He looked up when the floor creaked.

“There’s coffee.”

Iwaizumi poured a cup and leaned against the counter. “How’s the knee?”

“Manageable.” Oikawa closed the laptop halfway. “I canceled the extra session today. Coach didn’t argue.”

“Good.”

They didn’t talk much while Iwaizumi made eggs. Oikawa scrolled something on his phone and occasionally made a small sound that could have been amusement or irritation. When the plates were done Iwaizumi set one in front of him. Oikawa ate without looking up, then said, “There’s a match tonight. University team. Not mine. Just watching.”

“You want company?”

Oikawa’s fingers paused on the phone. “If you’re free.”

They went. The gym was smaller than Seijoh’s, the crowd thinner. They sat high enough that the players looked like they were moving through water. Oikawa watched the setter with his arms crossed, mouth tight. Halfway through the second set the opposing team ran a quick attack that landed clean. Oikawa’s shoulders dropped a fraction.

“That was pretty,” he said under his breath.

Iwaizumi glanced at him. “You could do it.”

“Not right now.”

After the match they walked back through the damp streets. Oikawa was quieter than usual. When they passed the convenience store he stopped for onigiri and two cans of coffee. He handed one to Iwaizumi without asking. They ate standing under the awning while the last of the evening traffic moved past.

“I used to think if I just got better no one could touch us,” Oikawa said. He peeled the plastic off his onigiri carefully. “Now I’m not sure what ‘better’ is supposed to look like when my knee keeps reminding me I’m twenty and already counting minutes.”

“You’re still the best setter I’ve ever played with.”

Oikawa looked at him then. The streetlight caught the edge of his jaw. “You don’t have to say that.”

“I’m not saying it because I have to.”

They finished the food and walked the rest of the way in silence. At the apartment door Oikawa unlocked it and held it open. Inside, he toed off his shoes and stood in the entryway like he wasn’t sure where to go next. Iwaizumi hung up his jacket.

“Iwa-chan.”

“Yeah.”

“If I take a break. A real one. Not just one session. Would that be—” He stopped. “I don’t know what I’m asking.”

“You’re asking if it’s allowed to stop for a minute.”

Oikawa’s mouth curved, small and tired. “Something like that.”

“It’s allowed.”

They stood there. The hallway light hummed. Oikawa reached out and straightened the collar of Iwaizumi’s shirt where it had folded under his jacket. His fingers stayed a second longer than necessary. Then he stepped back.

“I’m going to ice the knee and sleep,” he said. “Don’t stay up.”

“I won’t.”

Oikawa went down the hall. Iwaizumi listened to the freezer open and close, the quiet shift of weight on the couch, and stayed in the entryway until the apartment settled again.

---

Three weeks later the rain had become intermittent and Oikawa’s knee no longer swelled after practice. He still iced it, but he did it without being told. Iwaizumi came home one evening to find the kitchen table covered in notes and a half-finished set of drills written in Oikawa’s careful handwriting. Oikawa looked up from the floor where he was stretching.

“I talked to the athletic trainer today,” he said. “We’re adjusting the load. Not stopping. Just not pretending the knee doesn’t exist.”

“Good.”

Oikawa sat up. “Come here.”

Iwaizumi crossed the room. Oikawa caught his wrist and tugged until he sat on the floor across from him. Their knees almost touched. Oikawa studied his face for a moment, then said, “You’ve been carrying the ice packs and the grocery lists and the fact that I sometimes forget to eat like it’s your job.”

“It is my job. Athletic training major, remember.”

“Not what I meant.” Oikawa’s thumb moved once across the inside of Iwaizumi’s wrist. “I keep waiting for you to get tired of it.”

“I’m not tired.”

“You could be. Anyone else would be.”

“I’m not anyone else.”

Oikawa’s grip loosened but didn’t let go. The apartment was quiet except for the low hum of the refrigerator. Outside, a car passed. Oikawa looked at their hands, then back up.

“I don’t know how to do this without you,” he said. “I never have. Even when I was being an ass about it.”

“You’re still an ass about plenty of things.”

“True.” Oikawa’s mouth lifted at one corner. “But I’m trying not to be an ass about this.”

He leaned forward. The kiss was careful, almost hesitant, like he expected to be pushed away. Iwaizumi didn’t push. He set one hand against the side of Oikawa’s neck and kissed him back, slow, until the careful part gave way and Oikawa made a small sound against his mouth. When they pulled apart Oikawa stayed close, forehead resting against Iwaizumi’s.

“Okay,” he said, quiet.

“Okay.”

They stayed on the floor. Oikawa’s hand stayed on Iwaizumi’s wrist. Outside the rain started again, soft this time, and the apartment held them both while it fell.
