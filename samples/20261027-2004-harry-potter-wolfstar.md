The kitchen smelled of rosemary and the particular damp that clung to the cottage after rain. Remus set the kettle on the stove and watched the blue flame catch. Outside the window the garden had gone to seed; the last of the summer roses had dropped their petals overnight, and the grass was long enough to hide the path.

Sirius came in from the shed with his sleeves rolled to the elbows and a streak of oil across one forearm. He stopped when he saw Remus at the counter, then crossed to the sink and turned the tap.

“You’ll ruin the floor,” Remus said. Water already spotted the boards where Sirius stood.

“Floor’s older than both of us.” Sirius scrubbed at the oil with a cloth. The motion pulled the muscle along his shoulder. “James said he’d come by later. Bring the boy.”

Remus nodded. He poured the water over the tea leaves and set the pot to steep. The silence between them was not new. It had settled in the weeks after the Ministry cleared Sirius’s name, after the house in London proved too loud, after they had taken the keys to this place on the edge of the village and started, without discussion, to live in it together.

“You’re not sleeping,” Sirius said. He dried his hands on the same cloth and folded it over the rack.

“I’m sleeping.”

“You’re walking the landing at three in the morning. The boards creak.”

Remus poured two cups. He left Sirius’s black, the way he had always taken it. “The moon is close.”

“That’s not for another week.”

“Close enough.” Remus set the cups on the table. The wood was scarred from years of other tenants; he had sanded the worst of the gouges himself when they first arrived. “Sit.”

Sirius sat. He wrapped both hands around the cup and did not drink. The kitchen clock ticked. Outside a thrush tried the same three notes and failed.

“I keep thinking about the cell,” Sirius said. “Not the Dementors. The quiet after. When the guards stopped coming because there was nothing left to take. I used to count the cracks in the stone. There were forty-seven above the bed. I knew them better than I knew my own face.”

Remus looked at his own hands. The scars across the knuckles had faded to white lines. “You don’t have to tell me.”

“I do.” Sirius set the cup down. The tea sloshed. “Because you keep looking at me like you’re waiting for the other one to come back. The one who left. And he isn’t. He’s still in that cell, counting cracks. This one is here. He doesn’t know how to be in a house that doesn’t smell like metal and piss. He doesn’t know what to do with his hands when they’re not holding a wand or a bottle.”

The thrush tried again. Remus stood, crossed to the window, and closed it. The sound of the latch was small.

“I wasn’t waiting for anyone,” he said. “I was waiting to see if you would stay.”

“I’m staying.”

“You say that.” Remus turned. The light from the window caught the grey at Sirius’s temples. “You said it last month when the roof leaked. You said it when the neighbour’s dog kept you up. You pack a bag every time you go into the village and you don’t unpack it until I ask.”

Sirius’s mouth tightened. He looked at the oil still caught under his thumbnail. “The bag is habit.”

“Then break the habit.”

They ate the bread Remus had bought that morning. It was still warm in the middle. Sirius tore it with his fingers and left the crusts. Remus collected them without comment and set them aside for the birds. The tea went cold. Neither of them stood to reheat it.

After the plates were cleared Sirius stayed at the table. He traced one of the sanded gouges with a fingertip. The wood had taken the stain unevenly; the scar showed darker than the rest.

“I bought seeds in the village last week,” he said. “The woman at the shop said they would come up before the frost if I put them in now. I haven’t planted them.”

Remus dried his hands on a towel. “Why not?”

“Because if they come up I have to water them. And if I water them I have to be here when they need water.” Sirius looked at the window. The thrush had given up. “It is easier not to start things that expect you to stay.”

Remus folded the towel and hung it. “The rosemary is still alive. Someone watered that.”

“You did.”

“I did.” Remus leaned against the counter. The edge pressed into the small of his back. “You could help.”

Sirius did not answer. He stood, took the crusts from the plate, and carried them outside. Remus watched him scatter them near the hedge. A pair of sparrows dropped from the roof and began to quarrel over the pieces. Sirius stayed until they settled, then came back in and washed his hands again.

In the afternoon James arrived with Harry. The boy had grown another inch since spring. He stood on the garden path with his hands in his pockets and looked at the long grass as if he were measuring it for a match.

“Uncle Remus,” he said. The name still sat awkwardly in his mouth. “Dad said you needed help with the fence.”

“The fence can wait,” Remus answered. “Come inside. There’s cake.”

Harry glanced at Sirius, who had stayed on the step. Sirius lifted one shoulder. Harry went in.

James stayed on the path. He studied the house, the new glass in the kitchen window, the paint Remus had put on the door.

“He looks better,” James said.

“He’s eating.”

“That’s not what I meant.” James kicked at a clump of grass. “He still flinches when a door closes too hard. I saw it at the station last week.”

Remus watched Harry through the open door. The boy had found the cake and was cutting careful squares. “The flinch will take longer than the eating.”

“And you?”

“I’m fine.”

James made a sound that was not quite a laugh. “You’ve said that since we were fifteen. It was a lie then.”

They stood until Harry came back out with two plates. He offered one to Sirius without speaking. Sirius took it. Their fingers brushed. Harry did not pull away.

Harry stayed for another hour. He helped Sirius carry the spare boards from the shed to the fence line, even though Remus had said the fence could wait. They measured, marked, and set the posts. Harry talked about the train and the new Defence teacher and the way the corridor outside the potions classroom still smelled of burnt sugar. Sirius listened and answered in short sentences. When Harry laughed at something, Sirius’s mouth moved in a shape that was almost a smile.

James watched from the kitchen window with Remus. “He’s good with him,” James said.

“He’s careful.”

“Careful is more than I expected.” James turned the mug in his hands. “I keep waiting for the old Sirius to turn up and start a fight in the garden. He hasn’t.”

“The old one is still counting cracks,” Remus said. He rinsed James’s mug and set it on the rack. “This one is learning the difference between a door that locks and a door that closes.”

After they left, the light had gone thin. Remus washed the plates. Sirius dried. They worked without speaking until the rack was full.

“I keep the bag by the door,” Sirius said, “because if I put it in the wardrobe I have to open the wardrobe. And the wardrobe has your coats in it. And if I see the coats I have to think about what happens when the moon is closer than a week and you don’t come downstairs for breakfast.”

Remus set the last plate on the rack. Water ran down his wrist. “I come downstairs.”

“Not always.” Sirius folded the towel once, then again. “Last month you stayed up there for two days. I left food on the landing. You ate it. You didn’t open the door.”

“I didn’t want you to see.”

“I have seen.” Sirius’s voice stayed level. “I saw you at sixteen. I saw you at twenty. I saw the scars you tried to hide under the sleeves. I am not afraid of the moon. I am afraid of the part where you decide I shouldn’t have to be in the room with it.”

Remus turned off the tap. The pipes knocked once and went quiet. “If I open the door, you will stay on the other side of it.”

“Try me.”

They went upstairs together. Remus opened the bedroom door. The room smelled of the lavender he kept in the drawer with his shirts. The bed was made. Sirius’s side of the wardrobe was empty except for one hanger.

Sirius set the bag on the floor. He opened it, took out the spare shirt and the toothbrush and the book he had been reading for three weeks, and put them where they belonged. He closed the bag and slid it under the bed. The floorboards did not creak.

“There,” he said.

Remus sat on the edge of the mattress. His hands rested on his knees. “The moon is in six days.”

“I’ll be here.”

“You don’t have to—”

“I will be here.” Sirius sat beside him. Their shoulders touched. The contact was brief and then not. “You can lock the door if you want. I’ll still be on this side of it when you open it again.”

Remus looked at the window. The garden was almost dark. The thrush had stopped. “I don’t want to lock it.”

“Then don’t.”

They stayed like that until the room was fully dark. Remus stood first and lit the lamp. The light made the scars on his hands look newer than they were. Sirius watched him do it. Neither of them spoke about the years between the cell and the cottage, or the years before the cell. They did not need to. The bag was under the bed. The coats were in the wardrobe. The moon would come, and when it left, the kitchen would still smell of rosemary, and someone would still be downstairs waiting for the kettle to boil.

In the morning Remus found the seed packet on the kitchen table, already opened. A thin line of dark soil ran along the edge of the garden where the grass had been cut back. Sirius was on his knees with a trowel, pressing the soil flat with the flat of his hand. He did not look up when Remus opened the door.

“They might not take,” Sirius said. “The woman said the frost could still get them.”

“They might.” Remus stepped onto the path. The boards were damp under his feet. “I’ll water them if you forget.”

Sirius nodded once. He covered the last of the seeds and sat back on his heels. Dirt clung to the creases of his palms. Remus went inside, filled the kettle, and set it on the flame. When he looked out again Sirius was still there, watching the turned earth as if it might answer him. The sparrows had returned to the hedge. The thrush tried its three notes and, this time, finished the phrase.
