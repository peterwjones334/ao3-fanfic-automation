The Weight of the Quiet

The call came in at 2:17 a.m. Structure fire, two-story duplex, possible occupants. Buck was already in the engine bay when the tones hit, boots half-laced, the way he always was when the nights stretched too long and sleep stayed out of reach. Eddie met him at the door of the truck, face set, gear bag slung over one shoulder.

“You good?” Eddie asked, voice low under the diesel rumble.

Buck nodded once. “Yeah.”

They didn’t talk much on the way. The streets were empty enough that the lights painted everything red and blue in pulses, and Buck watched the way Eddie’s hands rested on his knees, fingers tapping once, twice, then still. Same rhythm he’d had since the well. Same one he got when something felt off before it even started.

The duplex was already half-involved when they arrived. Flames pushed out of the upper windows in steady sheets. A woman on the sidewalk kept saying her sister was inside, third floor, left side. Buck took the information, repeated it back, and moved.

Inside, the stairs were still holding. Smoke banked low. Buck went up first because that was the habit, and Eddie let him because arguing wasted seconds. They cleared the second floor empty. On the third, the left door was closed and hot. Buck tested the knob with the back of his glove, then kicked. The room beyond was black with smoke and bright with fire along the far wall. A shape on the floor. Eddie covered him while Buck dropped, got an arm under the woman, and started back. Halfway to the stairs the ceiling let go.

Buck heard the crack and shoved. The woman went forward into Eddie’s hands. Then the weight hit him across the shoulders and he went down hard on one knee. Something sharp punched through the turnout coat at his ribs. He stayed low, got a breath that tasted like ash, and kept crawling until the air changed and hands pulled him the rest of the way.

Outside, the woman was coughing and alive. Buck sat on the bumper of the engine while a medic cut the coat open. The piece of joist had gone in clean, missed the lung, caught the muscle and a rib. He watched Eddie talk to the captain, watched the set of his shoulders, and didn’t say anything when the medic told him he was going to the hospital.

Eddie drove him. The truck stayed with the crew. In the passenger seat of Eddie’s truck, Buck kept his arm pressed against the bandage and stared at the dashboard clock. 3:41. The radio was off. Eddie’s hands were steady on the wheel.

“You should’ve let me take point,” Eddie said after four blocks.

“You would’ve done the same thing.”

“That’s not an answer.”

Buck closed his eyes. The pain was a clean line under the meds they’d given him. “She’s out. That’s the answer.”

Eddie didn’t speak again until they were in the ER bay. He filled out the forms because Buck’s hands were shaking by then, small tremors he couldn’t hide once the adrenaline dropped. When the nurse asked about next of kin, Eddie said his own name without looking up.

They kept Buck for observation until morning. The rib was cracked, the wound deep but clean. He was discharged with antibiotics and instructions not to lift anything heavier than a coffee mug for ten days. Eddie took the papers, drove him home, and parked in the driveway like he still lived there.

Inside, the house was quiet. Christopher was at school. The living room still held the evidence of yesterday—textbooks stacked on the coffee table, one of Buck’s hoodies draped over the arm of the couch. Eddie set the discharge papers on the counter and turned.

“Sit.”

Buck sat. Eddie cut the old bandage away, cleaned the site with the careful economy of someone who’d done field dressings more times than he could count, and taped a fresh one in place. His fingers were warm where they held Buck steady. Neither of them mentioned it.

“You can take the bed,” Eddie said. “I’ll sleep on the couch.”

“I’m not kicking you out of your own bed.”

“You’re not.” Eddie rinsed the scissors in the sink. “I’m choosing it.”

Buck stayed on the couch that night anyway. He woke twice to the sound of Eddie moving through the house—once for water, once to check the lock on the front door. At 6:40 the light under the kitchen door came on. Buck pushed himself upright, ignored the pull in his side, and went to the doorway.

Eddie was at the stove in a T-shirt and sweatpants, eggs in one pan, coffee already poured into two mugs. He didn’t turn around.

“You should be lying down.”

“I can stand for five minutes.”

Eddie slid a plate across the counter. Scrambled eggs, toast, nothing that required chewing with both sides of his mouth. Buck took it and sat at the table. Eddie sat across from him with his own plate and ate without looking up. The kitchen smelled like coffee and the last of the rain that had come through overnight.

“Chris asked about you this morning,” Eddie said after a while. “I told him you were fine.”

“I am fine.”

“You will be.” Eddie set his fork down. “That’s not the same thing.”

Buck studied the edge of his plate. “You don’t have to stay. I can manage.”

“I know.” Eddie’s voice was even. “I’m staying anyway.”

They spent the day like that. Eddie went to the station long enough to finish paperwork and came back with soup from the place on Fourth. Buck dozed on the couch with the television on low. When he woke the second time, Eddie was on the floor in front of the coffee table, sorting through a stack of Christopher’s school papers and correcting math problems in the margins with a pen. The late light cut across his shoulders. Buck watched the way his mouth moved when he concentrated, the small crease between his brows, and felt the ache in his chest that had nothing to do with the rib.

At dusk Eddie made dinner. Pasta, simple, the kind that didn’t need much. They ate on the couch because the table felt too formal. Buck’s pain meds were wearing off; he shifted once and stopped when Eddie’s hand settled on his knee, light, just enough to keep him still.

“Stop moving,” Eddie said.

“I’m not.”

“You are.” The hand stayed. “I’ve got you.”

Buck looked at the hand, then at Eddie’s face. The television was still on, some old movie neither of them was watching. Eddie’s thumb moved once against the seam of Buck’s jeans, then stopped. Neither of them pulled away.

“This isn’t—” Buck started.

“I know what it is.” Eddie’s voice was quiet. “I’ve known for a while. I just didn’t want to make it harder.”

Buck swallowed. The words sat between them, solid as the coffee table. “It already is.”

Eddie nodded once. His hand didn’t move. “Then we stop pretending it isn’t.”

They didn’t kiss. Buck’s side hurt too much for that, and Eddie didn’t push. They sat until the movie ended and the credits rolled in silence. Eddie cleared the plates. Buck stayed on the couch and listened to the sound of water in the sink, the ordinary noise of someone else in the house who wasn’t leaving.

Later, when Eddie came back with two glasses of water, Buck took his and set it on the floor. Eddie sat on the edge of the couch, close enough that their knees touched.

“You can stay,” Buck said. “If you want. The bed’s big enough.”

Eddie looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, stood, and offered a hand. Buck took it. They moved slowly down the hall. In the bedroom Eddie turned down the covers on the far side and waited while Buck eased himself down. The mattress dipped when Eddie lay beside him, careful of the space between them.

Buck closed his eyes. The pain was still there, a steady throb under the bandage, but the rest of the room was quiet. Eddie’s breathing evened out. After a while Buck felt the mattress shift again, felt the careful weight of an arm settle across his waist, low enough to miss the injury.

“Sleep,” Eddie said against the dark.

Buck did.

In the morning the light was gray through the blinds. Eddie was already awake, propped on one elbow, watching him with the same expression he got when he was checking a patient who was finally stable. Buck’s side ached. His mouth was dry. Eddie’s arm was still across him.

“Still here,” Buck said.

“Yeah.” Eddie’s thumb moved once against the fabric of Buck’s shirt. “Still here.”

They stayed like that until the alarm on Eddie’s phone went off and Christopher’s bus was due. Eddie got up first. Buck listened to the sound of the shower, then the coffee maker, then the front door opening and closing when Eddie walked Christopher to the corner. When Eddie came back he brought a fresh mug and set it on the nightstand.

“Ten days,” Eddie said. “Then we figure out the rest.”

Buck took the mug. The coffee was exactly how he liked it. Outside, the street was ordinary. Inside, the house held the weight of two people who had finally stopped counting the reasons not to stay.
