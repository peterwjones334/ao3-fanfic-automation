The van smelled like old cigarettes and the cheap cologne Eddie had started wearing again after the hospital. Steve sat on the edge of the open side door, one foot on the gravel of the trailer park lot, the other tucked under him. Eddie was inside, on the thin mattress that still sagged in the middle, trying to get a sock on without using his left hand much.

“You don’t have to hover,” Eddie said. His voice was rough from the last round of antibiotics. “I’m not gonna keel over putting on a sock.”

“I’m not hovering.” Steve watched the way Eddie’s fingers fumbled the fabric. The scars on the left forearm were still pink and raised. “I’m waiting. There’s a difference.”

Eddie snorted and finally got the sock over his heel. “Sure. Waiting. Like you waited outside the hospital every day for three weeks.”

Steve didn’t answer that. He picked at a loose thread on his jeans instead. The kids were at the Wheelers’ for movie night. Robin was covering a shift at Family Video. That left the two of them and the quiet that had settled over the trailer since Wayne left for the night shift at the plant.

Eddie swung his legs over the side of the mattress and stood. He was careful about it, the way he’d been careful for the last month. The doctors had said the nerve damage might never fully go away. Eddie had called it “metal detector arm” once, trying to make it a joke. Steve hadn’t laughed.

“You hungry?” Steve asked. “I brought the good cereal. The one with the marshmallows.”

“You mean the one that tastes like cardboard and regret?” Eddie grabbed a flannel from the hook by the door. It was Steve’s, left behind last week. Eddie put it on without commenting. The sleeves covered most of the scars. “Yeah. I’m hungry.”

They ate at the little table that doubled as Eddie’s desk. The cereal was stale. Neither of them mentioned it. Steve watched Eddie’s left hand rest on the table, fingers slightly curled, the way they always were now when he wasn’t thinking about them. Eddie caught him looking.

“It doesn’t hurt as much,” he said. “Most days. Just feels… wrong. Like the skin’s too tight.”

Steve nodded. He’d heard the same thing from Max after her casts came off. “You doing the exercises?”

“Every day, Mom.” Eddie’s mouth twitched. “Want me to demonstrate?”

“I want you to not reinjure yourself because you’re stubborn.”

“Pot, kettle.” Eddie pushed the bowl away. “You still sleeping in the guest room at the Hendersons’?”

“Sometimes.” Steve scraped the last of the milk with his spoon. “Sometimes the couch at Robin’s. Depends on whose parents are being less weird about it.”

Eddie was quiet for a minute. The only sound was the fridge humming and the distant traffic on the main road. “You could stay here,” he said. “Wayne doesn’t care. Trailer’s got the pull-out. Or the floor. Whatever.”

Steve looked up. Eddie was studying the cereal box like it held the answers to something. His hair was longer than it had been before everything, the curls still damp from a shower Steve hadn’t been invited to watch but had heard through the thin walls.

“You sure?” Steve asked.

“I’m sure I’m tired of you showing up looking like you slept in your car,” Eddie said. He stood and took both bowls to the sink. His left hand was careful around the ceramic. “And I’m sure I sleep better when there’s someone else breathing in the next room. Sue me.”

Steve stayed.

---

The first night he took the pull-out. The mattress was thinner than the one in the van and smelled like Wayne’s work shirts. Steve lay on his back and listened to Eddie move around in the bedroom. The light under the door stayed on for a long time. When it finally clicked off, Steve turned onto his side and tried not to count the minutes.

He woke up at three because Eddie was standing in the doorway.

“Sorry,” Eddie said. His voice was low. “Didn’t mean to wake you. Leg’s being a bitch.”

Steve sat up. The trailer was cold. “You want the heating pad?”

“Already used it.” Eddie shifted his weight. In the dark, the scars on his arm were just shadows. “Can I—fuck. This is stupid.”

“What?”

“Sit. Just for a minute. The quiet’s too loud when it’s just me.”

Steve moved over. Eddie sat on the edge of the pull-out, careful not to jostle it. Their knees didn’t touch. Steve could smell the toothpaste Eddie had used and the faint metallic scent that still clung to him sometimes, no matter how many showers he took.

They didn’t talk. After a while Eddie’s breathing evened out. He didn’t go back to his room. Steve stayed awake until the sky outside the window turned gray, listening to Eddie sleep and trying not to think about how easy it would be to reach over and fix the way Eddie’s left hand was resting, palm up, fingers curled like they were waiting for something.

---

It became a pattern. Steve brought dinner more nights than not. Eddie complained about the food and ate it anyway. They watched movies with the sound low so they wouldn’t wake Wayne when he got home early. Eddie started leaving the bedroom door open. Steve started keeping a toothbrush in the bathroom cup next to Eddie’s.

On a Thursday in late October, the power went out. A tree had come down on a line somewhere past the park. The trailer went dark except for the emergency light Wayne kept under the sink. Eddie swore and dug out candles from a drawer.

“Romantic,” Steve said, watching Eddie strike a match with his right hand.

“Shut up.” Eddie lit three candles and set them on the table. The light made the scars on his arm look deeper. “We could go to Robin’s. She’s got power.”

“She’s got a date,” Steve said. “With the girl from the record store. The one with the nose ring.”

Eddie made a face. “Good for her.” He sat on the couch and pulled his feet up. The left one dragged a little. Steve noticed. He always noticed.

They ended up on the floor with the emergency blanket and a deck of cards Eddie found in the junk drawer. Eddie dealt with his right hand. Steve watched the left one rest on his knee, the fingers twitching every so often like they were trying to remember how to move on their own.

“You ever think about leaving?” Eddie asked. He was looking at his cards. “Hawkins. Just… getting in the van and going.”

“Sometimes.” Steve discarded a three. “Not as much as I used to.”

“Why not?”

Steve shrugged. The candlelight caught on the ring Eddie wore on his right hand, the one he’d dug out of a box after the hospital. “People I care about are still here.”

Eddie was quiet for a long time. Then he said, “Yeah. Me too.”

They played until the candles burned low. When the last one guttered, Eddie didn’t move to find another. Steve could just make out the shape of him in the dark.

“Steve,” Eddie said.

“Yeah.”

“I’m not good at this.”

“At cards?”

“At—whatever this is. The staying. The not running.” Eddie’s voice was careful. “I keep waiting for you to get tired of it. Of me. Of the hand and the nightmares and the way I still flinch at loud noises.”

Steve set his cards down. He could hear Eddie breathing, a little too fast. “I’m not tired.”

“You will be.”

“Maybe.” Steve shifted closer. Their shoulders touched. Eddie didn’t pull away. “But I’m not right now. And I’m not planning on being tired tomorrow either.”

Eddie laughed once, short and rough. “That’s a shitty plan.”

“I’ve had worse.”

They stayed like that until the power came back on with a hum and a click of the fridge. Eddie blinked in the sudden light. His left hand was still resting on his knee. Steve reached over and covered it with his own. Eddie’s fingers twitched, then slowly uncurled, just enough to let Steve’s settle between them.

“Okay,” Eddie said. His voice was quiet. “Okay.”

---

Winter settled in hard. The trailer park roads iced over. Steve started keeping a bag in the van so he wouldn’t have to drive back to the Hendersons’ when the weather turned. Eddie’s physical therapist said the strength in his left hand was improving. Eddie still dropped things sometimes. Steve stopped offering to pick them up unless Eddie asked.

On the first clear night in December, they sat on the steps outside the trailer and watched the stars. Eddie had a cigarette he wasn’t smoking, just turning between the fingers of his right hand. Steve had a thermos of the coffee Wayne had left in the pot.

“I used to think,” Eddie said, “that if I got out of here, everything would be different. I’d be different. Less… this.”

“And now?”

Eddie shrugged. The motion pulled at the scar on his shoulder. Steve had seen it once, when Eddie changed shirts without thinking. It ran from the base of his neck down across his collarbone, pale and uneven. “Now I think maybe different isn’t the point. Maybe staying is.”

Steve took a drink of coffee. It was bitter. “I used to think I had to be the guy who fixed everything. The one who showed up with the bat and the bandages and the plan.”

“You still do that.”

“Yeah.” Steve set the thermos down. “But I’m getting better at not doing it when you don’t want me to.”

Eddie was quiet. Then he reached over and took Steve’s hand, the way Steve had taken his weeks ago. His left hand. The grip was lighter than it used to be. Steve could feel the tremor in it, the way the muscles still fought him sometimes. He didn’t tighten his own grip. He just held on.

“I’m still scared,” Eddie said. “That this is temporary. That you’ll wake up one day and decide you want something easier.”

“I don’t want easier,” Steve said. “I want this. You. Even when you’re a pain in the ass about the cereal and the socks and the way you leave wet towels on the bathroom floor.”

Eddie huffed a laugh. “I do that on purpose now. To see if you’ll say something.”

“I know.”

They sat until the cold got into their bones. When they went inside, Eddie didn’t let go of Steve’s hand until they reached the bedroom doorway. He stopped there, like he was waiting for permission or for Steve to change his mind.

Steve didn’t. He followed Eddie in and closed the door behind them. The bed was narrow. They made it work. Eddie’s left arm rested across Steve’s chest, the weight of it careful and deliberate. Steve listened to Eddie’s heartbeat slow and thought about the way some things took time, and how that didn’t make them any less real.

In the morning, Eddie’s hand was still there. The fingers had curled in the night, but they were warm, and they stayed.
