The rain had not stopped in three days. Harry stood at the kitchen window of the little cottage outside Hogsmeade and watched it sheet across the garden, turning the overgrown grass silver. Behind him, the kettle clicked off. He did not move to pour it.

Draco had been in the spare room for six nights. He had arrived with a single trunk, a letter from the Ministry that neither of them had read aloud, and the careful, brittle posture of someone who had not slept properly in weeks. Harry had given him the room, the key to the back door, and the instruction not to lock it if he needed anything. Draco had locked it every night.

Harry poured the tea anyway. Two cups. He set one on the table and carried the other upstairs. The spare-room door was ajar. Draco sat on the edge of the bed in shirtsleeves, one hand pressed flat to his ribs. The bandages underneath the shirt had been changed; Harry could smell the clean linen and the faint bite of dittany.

“I can do it myself,” Draco said without looking up.

“I know.” Harry set the cup on the nightstand. “You also nearly dropped the basin last night. Sit still.”

Draco’s mouth tightened. He did not argue. Harry knelt, undid the lowest buttons of the shirt, and peeled the old dressing away with the care he used on burns. The wound underneath was closing, but the edges were still angry. He cleaned it, applied the salve, and wrapped it again. Draco’s breathing stayed even until Harry’s knuckles brushed the uninjured skin just above the bandage. Then it hitched, once, and steadied.

“You don’t have to stay,” Draco said. His voice was low. “The Ministry only asked you to house me until the hearing. They did not require tea and nursing.”

“I’m not doing it for the Ministry.” Harry fastened the last pin and sat back on his heels. The floor was cold through his socks. “I’m doing it because you came here.”

Draco finally looked at him. The light from the window was grey and flat. It made the shadows under his eyes deeper. “That was not a choice. They gave me two options. This was the one that did not involve a cell.”

“Still,” Harry said. He stood. “The tea will get cold.”

They drank it downstairs in silence. Draco held the cup with both hands. Harry watched the rain. When the cups were empty, Draco stood to wash them. Harry let him. The ordinary motion of drying a plate seemed to steady something in the line of Draco’s shoulders.

That night the storm worsened. Thunder rolled low over the hills. Harry was halfway down the stairs with a book when he heard the spare-room door open. Draco stood at the top of the landing in his shirtsleeves, barefoot, one hand on the banister. His face was pale.

“The shutters,” he said. “They’re banging. I can’t—” He stopped. His free hand had gone to his ribs again.

Harry climbed the rest of the way. “I’ll fix them. Go back to bed.”

“I’ll help.”

“You won’t.” Harry kept his voice even. “Sit on the landing if you want. I’ll be five minutes.”

He went outside into the driving rain and secured the loose shutter with a sticking charm and a length of rope. When he came back in, dripping, Draco was still on the top step. He had pulled a blanket around his shoulders. Harry shed his wet jumper and sat on the step below him so their eyes were level.

“Better?” he asked.

Draco nodded once. Water dripped from Harry’s hair onto the blanket. Neither of them moved to stop it. The thunder moved farther off. Draco’s hand rested on the step between them, fingers curled. Harry looked at it, then at Draco’s face. The careful distance they had kept for six days felt thinner in the dark.

“You can stay out here if the room is too loud,” Harry said. “The sofa is long enough.”

“I don’t want the sofa.” Draco’s voice was quiet. “I want—” He stopped. His fingers flexed once on the wood. “I want to know why you said yes.”

Harry considered the question. The rain drummed on the roof. “Because you wrote the letter yourself,” he said. “Not the Ministry. You. And because I have spent eight years wondering what I would do if you ever asked me for anything that wasn’t an insult.”

Draco let out a short breath that might have been a laugh. “And this is what you decided.”

“Yes.”

They sat until the storm eased. When Draco finally stood, he offered Harry a hand up. Harry took it. The grip was brief and careful. At the spare-room door, Draco paused.

“The lock stays open tonight,” he said.

“All right,” Harry answered.

In the morning the rain had stopped. Draco was already in the kitchen when Harry came down. Two cups of tea waited on the table. The spare-room door stood open at the top of the stairs, and the key was on the table beside Harry’s cup.
