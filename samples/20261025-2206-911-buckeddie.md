The kitchen light at the station hummed the way it always did after midnight, a low fluorescent drone that made everything look a little too sharp. Buck stood at the sink with his sleeves pushed past his elbows, scrubbing a mug that had already been clean for five minutes. The call had been a structure fire in a duplex that had gone sideways when the floor gave. No one dead, but a kid had been trapped long enough that the sound of her coughing still sat behind Buck’s ribs.

Eddie came in without turning on the rest of the lights. He moved like his shoulder was louder than the rest of him. The bandage under his shirt was white against the gray fabric; Buck had watched him refuse the hospital again, the way he always did when the injury was “just” a sprain and a few stitches.

“You should ice that,” Buck said.

“Later.” Eddie set two bottles of water on the counter. One of them had the cap already cracked. He pushed that one toward Buck. “Drink.”

Buck turned the faucet off. Water clung to his hands and the edge of the sink. He dried them on a towel that smelled like the cheap detergent the department bought in bulk. “You first.”

Eddie didn’t argue. He twisted the bottle open with his left hand and drank half of it in one go. When he lowered it, a drop ran down the side of his wrist and disappeared under the cuff. Buck watched it go. He had been watching small things about Eddie for months—how he tilted his head when he was listening past the radio, how his laugh stayed low when Christopher told a joke that only the three of them understood, how he went quiet after the worst calls instead of filling the silence the way Buck used to.

“Christopher texted,” Eddie said. “Wanted to know if we were still coming over tomorrow for the science fair thing. I told him yes.”

“We are.” Buck picked up the water. The plastic was cold. “Unless your shoulder decides otherwise.”

“It won’t.”

They stood there with the dishwasher clicking through its cycle and the rest of the station asleep. Buck’s knee ached where a beam had glanced it; he kept most of his weight on the other leg. Eddie noticed. He always noticed now. He shifted half a step closer so Buck could lean if he wanted to. Buck didn’t lean. He wanted to. That was the problem that had been growing for a long time, the way wanting to had started to feel like a thing he had to manage instead of a thing he could act on.

“You were good with her,” Eddie said. The girl. The one who had been under the bed with smoke in her lungs. “She stopped panicking when you got there.”

“She stopped panicking when you told her her mom was outside.” Buck set the bottle down. His fingers left wet prints. “I just stayed in the doorway so she could see someone who wasn’t on fire.”

Eddie huffed something that wasn’t quite a laugh. “You stayed longer than you should have.”

“So did you.”

The silence after that had weight. Buck knew the shape of it. They had been circling the same conversation for weeks without starting it—after the hospital run when Eddie’s hand had stayed on Buck’s wrist longer than the pulse check required, after the night Christopher had fallen asleep between them on the couch and neither of them had moved until the credits rolled. Buck had started keeping a mental list of the times Eddie looked at him like he was deciding something. The list was long enough now that Buck stopped adding to it.

Eddie’s shoulder twitched. He rolled it once, carefully, and the motion pulled the bandage taut. Buck reached without thinking. His fingers stopped an inch from the edge of the gauze.

“Can I?” he asked.

Eddie nodded. He turned so the light hit the shoulder better. Buck peeled the tape back just enough to check the stitches. They were neat. The skin around them was hot but not worse than it should have been. He smoothed the bandage down again, thumb resting for a second longer than necessary against the warm skin above the edge. Eddie’s breath went shallow. Buck felt it under his hand.

“Still there,” Eddie said quietly.

Buck didn’t pretend not to understand. He left his hand where it was. “Yeah.”

They had been friends long enough that the next part should have been easy. It wasn’t. Buck had spent most of his life learning how to leave before anyone asked him to stay. Eddie had spent most of his learning how to carry things alone so no one else had to. Both of them were bad at the middle ground where one person put a hand on the other and waited.

Buck’s thumb moved once, a small stroke along the line of Eddie’s collarbone. Eddie closed his eyes. When he opened them again, the look he gave Buck was the same one he used when he was checking a scene for the thing that could still go wrong. Careful. Present.

“I keep thinking about what happens if I say it and it changes everything,” Eddie said.

“It already changed,” Buck answered. His voice came out lower than he expected. “We’re just still standing in the kitchen pretending it didn’t.”

Eddie turned under Buck’s hand until they were facing each other. The movement brought them close enough that Buck could see the tired lines at the corners of Eddie’s eyes and the place where soot had settled into the crease of his neck. Eddie’s good hand came up and settled at Buck’s waist, fingers curling into the fabric of his shirt like he was checking that Buck was still solid.

“Christopher knows,” Eddie said. “Not the details. Just that I look at you the way I used to look at Shannon when I was trying not to.”

Buck’s chest did something complicated. He had been in Eddie’s house enough times to know the sound of Christopher’s wheelchair on the hardwood, the way the kid announced himself before he entered a room so no one had to be startled. He had also been there for the nights Eddie sat on the edge of the couch after Christopher was asleep and stared at the blank television like he was waiting for permission to stop being only a father.

“I don’t want to be another thing you have to manage,” Buck said.

“You’re not.” Eddie’s thumb moved against Buck’s side, a small, grounding press. “You’re the thing I keep choosing even when I’m tired.”

The dishwasher finished its cycle with a soft click. Somewhere down the hall a radio chirped and went quiet again. Buck leaned in until their foreheads touched. Eddie’s skin was warm. He smelled like smoke and the mint soap from the station showers and something underneath that was just him. Buck stayed there and breathed. Eddie’s hand tightened once at his waist and then loosened, like he was reminding himself he didn’t have to hold on hard enough to leave a mark.

“Tomorrow,” Eddie said. “After the science fair. We can tell him together if you want. Or we can wait. I’m not in a hurry to get the words right if the rest of it is already true.”

Buck nodded against him. The motion made their noses brush. He felt Eddie smile, small and real. For the first time in months the wanting didn’t feel like a thing he had to keep separate from the rest of his life. It felt like the same steady pull that had kept him in the doorway with the girl under the bed, the same pull that made him check Eddie’s stitches without being asked.

“Okay,” Buck said. “Tomorrow.”

They stayed in the kitchen until the light stopped humming quite so loud. Eddie’s hand stayed at Buck’s waist. Buck’s fingers stayed at the edge of the bandage, not checking anymore, just resting. Outside, the city kept moving. Inside, the two of them stood still long enough for the next part to feel possible instead of dangerous.

When they finally stepped apart, Eddie’s shoulder had stopped looking like it hurt as much. Buck’s knee still ached. Neither of them mentioned it. They finished the water, turned off the light, and walked down the hall toward the bunks with their shoulders close enough that Buck could feel the heat of Eddie’s arm through both their shirts. Christopher’s text sat unanswered on Eddie’s phone. They would answer it in the morning. They would show up. They would figure out the rest of the sentences when they got there.

For now, the quiet between them was enough. It had Eddie’s handprint in it and Buck’s answer and the ordinary promise of tomorrow. That was more than either of them had walked into the kitchen with. It was also, Buck thought as the bunk room door clicked shut behind them, exactly what they had both been carrying for longer than they had known how to set down.
