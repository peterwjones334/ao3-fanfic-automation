The call came in as a structure fire, two-story house on the edge of the district, smoke already showing when they rolled up. Buck was first through the door with Eddie on his shoulder, the usual rhythm, the usual trust. They cleared the downstairs, found the kid in the back bedroom, and got her out. The roof went while they were coming back for the parents. Buck shoved Eddie toward the exit and took the falling beam across his shoulder instead.

Eddie dragged him clear. That was the part he kept replaying in the truck on the way to the hospital, the way Buck’s glove had locked around his wrist and refused to let go until they were outside.

At the hospital they cleaned the burns, stitched the gash, and told Buck he was lucky. Eddie stood against the wall with his arms folded and did not correct them. Lucky was a word for people who hadn’t seen the beam drop. He drove Buck home because Buck’s truck was still at the station and because the idea of leaving him to an Uber felt wrong in a way Eddie did not examine too closely.

The apartment was quiet. Christopher was at a sleepover. Eddie got Buck onto the couch with a bottle of water and the discharge instructions and then stood in the kitchen longer than he needed to, staring at the empty coffee pot.

“You can go,” Buck said from the other room. His voice was rough from the smoke. “I’m fine.”

Eddie came back with the water and set it on the table. “You’re not.”

Buck looked up at him. The left side of his face was smudged with soot they hadn’t quite got off, and the hospital gown had been replaced with a station t-shirt that hung loose over the bandages. He looked smaller than usual.

“I’m not going to die on the couch,” Buck said.

“I know.” Eddie sat on the coffee table so they were eye level. “Doesn’t mean I’m leaving.”

They didn’t talk about it after that. Eddie made soup. Buck ate half of it. When the painkillers kicked in, Buck listed sideways and Eddie caught him, eased him down until his head was on a pillow and his bad shoulder was clear of the armrest. Eddie sat on the floor with his back against the couch and listened to Buck breathe until the sound evened out.

He stayed the night.

In the morning Buck was pale and stubborn and insisted on making coffee one-handed. Eddie took the pot away from him without arguing and did it himself. They drank it at the table with the window open. The city noise came in soft and ordinary.

“You don’t have to do this,” Buck said.

Eddie looked at the way Buck’s fingers were curled around the mug, careful, like he was measuring how much he could lift without pulling the stitches. “Yeah, I do.”

He took the week off. Bobby signed the papers without asking questions. Eddie brought Christopher over on the second day and the three of them watched a movie with the volume low. Christopher fell asleep between them on the couch. Buck’s good hand rested on the boy’s hair and Eddie watched the way Buck’s face went soft and unguarded, the way it always did around Christopher, and felt something in his chest shift a fraction further out of place.

On the fourth day Buck tried to cook dinner and burned the garlic. Eddie took over without comment. They ate standing at the counter because the table still had the hospital paperwork on it. Buck’s shoulder was stiff. He kept rolling it and then stopping when the motion pulled.

“Let me look,” Eddie said.

Buck went still. “It’s fine.”

“Let me look anyway.”

In the bathroom Eddie unwrapped the bandages carefully. The burns were angry but clean. The stitches held. He cleaned the edges with the saline they’d sent home and taped fresh gauze in place. His fingers brushed the uninjured skin above Buck’s collarbone. Buck’s breath caught, once, and then steadied.

“Thanks,” Buck said, quiet.

Eddie nodded. He didn’t trust his voice.

They fell into a pattern that wasn’t quite recovery and wasn’t quite anything else. Eddie came over after his shifts. Sometimes Christopher came with him. Sometimes it was just the two of them, eating leftover takeout and watching whatever was on. Buck started sleeping better. The color came back to his face. Eddie kept finding reasons to stay past the point when he should have gone home.

One night the rain started hard against the windows. Buck was on the couch with his arm in a sling the doctor had finally ordered. Eddie was in the kitchen washing dishes because the sink was full and neither of them had said anything about it. When he came back, Buck was watching him.

“You can stop,” Buck said.

Eddie dried his hands on a towel. “Stop what?”

“Acting like you’re waiting for me to fall apart.”

Eddie sat on the other end of the couch. The space between them was small. “I’m not.”

“You are.” Buck’s mouth was set. “You’ve been here every night. You won’t let me drive. You flinch every time I reach for something with the wrong arm. I get it. I scared you. But I’m not—” He stopped. Looked away. “I’m not going anywhere.”

The rain filled the silence. Eddie studied the line of Buck’s jaw, the way the light from the lamp caught the new scar along his temple. He thought about the beam and the way Buck’s hand had locked around his wrist. He thought about all the other times Buck had put himself between Eddie and something that wanted to kill them both.

“I know,” Eddie said. His voice came out lower than he meant it to. “That’s the problem.”

Buck turned his head. The look on his face was careful and open at the same time, the way it got when he was trying not to hope too loud.

Eddie set the towel down. He reached across the space and set his hand, palm up, on the cushion between them. Buck looked at it for a long moment. Then he put his good hand in Eddie’s and held on.

They didn’t kiss that night. They sat with their fingers linked and the rain coming down and the city moving past outside, and Eddie felt the knot in his chest loosen by degrees. When Buck’s head tipped sideways and rested against Eddie’s shoulder, Eddie didn’t move him. He stayed where he was until the rain stopped and the apartment went quiet again.

In the morning Buck made coffee with both hands. The sling was on the counter. Eddie watched him move around the kitchen and felt the same shift he’d felt on the couch with Christopher, only clearer now. He crossed the room. Buck turned. Eddie put a hand on the side of Buck’s neck, careful of the healing skin, and kissed him once, slow and sure.

Buck kissed him back.

They stood in the kitchen with the coffee going cold and the light coming in through the window, and neither of them said anything about leaving.
