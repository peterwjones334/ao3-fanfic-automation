The apartment always smelled like wet concrete and instant ramen. Iwaizumi Hajime kicked the door shut with his heel, backpack sliding off one shoulder, and stopped when he saw the light under Oikawa’s door.

It was past two. Practice had ended at eleven. Oikawa should have been asleep or, more likely, still at the university gym arguing with the second-years about serve receive rotations. Instead the strip of light stayed steady, no shadow moving across it.

Iwaizumi set his bag down. The kitchen clock ticked. He filled a glass at the sink, drank half of it, and stared at the closed door. Three years of sharing a two-bedroom off campus had taught him the difference between Oikawa working and Oikawa spiraling. The light without movement was the second kind.

He knocked once. “Oi. You eating?”

No answer. He opened the door anyway.

Oikawa sat on the floor with his back against the bed frame, laptop closed beside him, a half-finished protein shake sweating onto the carpet. His left knee was wrapped in the same ice pack he’d used after the practice match against the engineering faculty. The pack had gone soft. He wasn’t looking at it. He was looking at his hands, flexing the fingers of the right one slowly, as if counting something only he could hear.

“Iwa-chan,” he said. The nickname came out flat. “Did you lock the front door?”

“Yeah.” Iwaizumi stepped in and closed the door behind him. The room was cold. Oikawa had the window cracked even in late October. “Your knee.”

“It’s fine.”

“It wasn’t fine at the end of the third set.”

Oikawa made a sound that might have been a laugh if it had any air behind it. “They scored on my serve. Three in a row. I could hear the second-years talking about it in the locker room. They think I’m washed.”

“They’re nineteen. They think everyone over twenty-two is washed.” Iwaizumi crouched. The ice pack was mostly water. He peeled it off, set it on the desk, and pressed two fingers carefully above the kneecap. Oikawa didn’t flinch, but his breath stopped for a second. “Swollen. You iced it too long.”

“I was thinking.”

“About what.”

Oikawa looked at him then. The usual brightness was gone; what was left was the careful, measuring expression he used on the court when a new spiker was testing him. “About next year. About whether I should keep playing or take the coaching internship in Sendai. About how every time I serve now I think about the one that went out in the quarterfinals last spring and how you didn’t say anything after, just stood there with your hands on your hips like you were waiting for me to stop being dramatic.”

Iwaizumi’s fingers stilled on the knee. “I was waiting for the trainer.”

“You could have said something.”

“You wouldn’t have heard it.”

The silence stretched. Outside, a scooter went past on the street below, engine whining. Oikawa’s hand rested on the carpet beside Iwaizumi’s knee, close enough that the knuckle brushed the hem of his shorts. Neither of them moved it.

“I keep thinking,” Oikawa said, quieter, “that if I stop, you’ll stop too. And then we’ll just be two guys who used to play volleyball and live together because the rent is cheaper. And one day you’ll find a girlfriend or a job in Tokyo and the light under my door won’t matter because you won’t be there to notice it.”

Iwaizumi sat back on his heels. The room felt smaller. He had known Oikawa since they were twelve. He had known the exact pitch of his laugh when he was truly happy and the exact way his shoulders dropped when he was pretending. He had not known this.

“I’m not going to Tokyo,” he said.

“You might.”

“I’m not.” He reached over and turned the laptop so the screen faced the wall. “And you’re not washed. You served thirty-eight points today. The ones that went out were because their libero is six-three and jumps like he’s on a trampoline. We adjust tomorrow.”

Oikawa’s mouth twitched. “We.”

“Yeah. We.” Iwaizumi stood, knees cracking. “Get on the bed. I’ll get a fresh pack and the anti-inflammatory. And then you’re going to eat the leftover rice or I’m calling your sister.”

“You wouldn’t.”

“Try me.”

Oikawa let himself be pulled up. His weight settled against Iwaizumi’s shoulder for half a second longer than necessary before he sat on the edge of the mattress. Iwaizumi went to the kitchen, ran the tap until the water was cold, filled a new bag, and came back. Oikawa had changed into the old Seijoh practice shirt that was too small across the chest now. The fabric pulled at the seams. Iwaizumi set the ice on the knee and sat on the floor again, back against the bed frame so they were side by side, shoulders almost touching.

“I don’t want a girlfriend,” Iwaizumi said to the opposite wall.

Oikawa was quiet for a long moment. “Okay.”

“I mean I don’t want one. Not right now. Not—” He stopped. The words felt clumsy in his mouth. He had spent years translating Oikawa’s moods into practical next steps: ice, stretch, eat, sleep, show up. He had never had to translate his own. “I notice the light. I always notice it. Even when I’m tired. Even when I want to sleep. So if you’re going to sit in here thinking about quitting, at least do it where I can hear you.”

Oikawa’s hand found his on the carpet. The grip was careful, like he was testing the strength of something that might give. Iwaizumi turned his palm up. Their fingers fit the way they always had when they high-fived after a point: solid, a little rough from calluses, familiar.

“I’m not quitting,” Oikawa said. “Not this year. Maybe not next year either. But I need you to stop standing there with your hands on your hips like you’re waiting for me to figure it out alone.”

“I can do that.”

“Can you?”

Iwaizumi looked at him. Oikawa’s eyes were red at the rims, but steady. The ice pack dripped once onto the sheet. Outside, the scooter had long since gone. The building was quiet except for the low hum of the refrigerator and the sound of both of them breathing.

“Yeah,” Iwaizumi said. He squeezed the hand in his once, then let go so he could adjust the ice. “Eat the rice. Then sleep. Tomorrow we work on the serve. I’ll block for you until you stop aiming at the antenna.”

Oikawa huffed, the sound almost a laugh this time. “Bossy.”

“Someone has to be.”

They stayed like that until the ice melted again. Iwaizumi made the rice. Oikawa ate it sitting cross-legged on the bed, knee propped on a pillow, talking about the second-year libero’s footwork and whether they should switch the practice match to Friday so the new first-years could watch. When the bowl was empty, he set it on the nightstand and looked at Iwaizumi, who was still on the floor with his back against the frame.

“You can stay,” Oikawa said. “If you want. The floor’s bad for your back.”

Iwaizumi thought about the empty bed in his own room, the textbook he still needed to finish for the sports management seminar, the way the light under this door had been a fixed point for three years. He stood, turned off the lamp, and sat on the edge of the mattress. The springs creaked. Oikawa shifted to make room, careful of the knee, until their shoulders touched again in the dark.

“Tomorrow,” Iwaizumi said.

“Tomorrow,” Oikawa agreed. His voice was already slowing. “Don’t let me sleep through the alarm.”

“I won’t.”

In the morning the light under the door was gone because the door was open and the apartment smelled like coffee instead of wet concrete. Iwaizumi found Oikawa at the kitchen table with both knees taped, scrolling through film on his laptop, already arguing with himself about the serve. He looked up when Iwaizumi came in, and the measuring expression from the night before had been replaced by something quieter and more certain.

“Iwa-chan,” he said. “You’re late. We have film to watch.”

Iwaizumi poured coffee into the second mug Oikawa had already set out. “I’m not late. You’re early.”

“Same thing.” Oikawa pushed the laptop toward him. On the screen, a freeze-frame of last night’s serve: ball high, arm extended, the second-year libero already moving. “Watch this. Tell me where I’m aiming wrong.”

Iwaizumi sat. Their knees bumped under the table. Neither of them moved away. Outside, the city was waking up. Inside, the light was ordinary morning light, and the door was open, and for the first time in a long while neither of them was waiting for the other to speak first.
