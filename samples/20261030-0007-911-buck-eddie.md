The fluorescent lights hummed above the hospital bed, the same low note they’d been singing for three days. Eddie Diaz watched the ceiling tiles and counted the dark flecks in the one directly over his head. Seventeen. He’d counted them twice already. His left shoulder ached under the sling, a deep, bone-deep throb that the painkillers only dulled at the edges. The morphine button sat untouched on the rail. He didn’t trust the way it made the room tilt.

Buck was in the chair again. Same chair, same pair of jeans, same coffee from the machine that tasted like burnt plastic. He’d changed shirts at some point—blue now instead of the gray he’d worn when they wheeled Eddie out of the ambulance—but the crease between his eyebrows hadn’t moved. He was reading something on his phone with the brightness turned low, one knee bouncing, the other foot hooked around the chair leg so it wouldn’t scrape.

“You don’t have to stay,” Eddie said. His voice came out rougher than he meant. “Shift ended hours ago.”

Buck didn’t look up. “Bobby said the paperwork’s still sitting on his desk. I’m not clocking out until he signs it.”

Eddie almost smiled. Bobby had signed the paperwork yesterday. “Liar.”

“Maybe.” Buck finally set the phone down. His eyes were red at the corners. “Christopher’s with Pepa. She says he’s fine. Drawing you a picture of the truck.”

“The one that fell on me.”

“Yeah. He gave it wheels that look like flowers. She sent a photo.” Buck turned the screen so Eddie could see. The truck was purple. The wheels were definitely flowers. Eddie’s throat closed around something that wasn’t pain.

They sat with that for a while. Outside, a nurse pushed a cart past the door. The wheels squeaked. Eddie shifted and the stitches in his side pulled. He breathed through it the way the physical therapist had shown him—in for four, out for six—and when he opened his eyes Buck was watching him again, that same careful attention he used on a call when someone was about to drop.

“You scared the shit out of me,” Buck said quietly.

Eddie closed his eyes. “I know.”

The building had come down in sections. They’d been clearing the second floor when the support beam above the stairwell let go. Eddie had shoved Buck toward the landing and taken the worst of the debris across his back and shoulder. He’d been conscious the whole way down, which he counted as a mixed blessing. He’d heard Buck’s voice the entire time—steady, too steady—calling for a second crew, for a board, for anyone who could hear him. Eddie had tried to answer. The dust in his mouth had turned the words into coughs.

Now Buck’s hand rested on the edge of the mattress, close enough that Eddie could feel the heat of it without touching. Neither of them moved to close the distance.

---

By the end of the week Eddie was home. The apartment smelled like the lemon cleaner Pepa used and the coffee Buck had started making in the mornings before he left for the station. Christopher had taped the purple truck drawing to the fridge. The flowers on the wheels were labeled in careful block letters: DAD’S TRUCK.

Buck cooked. Not the elaborate things he made when he was showing off—just scrambled eggs and toast, or the arroz con pollo recipe Eddie’s abuela had written out years ago and Buck had somehow memorized. He moved around the kitchen like he’d always lived there, which he almost had, except he still kept his keys in his pocket and his duffel by the door. Eddie watched him from the couch and tried not to count the times Buck’s hand brushed his when he passed the remote or the ice pack. The touches were accidental and then not. Buck would pause half a second longer than necessary, fingers warm against Eddie’s knuckles, before he pulled away and found something else to do with his hands.

On the second afternoon Hen stopped by with soup and a look that said she’d already heard the whole story from Bobby. She stayed long enough to check Eddie’s range of motion and to tell Buck, without looking at him, that the station was covering his shifts until Eddie could manage the stairs alone. Buck nodded like he hadn’t already known. After she left, Christopher asked if Aunt Hen was going to bring more soup next time. Buck said yes before Eddie could answer.

The first night Eddie woke from a dream he couldn’t remember, only the feeling of weight and the sound of concrete shifting. He lay still and listened to Christopher’s white-noise machine through the wall. After a minute he heard the soft creak of the couch in the living room. Buck’s voice, low: “You good?”

Eddie didn’t answer right away. His shoulder throbbed in time with his pulse. “Yeah. Just—noise in my head.”

Buck didn’t push. He stayed where he was. Eddie listened to him breathe until the noise faded and the room was only dark again.

On the fourth night the dream stuck. Eddie came out of it with his good hand fisted in the sheet and his breath coming short. The hallway light was on. Buck stood in the doorway in the T-shirt he slept in, hair flattened on one side, eyes wide and awake in a way that said he hadn’t been sleeping either.

“Can I—” Buck stopped. Started again. “You want company?”

Eddie nodded. His mouth was dry. Buck crossed the room and sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the shoulder. He didn’t touch him. He just sat, solid and warm, and after a while Eddie’s breathing matched his.

“I keep seeing it,” Eddie said. The words felt small. “The beam. You under it instead of me.”

Buck’s hand settled on the mattress between them, palm up. Eddie looked at it for a long moment before he set his own hand over it. Buck’s fingers closed, slow, like he was afraid of squeezing too hard.

“I’m right here,” Buck said.

They stayed like that until the sky outside the window turned gray.

---

Eddie started physical therapy on a Tuesday. Buck drove him. He waited in the lobby with a paperback he’d bought at the grocery store and didn’t pretend to read. When Eddie came out, arm trembling from the exercises, Buck stood and took the sling without being asked. He adjusted the straps the way the therapist had shown them, his fingers careful at the back of Eddie’s neck. The touch lingered. Eddie felt the calluses on Buck’s fingers and the way his breath caught, just once, before he stepped back.

In the car Eddie said, “You don’t have to keep doing this.”

Buck’s hands stayed on the wheel. “Doing what?”

“All of it. The cooking. The driving. Sleeping on the couch. You have your own place.”

“I like your couch.”

“Buck.”

The light changed. Buck pulled through the intersection and didn’t answer until they were parked in front of the building. He turned the engine off and looked at his hands.

“I keep thinking about what would’ve happened if you’d been two steps slower,” he said. “Or if I’d been the one who went up first. And I know that’s not useful. I know you’re here. But every time I leave the apartment I have to talk myself into it. So if I’m here, at least I don’t have to do that.”

Eddie studied the line of Buck’s jaw, the way his throat moved when he swallowed. “You can stay,” he said. “As long as you want. But not because you’re scared. Because you want to.”

Buck looked at him then. The parking lot was bright and ordinary. Somewhere a car alarm chirped and cut off.

“I want to,” Buck said. His voice was rough. “I’ve wanted to for longer than the building. Longer than I knew what to do with it.”

They sat with that until the air in the car felt too warm. Eddie reached over with his good hand and rested it on Buck’s wrist, just above the watch he always wore. Buck turned his palm up without looking down. Their fingers fit the way they had on the bed three nights before—slow, deliberate, like they were both learning the shape of permission.

---

Christopher noticed first. He came home from school one afternoon and found them on the couch, Eddie’s arm still in the sling, Buck’s shoulder under it so the weight didn’t pull. The remote was between them. Neither of them was watching the television.

“Are you guys dating now?” Christopher asked. He said it the way he said everything—curious, no judgment, already moving toward the kitchen for a snack.

Eddie felt Buck go still. “We’re—figuring it out,” Eddie said.

Christopher nodded like that made sense. “Okay. Can we have pizza?”

After he disappeared into the kitchen Buck exhaled a laugh that sounded more like relief. “Out of the mouths of babes.”

“He’s not wrong,” Eddie said. His voice was steadier than he expected. “I’ve been figuring it out for a while. I just didn’t know how to say it without making it harder.”

Buck turned his head. They were close enough that Eddie could see the faint scar on Buck’s eyebrow from the ladder truck years ago, the one Eddie had stitched in the field before the ambulance arrived. Buck’s eyes were very blue in the afternoon light.

“I’ve been figuring it out too,” Buck said. “Since before the building. Since before a lot of things.”

Eddie shifted until their knees touched. The movement pulled at his shoulder but he didn’t care. “Then we don’t have to figure it out alone.”

Buck’s hand found his again, easier this time. His thumb moved once across Eddie’s knuckles, a small, deliberate stroke. Then he leaned in, slow enough that Eddie could have stopped him, and pressed his mouth to the corner of Eddie’s. It was barely a kiss. It was enough. Eddie turned his head and met him properly—careful of the sling, careful of everything else they’d spent years not saying. Buck’s hand came up to cup the back of his neck, warm and steady.

When they broke apart Christopher’s voice carried from the kitchen: “Pepperoni or cheese?”

Buck laughed against Eddie’s mouth. “Both,” he called back. “We’re celebrating.”

Outside, the city kept moving. Inside, the refrigerator hummed and Christopher opened a cabinet and closed it again. Eddie leaned his head against Buck’s shoulder—the good one, the one that could take the weight—and closed his eyes. The ache in his own shoulder was still there. It would be there for weeks. But Buck’s heartbeat was steady under his ear, and for the first time in days the count in Eddie’s head stopped at the number of times Buck’s fingers tightened around his, and that was enough.
