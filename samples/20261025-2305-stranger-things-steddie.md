Steve’s shift ended at nine. The parking lot was half empty and the air smelled like wet asphalt and the fryer from the place next door. He was halfway to the Beemer when the light in the record store across the street flickered once and stayed on.

He knew the owner left at six. He also knew Eddie Munson had been closing most nights for the last month.

Steve stood with his keys in his hand and watched the light. After a minute he crossed the street.

The bell above the door was new and too loud. Eddie looked up from a stack of sleeves he was sorting, hair half out of its usual knot. He didn’t look surprised.

“Harrington.”

“Store’s closed.”

“Door wasn’t locked.” Eddie set a record down carefully. “You buying something or you just checking I didn’t get murdered by a customer who wanted the new Madonna?”

“Neither.” Steve’s shoulder ached where the demobat had gotten him last spring. He rolled it without thinking. “Saw the light.”

Eddie studied him for a second, then jerked his chin toward the back. “There’s a stool if you want to sit while I finish. Or you can leave. Both are free.”

Steve stayed.

They didn’t talk much. Eddie alphabetized. Steve sat and watched the street through the front window. When the last crate was done, Eddie locked the register and killed the main lights. Only the small one over the counter stayed on.

“Wayne’s working overnight,” Eddie said. “Place is quiet. You can hang if you want. Or don’t.”

Steve almost said he had to get home. Instead he said, “I’ve got beer in the trunk.”

They drank it on the loading dock behind the store, feet dangling off the edge. The night was cool enough that Steve kept his jacket on. Eddie didn’t. His arms were bare under the cutoff, the scars from the Upside Down still pink and raised. Steve tried not to look at them and failed.

“Does it still hurt?” he asked.

Eddie flexed his fingers. “Not really. Itches sometimes. You?”

“Same.”

They finished the six-pack in silence that wasn’t quite comfortable and wasn’t quite the opposite. When Steve stood to leave, Eddie walked him around to the front. The street was empty.

“See you,” Steve said.

“Yeah,” Eddie answered. “See you.”

He didn’t, not for three days. Then Dustin dragged him into the record store because he needed a copy of some band Steve had never heard of and refused to explain. Eddie was behind the counter, talking a kid through the difference between two pressings. He glanced up when the bell rang, saw Steve, and his mouth did something small before he looked back at the customer.

Steve waited by the metal section. When the kid left, Eddie came over without being asked.

“Henderson’s looking for something specific,” he said. “I’ve got it in the back if you want to tell him it’s twelve bucks.”

“Tell him yourself.”

“He’d argue with me. He won’t argue with you.”

Steve took the record and the message. On the way out he paused. “You closing tonight?”

“Yeah.”

“I’ll bring the beer.”

It became a thing. Not every night. Two or three times a week, after both of them were done, they sat on the dock or in the back room with the door cracked so the night air could come in. Sometimes they talked. Mostly they didn’t. Steve learned that Eddie preferred the cheap beer because the good stuff reminded him of parties he didn’t get invited to. Eddie learned that Steve’s shoulder still woke him up if he slept on it wrong.

One night it rained hard enough that the loading dock was useless. They stayed inside. Eddie put a record on low—something with too much guitar—and Steve stretched out on the floor with his back against a crate of returns. The scar tissue pulled. He shifted and hissed.

Eddie turned the volume down. “Bad one?”

“Just stiff.”

“Sit up.”

Steve did. Eddie crouched behind him, careful not to crowd. His hands hovered for a second.

“Can I?”

“Yeah.”

The first touch was light. Eddie worked his thumbs along the edge of the scar, not digging, just warming the muscle. Steve’s breath went out of him. He kept his eyes on the opposite wall. Eddie’s hands were callused from the guitar and warmer than Steve expected. When he hit a knot Steve made a sound he didn’t mean to make.

Eddie stopped. “Too much?”

“No. Keep going.”

He did. Neither of them spoke until Eddie’s hands stilled and stayed where they were, palms flat against Steve’s shoulder blades.

“You can come over,” Eddie said. His voice was quiet. “If the shoulder’s bad and Wayne’s not home. Couch is better than the floor.”

Steve nodded once. Eddie’s hands dropped away.

The next time the shoulder woke him, Steve drove over at two in the morning. The trailer was dark except for the porch light. Eddie opened the door before Steve knocked, like he’d been waiting. He didn’t ask questions. He pointed at the couch, tossed Steve a pillow, and went back to his room. Steve lay there listening to the rain on the roof and the low sound of Eddie’s breathing through the thin wall. He slept better than he had in weeks.

They didn’t talk about it the next day. Or the day after. But Steve started leaving his jacket on the back of the couch when he came over, and Eddie stopped asking if he wanted the light on.

One Thursday the power went out across half of Hawkins. The record store closed early. Steve found Eddie in the parking lot, sitting on the hood of his van with a flashlight and a cigarette he wasn’t smoking.

“You good?”

“Wayne’s stuck at the plant. Generator’s busted. Figured I’d wait it out here.”

Steve looked at the dark store, then at Eddie’s bare arms. “Come on.”

They drove to Steve’s. The house was empty; his parents were in Chicago again. Steve found candles in a drawer and lit three on the kitchen table. The light made everything soft. Eddie stood in the middle of the room like he wasn’t sure he was allowed to touch anything.

“You can sit,” Steve said.

Eddie sat. They ate cold leftover pizza and didn’t turn on the radio because the batteries were dead. At some point Eddie’s knee bumped Steve’s under the table and neither of them moved it.

“I used to think you hated me,” Eddie said. The candlelight caught the edge of his mouth. “Back in school.”

“I didn’t know you.”

“Yeah. That tracks.”

Steve turned his bottle in his hands. “I don’t hate you now.”

“I know.”

They sat with that. The candles burned lower. Eddie’s hand was on the table, close enough that Steve could see the old ink stains on his fingers. He reached out and covered it with his own. Eddie’s fingers twitched, then settled. They stayed like that until the last candle guttered.

In the morning Eddie was still on the couch, blanket pulled up to his chin, one arm dangling. Steve stood in the doorway with coffee he didn’t remember making and watched the rise and fall of his chest. When Eddie opened his eyes he didn’t startle.

“Hey,” he said, voice rough.

“Hey.”

Steve set the mug on the table. Eddie sat up, hair everywhere, and looked at him like he was waiting for the part where Steve took it back. Steve didn’t. He sat on the edge of the coffee table instead, close enough that their knees touched again.

“Shoulder’s better,” he said.

“Good.”

Eddie reached up and fixed a piece of Steve’s hair that had fallen forward. His hand stayed there, thumb resting against Steve’s temple. Steve leaned into it. The motion was small. Eddie’s breath caught.

“This okay?” Eddie asked.

“Yeah.”

They stayed like that until the coffee went cold. Outside, the power was still out and the street was quiet. Inside, Eddie’s hand was warm against Steve’s face and neither of them moved to break the contact. Steve closed his eyes. For the first time in a long time, the ache in his shoulder didn’t register at all.
