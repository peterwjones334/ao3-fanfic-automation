The apartment above the hardware store had always been Buck’s alone. One bedroom, a couch that sagged in the middle, and a kitchen table covered in half-finished crossword books. He liked it that way until the pipe burst two floors down and the landlord texted Eddie that the place wouldn’t be livable for at least a week.

Eddie stood in Buck’s doorway with Christopher’s duffel in one hand and a grocery bag in the other. Christopher leaned against his crutches, looking past Buck’s shoulder at the shelf of action figures.

“We’ll be quiet,” Eddie said.

“You don’t have to be.” Buck stepped aside. “Guest room’s the couch. I already put sheets on it.”

Christopher made it three steps inside before he stopped. “You have the red Spider-Man.”

“Yeah. You can take it down if you want.”

They settled in by dinner. Eddie cooked because Buck’s fridge held mostly protein bars and orange juice. The three of them ate at the small table, Christopher talking about a science project while Eddie corrected his grip on the fork without looking. Buck watched the way Eddie’s shoulders stayed tight even when he laughed at something Christopher said.

After Christopher was asleep on the couch, Eddie washed the plates. Buck dried.

“You don’t have to do that,” Eddie said.

“I know.”

The silence stretched. Water ran. Outside, a car alarm went off and stopped.

“Thanks for this,” Eddie said eventually. “I called my abuela. She would’ve taken us, but Christopher’s physical therapy is closer from here.”

“It’s fine. Really.”

Eddie nodded once and didn’t say anything else. Buck kept drying the same plate until the water cooled.

The second night, Buck woke to the sound of the couch creaking. He found Eddie in the kitchen with the light off, phone in his hand, staring at the screen without touching it.

“Couldn’t sleep?” Buck asked.

Eddie locked the phone and set it face-down. “Old habit. Checking the weather. Checking the route to school. Checking nothing.”

Buck opened the fridge, took out two beers, and set one on the counter between them. Eddie didn’t reach for it right away.

“I keep thinking about the last time the apartment flooded,” Eddie said. “Shannon was still here. We argued about the boxes. She wanted to throw half of them out. I wanted to keep everything.”

Buck leaned against the opposite counter. The beer was cold against his palm.

“You kept the boxes,” he said.

“Most of them. After she left I still didn’t open them.” Eddie finally picked up the bottle. “Christopher asked about her last week. Not crying. Just… asking if she liked rain.”

They drank. The refrigerator hummed. Buck wanted to say something about the way Eddie’s voice went flat when he talked about her, the way he still checked the windows at night like weather could take something else. He didn’t. He finished his beer and put the empty bottle in the recycling without making noise.

On the fourth day Christopher’s leg hurt after therapy. He sat on the floor with his back against the couch and didn’t want to talk. Eddie crouched beside him for ten minutes, then stood and walked into the kitchen like he needed something to do with his hands. Buck sat down where Eddie had been.

“You don’t have to make it better,” Christopher said, eyes on the carpet.

“Okay.”

They stayed there. Buck’s knee cracked when he shifted. Christopher’s breathing evened out. When Eddie came back with a glass of water he stopped in the doorway and didn’t speak. Buck looked up. Eddie’s face was careful, the same expression he used at calls when a parent was trying not to fall apart in front of their kid.

Later, after Christopher was asleep, Eddie stood at the sink again.

“He doesn’t let most people sit with him when it hurts,” Eddie said.

Buck rinsed a cup. “I didn’t do anything.”

“That’s the point.”

The week stretched. Eddie’s shirts appeared on the drying rack next to Buck’s. Christopher’s homework migrated from the coffee table to the kitchen counter and back. Buck started buying the cereal Christopher liked without being asked. Eddie fixed the loose hinge on the cabinet door while Buck was at the station, and when Buck came home the door closed without catching.

One afternoon Buck came off a twenty-four and found Eddie on the floor with a screwdriver, the hinge plate already off. Christopher sat at the table with a worksheet, pencil tapping.

“You didn’t have to,” Buck said.

Eddie didn’t look up. “It was catching every time someone went for a glass.”

Buck watched the way Eddie’s hands moved, steady and precise. There was a smear of grease on his wrist. Buck wanted to reach over and wipe it away. He didn’t. He took a shower instead and stood under the water until his skin went pink, thinking about the space Eddie took up in the apartment now and how it already felt like it belonged.

They didn’t talk about the way they moved around each other. Eddie left the bathroom light on at night because Buck still woke up sometimes and needed to see the hallway. Buck stopped leaving his keys on the table where Christopher might knock them off. When Eddie’s shoulder seized after a long shift, Buck stood behind him in the kitchen and pressed his thumbs into the muscle until Eddie’s head dropped forward and he exhaled through his teeth. Neither of them mentioned it the next morning. Buck’s hands smelled like Eddie’s soap for hours afterward.

On the fifth evening they ordered pizza because no one wanted to cook. Christopher fell asleep halfway through a movie with his head on Buck’s thigh. Eddie sat on the other end of the couch, one arm stretched along the back. His fingers brushed the hair at the nape of Buck’s neck once, light, then stayed there. Buck kept his eyes on the screen. His pulse was loud in his ears. When the credits rolled Eddie didn’t move his hand right away. Christopher shifted and mumbled something. Eddie’s fingers slid away. Buck felt the absence like a draft.

The next morning Eddie made coffee before Buck’s alarm. The mug was already on the counter, black the way Buck drank it. Eddie was packing Christopher’s lunch at the table, methodical, the same way he packed a med kit. Buck stood in the doorway in his sleep pants and watched the ordinary motion of it. Eddie looked up, caught him looking, and didn’t look away.

“You’re off today,” Eddie said.

“Yeah.”

“Christopher has a half day. If you want—” He stopped, adjusted the sandwich, started again. “We could go to the park. He likes the one with the ramps.”

Buck nodded. They went. Christopher raced the ramps on his crutches while Eddie timed him on his phone and Buck sat on the bench with his elbows on his knees, watching the way Eddie’s face changed when he laughed. The sun was bright. Eddie’s shoulder brushed Buck’s when he sat down. Neither of them shifted away.

On the sixth night the landlord called. The apartment would be ready tomorrow. Eddie stood in the living room with the phone still in his hand after he hung up. Christopher was already asleep. The red Spider-Man sat on the arm of the couch.

“I should pack,” Eddie said.

Buck nodded. He didn’t move from the kitchen doorway.

Eddie set the phone down. “You don’t have to keep doing this. Watching him. Making room.”

“I know.”

“Buck.”

Buck looked at the floor. The boards were scuffed where Christopher’s crutches had dragged. “I like it when you’re here.”

The words sat between them. Eddie’s hands were loose at his sides. He took one step, then another, until he was close enough that Buck could see the faint line of a scar at his hairline from an old call.

“I keep waiting for you to get tired of it,” Eddie said.

“I’m not.”

Eddie reached up and set his palm against the side of Buck’s neck. His thumb rested just under the jaw. Buck’s pulse jumped. Eddie’s eyes stayed on his face like he was checking for a flinch that didn’t come.

“We should pack in the morning,” Eddie said, but he didn’t step back.

Buck turned his head enough that his mouth brushed Eddie’s wrist. Eddie’s breath caught. They stayed like that, the apartment quiet around them, until Eddie’s hand slid up into Buck’s hair and Buck leaned in the rest of the way.

The kiss was slow. Eddie’s mouth was warm. Buck’s hands found the hem of Eddie’s shirt and stayed there, not pulling, just holding. When they broke apart Eddie’s forehead rested against Buck’s. His eyes were closed.

“Tomorrow,” Eddie said.

“Yeah.”

They packed in the morning. Christopher carried the Spider-Man to the car himself. Eddie loaded the last bag and closed the trunk. Buck stood on the sidewalk with his hands in his pockets.

Eddie walked back to him. Christopher was already buckled in, earbuds in, looking at his tablet.

“We’ll be two blocks over,” Eddie said.

“I know.”

Eddie glanced at the car, then at Buck. He leaned in and kissed him once, quick, where anyone could see. Buck’s fingers caught in Eddie’s sleeve and held on for a second longer than necessary.

“Dinner,” Eddie said. “Friday. Christopher wants tacos.”

“I’ll be there.”

Eddie nodded. He got in the car. Buck watched them pull away, then went upstairs and opened the cabinet Eddie had fixed. The door swung shut without a sound. He left it that way.
