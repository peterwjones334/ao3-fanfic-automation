The apartment smelled like antiseptic and the cheap detergent the agency used for spare clothes. Kirishima set the grocery bags on the counter and listened. No television. No music. Only the low hum of the fridge and the occasional creak of the building settling.

“Kacchan?” he called, then corrected himself. “Bakugou?”

A grunt came from the living room. Kirishima followed it. Bakugou was on the couch with one leg elevated on a pillow, the cast running from mid-thigh to ankle. The doctor had said six weeks if he didn’t push it. Bakugou had already tried to stand twice since the discharge papers were signed that morning.

“I got the stuff you asked for,” Kirishima said. “And some other things. Soup. Eggs. That spicy ramen you like.”

Bakugou didn’t look up from the phone in his hands. “Didn’t ask for ramen.”

“You always want the ramen when you’re hurt.” Kirishima started unpacking. The silence stretched. He kept his back to the couch while he put the milk away. “They said the agency would cover the place for a month. After that we can figure something else out.”

“We,” Bakugou repeated. Flat.

Kirishima closed the fridge. “Yeah. We. I’m not leaving you here to fall on your face the first time you try to get to the bathroom.”

Bakugou’s thumb stopped moving on the screen. For a second the only sound was the ice machine cycling in the freezer. Then he said, “Fine. But you sleep on the couch. And you don’t hover.”

“Deal.”

The first three days were mostly logistics. Kirishima learned the exact height Bakugou could lift his cast without the pain spiking. He learned that Bakugou would rather sit in the dark than ask for the lamp switched on. He learned that the pain meds made Bakugou quiet in a way that felt worse than the usual volume. On the fourth night Kirishima woke at two a.m. to the sound of the shower running. He stood in the hallway and counted to thirty before he knocked.

“You good?”

The water shut off. “Go back to sleep.”

Kirishima waited anyway. When the door opened Bakugou was in a towel and a pair of shorts, hair dripping, face pale under the bathroom light. He had one hand braced on the counter. The cast was dry. He must have balanced on the other leg the whole time.

“You could’ve called me,” Kirishima said.

“I can take a shower by myself.”

“You almost didn’t.”

Bakugou’s jaw worked. He didn’t argue. Kirishima stepped aside and let him pass, then went in and wiped the water off the floor so Bakugou wouldn’t slip later. He heard the couch springs when Bakugou sat down. Neither of them mentioned it in the morning.

On the sixth day the physical therapist came and went. Bakugou did the exercises without complaint and then spent the afternoon staring at the ceiling. Kirishima made rice and grilled fish and set the plate on the coffee table. Bakugou ate half of it. When he pushed the rest away Kirishima took it back to the kitchen without comment. He washed the dishes by hand because the dishwasher was loud. From the living room Bakugou said, “You don’t have to do that.”

“I know.”

“Then stop.”

Kirishima dried his hands. He walked back in and sat on the floor next to the couch so they were closer to the same height. Bakugou’s eyes were red at the edges. Not from crying. From not sleeping.

“I can handle the cast,” Bakugou said. “I can’t handle sitting here while everyone else is still out there.”

“The team’s fine. They checked in this morning. Kaminari said they cleared the last of the debris on the east side.”

Bakugou turned his face toward the wall. “I should be there.”

Kirishima rested his forearms on his knees. “You will be. In a few weeks. Right now you’re here.”

They stayed like that until the light changed. Kirishima got up eventually and turned on a lamp. Bakugou didn’t tell him to turn it off.

The second week the pain dropped enough that Bakugou started moving around the apartment on his own. He used the crutches the way he used everything else: hard and fast, like he could force the injury to keep up. Kirishima stopped offering help after the third time Bakugou snapped at him for it. Instead he started leaving things within reach. Water bottles on the low table. Clean shirts folded on the arm of the couch. A spare phone charger plugged in next to the outlet Bakugou preferred.

On a Tuesday afternoon Kirishima came back from a supply run and found Bakugou on the balcony. The crutches were propped against the railing. Bakugou had one hip against the metal and both hands gripping it, face tipped up into the wind. The city noise was louder out there. Sirens somewhere below. Construction two blocks over.

Kirishima stepped out and closed the door behind him so the cold air wouldn’t sweep through the whole apartment. He didn’t speak. Bakugou didn’t turn around.

“I keep thinking I hear the alarms,” Bakugou said after a minute. “Even when there aren’t any.”

Kirishima leaned on the railing a careful distance away. “Yeah. I get that too sometimes. After big ones.”

Bakugou’s knuckles were white. “Does it stop?”

“Not all the way. Gets quieter, though. If you let it.”

They stood there until Bakugou’s arms started to shake. Kirishima picked up the crutches and held them out. Bakugou took them without looking at him. Back inside, he sat on the edge of the couch and stared at his hands. Kirishima made tea because it was something to do with his hands. He set a mug in front of Bakugou and kept one for himself.

“You don’t have to stay the whole month,” Bakugou said. “I can manage.”

Kirishima blew on his tea. “I know you can.”

“Then why are you still here?”

The question sat between them. Kirishima watched the steam rise. He thought about the way Bakugou had looked on the balcony, like he was bracing for something that wasn’t coming. He thought about the first night after the battle, when they’d both been too tired to argue about who would take the last open bed in the temporary barracks, and Bakugou had fallen asleep with his shoulder against Kirishima’s arm and hadn’t moved until morning.

“Because I want to be,” Kirishima said.

Bakugou’s mouth tightened. He picked up the mug and held it with both hands. The cast made the motion awkward. He didn’t drink. “That’s a stupid reason.”

“Maybe.”

They finished the tea in silence. When Bakugou set the empty mug down he said, “You can take the bed tonight. I’ll stay out here.”

“The couch is fine.”

“I’m not asking.”

Kirishima nodded. He washed the mugs and left them in the rack. Before he went into the bedroom he stopped in the doorway. Bakugou was already stretched out with his eyes closed, one arm over his face to block the light from the street.

“Night,” Kirishima said.

Bakugou didn’t answer. Kirishima turned off the overhead and left the small lamp on. He slept better than he had in days.

The third week the cast came off. The doctor cleared Bakugou for light duty and told him to keep the crutches for another ten days. Bakugou left them by the door and walked with a limp he pretended wasn’t there. Kirishima watched him cross the living room twice before he said anything.

“You’re compensating on the left.”

“I know what I’m doing.”

“You’re going to mess up your hip if you keep it up.”

Bakugou stopped. He put one hand on the back of the couch and shifted his weight carefully. The muscle in his jaw jumped. “Then tell me what to do instead of standing there.”

Kirishima came over. He showed him the stretch the therapist had demonstrated, the one that opened the hip without loading the knee. Bakugou did it once, then again. On the third try his breath caught. Kirishima put a hand on his shoulder without thinking. Bakugou went still under it.

“That’s enough for now,” Kirishima said. He moved his hand away.

Bakugou stayed where he was for a few seconds longer. Then he straightened. “Thanks.”

It was the first time he’d said it without sounding like the word hurt him.

That night they ordered takeout because neither of them wanted to cook. They ate at the small table by the window. Bakugou picked the mushrooms out of his noodles and left them on the edge of Kirishima’s container without asking. Kirishima ate them. Outside, the streetlights came on one by one. The agency had texted that morning about a possible reassignment once Bakugou was cleared. Kirishima hadn’t opened the message.

“What are you going to do after this?” Bakugou asked.

Kirishima looked at the half-empty container. “Same thing I was doing before. Patrol. Training. Whatever they need.”

“You could request a different partner.”

“I don’t want a different partner.”

Bakugou set his chopsticks down. He studied the table like the answer might be written there. “Even if I’m slower for a while.”

“Even then.”

The silence this time felt different. Not empty. Full of the things they hadn’t said in the last three weeks. Bakugou reached across the table and touched the back of Kirishima’s wrist with two fingers. The contact was light. Testing. Kirishima turned his hand over so their palms met. Bakugou’s fingers were warm. He didn’t pull away.

They stayed like that until the food went cold. When they finally cleared the table Bakugou limped into the kitchen to throw the containers away. Kirishima followed and stood close enough that their shoulders brushed. Bakugou turned. The overhead light caught the scar on his cheek, the one from last year that had faded to a thin line.

“If you stay,” Bakugou said, “it’s not because you feel sorry for me.”

“I don’t.”

Bakugou nodded once. He leaned in and rested his forehead against Kirishima’s for a moment, the way he sometimes did after a long shift when the words wouldn’t come. Then he stepped back.

“Couch is still yours if you want it,” he said. “Or the bed. I don’t care.”

Kirishima smiled. Small. Real. “We can figure it out.”

They left the light on in the kitchen. In the morning the agency called about a joint patrol on the south side. Bakugou answered. He said they’d both be there. Kirishima listened from the bedroom doorway and didn’t correct him.

Outside, the city kept moving. Inside, the apartment was quiet except for the sound of two people getting ready for the day at the same time. Bakugou’s limp was still there. Kirishima matched his pace on the way to the door. Neither of them mentioned it.
