Shane arrives at the rink before the lights are fully on. The air inside is cold enough that his breath fogs when he exhales, and the smell of ice and rubber is already familiar after three days of the camp. He drops his bag on the bench and starts lacing his skates without looking up when the side door opens.

He knows the sound of those skates. The blades hit the concrete with a particular weight.

Ilya Rozanov steps onto the ice ten minutes later in a plain black jersey and no helmet. The kids are still in the locker room. For a moment the only noise is the scrape of Ilya’s edges as he does a slow lap, testing the surface the way he always does.

Shane stays by the boards. “You’re early.”

“Traffic was light.” Ilya’s voice carries. He stops a few meters away, hands on his hips. “You are still skating like you are afraid of the ice.”

Shane doesn’t rise to it. “The ice is fine.”

They have not been alone like this since the season ended. The last time they spoke was after a playoff game, in a hallway that smelled of sweat and cleaner, both of them too tired for anything but the usual short exchange. Shane had thought that was the end of it for the summer. Then the camp director called and said the second coach had cancelled, and would Shane mind if they asked someone else. He had said yes before he heard the name.

The kids come out in a clatter of sticks and laughter. Most of them are twelve or thirteen. A few recognize Ilya and go quiet for a second. Ilya grins at them, wide and easy, and the quiet breaks.

“All right,” Shane says. “Skate hard, listen, and keep your sticks down. Pair up.”

They run drills until lunch. Ilya corrects a boy’s hands on the stick without raising his voice. Shane watches from the other end and pretends he is only watching the drill. When they break, Ilya skates over and offers him a water bottle he has already opened. Their fingers touch. Neither of them pulls away immediately.

“You still tape your stick the same way,” Ilya says.

“It works.”

“It is ugly.”

Shane almost smiles. He takes the bottle. “Go eat.”

In the afternoon they split the group. Shane takes the forwards. Ilya takes the defense. From across the ice Shane can hear Ilya’s low instructions in English that is careful and still slightly accented. One of the smaller boys misses a pass and stands with his shoulders up. Ilya skates beside him, says something Shane cannot hear, and the boy’s shoulders drop. They try the drill again. The pass connects.

After practice Shane is the last one off the ice. He sits on the bench to untie his skates and finds Ilya already there, bag open, towel around his neck.

“You are good with them,” Shane says.

Ilya shrugs. “They listen when you do not yell.”

“You used to yell.”

“I still yell. Just not at children.” Ilya looks at him. The towel has left a damp line along his collarbone. “You look tired.”

“Long week.”

“It is only Wednesday.”

Shane stands. His knee clicks. “See you tomorrow.”

He walks out into the heat of the parking lot. His car is warm inside. He sits with the door open for a minute before he starts it, watching the rink doors. Ilya comes out a few minutes later, bag over one shoulder, and gets into a rental car without looking back.

Thursday is hotter. The rink’s air conditioning struggles. By the end of the morning session the kids are flushed and loud. Shane calls an early break and sends them outside with their water. He and Ilya stay on the ice to clear pucks.

Ilya skates close enough that Shane can hear his breathing. “You have not asked why I am here.”

“The director said you offered.”

“I did.” Ilya stops. “I was in Ottawa. I thought it would be quiet.”

Shane picks up a puck and flips it toward the net. It hits the post. “Is it?”

“No.”

They finish clearing the ice. In the break room the coffee is bad and the chairs are plastic. Ilya sits across from him with his legs stretched out. His knee rests against Shane’s under the table. Neither of them moves it.

“Your parents are still in the city,” Ilya says.

“They are.”

“You have not told them I am here.”

“There is nothing to tell.”

Ilya’s mouth tightens, then relaxes. “All right.”

Friday morning one of the goalies takes a shot to the mask and sits down hard. Shane is there first. The boy is crying but not hurt. Shane talks him through it until his breathing steadies. When he looks up, Ilya is watching from the blue line, stick resting on the ice, expression unreadable.

After the session the director asks them both to stay for a short meeting. The camp ends tomorrow. She wants feedback. Shane gives his. Ilya gives his, shorter. When she leaves, the office is quiet.

“You handled that well,” Ilya says.

“He was scared.”

“Yes.” Ilya leans against the desk. “I would have told him to get up.”

“He did get up.”

“After you talked.”

Shane looks at the floor. The linoleum is scuffed. “Why did you come?”

Ilya is quiet long enough that Shane thinks he will not answer. Then he says, “I wanted to see if it was still the same.”

“What?”

“Being near you.” Ilya’s voice is low. “It is not the same.”

Shane’s hands are cold. He puts them in his pockets. “We have practice in the morning.”

“I know.”

They leave separately. Shane drives home and sits in his kitchen with the lights off. His phone is on the table. He does not pick it up.

Saturday is the final day. The kids play a short game. Shane and Ilya coach opposite benches. Ilya’s team wins by one. Afterward the parents clap and the kids line up for photos. Shane stands at the edge of it, helmet under his arm, and watches Ilya crouch to talk to one of the smaller players. The boy laughs. Ilya stands and looks across the ice until he finds Shane. He nods once.

When the rink is empty they stay. The Zamboni has not come yet. The ice is marked with the last game’s lines.

Ilya skates out to center. Shane follows. They stop a stick’s length apart.

“I am flying tomorrow,” Ilya says.

“Boston?”

“Yes.”

Shane nods. His throat feels tight. “You were good with them.”

“So were you.”

Ilya reaches out and touches the side of Shane’s jersey, just above the number. His hand stays there. Shane does not step back.

“I kept thinking,” Ilya says, “that if I came and it was nothing, I could leave it. But it is not nothing.”

Shane looks at the hand on his jersey. “No.”

“I do not know what to do with that.”

“Neither do I.”

They stand on the ice until the cold starts to sink through Shane’s skates. Ilya’s thumb moves once, a small press against the fabric, then he lets go.

“Text me when you land,” Shane says.

Ilya’s mouth curves. It is not quite a smile. “You will answer?”

“Yes.”

They skate off together. In the parking lot Ilya stops beside Shane’s car. The sun is high. Heat rises off the asphalt.

“Shane.”

“Yeah.”

Ilya leans in and kisses him. It is brief, careful, the kind of kiss that does not ask for more than the moment. Shane kisses back. When they separate, Ilya’s hand is still on the car door.

“Tomorrow,” Ilya says.

“Tomorrow.”

Shane watches him drive away. He sits in the car with the windows down until the air inside is the same temperature as the air outside. Then he starts the engine and drives home with the radio off, the taste of the kiss still on his mouth, and the knowledge that he will answer when the text comes.

The summer is not over. The season will start again. For now there is only the road in front of him and the phone in his pocket, and the quiet certainty that something has shifted on the ice and will not shift back.
