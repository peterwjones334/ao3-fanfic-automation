The hotel corridor was quiet at two in the morning. Shane stood outside Ilya’s door with his shoulder taped and his head still ringing from the hit he’d taken in the third. He had told himself he was only checking that the ice bag had been delivered. He had told himself a lot of things.

He knocked once. The door opened before he could decide to leave.

Ilya was in a t-shirt and shorts, hair damp, one sock on. He looked at Shane’s shoulder, then at his face, and stepped back without speaking. Shane walked in. The room smelled like the same hotel soap they both used and the faint metal of the skate sharpening kit Ilya always traveled with.

“You should be asleep,” Ilya said.

“So should you.”

Ilya closed the door. The click was soft. He crossed to the mini-fridge, pulled out the ice bag that had indeed arrived, and held it out. Shane took it. Their fingers did not touch.

They sat on the edge of the bed because there was nowhere else. The television was off. The only light came from the bathroom. Shane pressed the ice to his shoulder and felt the cold bite through the tape. Ilya watched the process like he was checking the edges of a cut.

“They said it is not separated,” Shane said.

“I saw the replay.”

“Of course you did.”

Ilya’s mouth moved, almost a smile, then settled. He reached over and adjusted the angle of the bag so it covered the joint properly. His hand stayed for a second longer than necessary, palm warm against the ice. Shane did not look at him.

“You always check the film,” Shane said.

“You always get hit when you try to go through the middle.”

“It was open.”

“It was not.”

They had this argument every season. Shane had started keeping a private tally of how often Ilya was right. The number was higher than he wanted to admit. He shifted the ice. The movement pulled at the muscle and he made a small sound he did not mean to make.

Ilya stood, went to his bag, and came back with a small bottle of the anti-inflammatory gel the team trainers used. He set it on the bed between them.

“I do not need that,” Shane said.

“You are holding your arm like it is broken.”

“It is not.”

Ilya uncapped the bottle anyway. He waited. Shane looked at the gel, then at Ilya’s hands, then at the carpet. After a moment he turned so his back was more available and pushed the sleeve of his t-shirt up. The tape was already there; the gel would go around it. Ilya’s fingers were careful. He worked the gel in with short, even strokes, avoiding the worst of the bruise. Shane felt each press as a separate point of contact. He kept his breathing even.

“You are quiet,” Ilya said.

“I’m thinking.”

“About the game.”

“About the next one.”

Ilya’s thumb circled once more, then stopped. He capped the bottle and set it aside. Shane stayed turned away for another few seconds before he faced him again. The ice bag had slipped. Ilya put it back without being asked.

They had shared hotel rooms on the road when the league still paired captains for media days. Shane remembered the first year, how Ilya had slept with the lights on and how Shane had pretended not to notice. He remembered later years when they had stopped pretending about most things except the one that mattered.

“I watched you in the penalty kill,” Shane said. “You were limping on the left side.”

“Old.”

“You are not.”

“I am twenty-seven.”

“You are twenty-six until next month.”

Ilya looked at him then, properly. The bathroom light caught the edge of his cheek and the small scar near his eyebrow from a high stick three seasons ago. Shane had been on the ice for that one too. He had skated over after the whistle and stood there until the trainers waved him off.

“You remember the date,” Ilya said.

“I remember a lot of things.”

The air between them changed the way it sometimes did in the locker room after everyone else had gone, when the only sound left was the hum of the lights. Shane set the ice bag on the nightstand. His shoulder throbbed in the sudden warmth. He flexed his fingers to check the range and stopped when Ilya’s gaze followed the motion.

“I am not going to the optional skate tomorrow,” Shane said.

“Good.”

“You will.”

“Yes.”

“Even with the limp.”

Ilya shrugged one shoulder. The movement was smaller than usual. Shane noticed and filed it away with everything else he noticed and did not say.

“Stay,” Ilya said. The word was quiet. “For a while. The ice needs to stay on.”

Shane nodded. He did not trust his voice for a moment. He lay back against the pillows on the far side of the bed, careful of his arm. Ilya turned off the bathroom light and the room went dark except for the thin line under the door from the hallway. Shane heard the rustle of the second pillow, the shift of weight as Ilya settled on his own side.

They did not touch. The space between them was the width of one of Ilya’s hands if he reached out. Shane listened to the sound of both of them breathing and counted the seconds until the ice bag would need replacing. Outside, a car passed on the street below. Somewhere down the hall a door opened and closed.

“I keep thinking about the play in the second,” Shane said into the dark. “The one where you dropped it back to me.”

“It was the right read.”

“It was the only time all night we were on the same page.”

Ilya was quiet long enough that Shane thought he might have fallen asleep. Then he said, “We are on the same page more than you think.”

Shane turned his head. He could just make out the shape of Ilya’s profile against the darker wall. “Are we?”

“Yes.”

The word sat there. Shane felt the old habit of deflection rise and set it aside. His shoulder ached in a steady, manageable way. The gel had left a faint medicinal smell on his skin.

“I almost called you last week,” he said. “After the game in Boston.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“Because I did not know what I would say when you answered.”

Ilya shifted. The mattress dipped. Shane felt the movement more than he heard it. A hand found his wrist in the dark, fingers light, checking the pulse the way trainers sometimes did after a hit. Ilya’s thumb rested over the vein. Shane did not pull away.

“You can say it now,” Ilya said.

Shane closed his eyes. The dark made it easier and harder at the same time. He thought of every road trip, every shared meal that had turned into three hours of talking about systems and line changes and nothing else, every time he had watched Ilya skate off the ice and felt the pull to follow and had not.

“I do not want this to stay the way it is,” he said. “The almost. The checking on each other and then going back to our own rooms.”

Ilya’s fingers tightened once, then loosened. He did not let go.

“I have been waiting for you to say that,” Ilya said. “I did not know how long it would take.”

“Long enough.”

“Yes.”

They lay still. Shane’s wrist stayed in Ilya’s hand. The ice bag on the nightstand had begun to melt; a thin line of water crept toward the edge. Shane heard it and did not move. Outside, the city continued without them. Inside the room the only sound was the slow, even pull of their breathing, no longer quite separate.

In the morning the optional skate would happen. One of them would go. The other would stay and ice the shoulder and answer the phone when it rang. For now the space between them on the bed had narrowed by the width of one careful hand, and neither of them moved to widen it again.
