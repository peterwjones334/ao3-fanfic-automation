The ice was already gone from the rink when Shane arrived. Early June, Montreal still carrying the last of the cold in its river wind, but the Voyageurs’ practice facility sat empty except for the janitorial staff and the distant hum of the Zamboni being serviced. Shane had come anyway. Habit. Or something quieter he didn’t name.

Ilya’s text had been three words, sent at 2:14 a.m.: *Knee is shit.*

Shane had stared at it until the screen dimmed. They didn’t do this. Not in the off-season. Not when there were no games to hide behind, no hotel rooms three blocks from the arena, no excuse that could be waved off as rivalry or adrenaline. He had booked the flight before sunrise.

Boston was worse. The air tasted like rain that couldn’t decide whether to fall. Ilya’s building was a converted warehouse on the edge of the Seaport, all brick and tall windows, the kind of place that looked better in photographs than it felt in person. Shane pressed the buzzer twice. No answer. He tried the code Ilya had given him once, years ago, for a night that had ended with both of them half-dressed and neither of them speaking. It still worked.

The loft smelled like menthol and stale coffee. Ilya was on the couch with his left leg propped on a pillow, a bag of frozen peas leaking condensation onto the fabric. He didn’t look up when Shane closed the door.

“You didn’t have to come,” Ilya said. His accent was thicker when he was tired. Or in pain. Shane couldn’t always tell the difference.

“You texted.”

“I text a lot of people.”

Shane set his bag down. The loft was the same as the last time he’d been here—bare walls, one good chair, a television too large for the room. A pair of crutches leaned against the kitchen island. He crossed the space and crouched beside the couch so he was eye-level with Ilya’s knee. The swelling was visible even through the sweatpants. Ilya’s hand rested on his thigh, fingers tight.

“What did the doctor say?”

“Rest. Ice. Don’t skate for three weeks. Maybe four.” Ilya finally looked at him. His eyes were bloodshot. “It is not serious. Just annoying.”

Shane reached out without thinking and set two fingers against the outside of Ilya’s knee, careful. The muscle twitched. “You played the last three games of the season on it.”

“We made the playoffs.”

“Barely. And you could have—”

“I know what I could have done.” Ilya’s voice dropped. “You are not my captain here.”

Shane pulled his hand back. The distance between them felt wider than the room. He stood, found the kitchen, and started the coffee machine because it was something to do with his hands. The grinder was loud. When it stopped, Ilya spoke again.

“Why did you come?”

Shane watched the coffee drip. “You asked.”

“I didn’t ask. I said the knee was shit.”

“Same thing.”

Ilya huffed something that might have been a laugh. It died quickly. Shane poured two mugs and carried them over. He set one on the floor within reach. Ilya didn’t take it.

They sat in the quiet for a long time. Rain started against the windows, soft at first, then steadier. Shane’s phone buzzed once—his mother, checking that he’d landed. He silenced it. Ilya’s phone was face-down on the cushion beside him, silent.

“You could have called Hayden,” Shane said eventually.

“Hayden would have told Jackie. Jackie would have told everyone. Then there would be questions.”

“About your knee?”

“About why Shane Hollander flew to Boston in June to sit on my couch.”

Shane’s stomach tightened. He had known the risk when he booked the ticket. He had booked it anyway. “I can leave.”

“You just got here.”

“Ilya.”

Ilya turned his head. The movement looked like it cost him something. “Stay. For now. The knee is bad at night. I don’t sleep.”

Shane stayed.

He slept on the couch the first night because the bed felt like a line neither of them was ready to cross. Ilya took the bedroom. Shane heard him moving—limping to the bathroom, the fridge, the window. At three he got up, found Ilya standing at the glass with his weight on his good leg, watching the streetlights smear in the rain.

“Pain?” Shane asked.

“Thinking.”

Shane came closer. Close enough that their shoulders almost touched. Ilya didn’t move away.

“My mother called yesterday,” Ilya said. “She wants me home in August. She has someone she wants me to meet. A girl. Her friend’s daughter. Nice. Quiet. From a good family.”

Shane didn’t answer. The rain filled the space.

“I told her I was busy with rehab.”

“Are you?”

“No.” Ilya’s breath fogged the glass. “I am busy with this. With not knowing what to do when the season ends and there is no game to play and no one is watching us hate each other.”

Shane’s hand found the edge of the window frame. His fingers were cold. “We don’t have to hate each other when no one is watching.”

“That is the problem.”

They didn’t touch. They stood until the sky lightened and Ilya’s knee forced him back to the couch. Shane helped him down, careful with the pillow, careful not to let his hands linger. Ilya closed his eyes. Shane stayed standing for another minute, then went back to his own side of the room and tried to sleep.

The second day was worse. Ilya’s knee locked when he tried to stand. He cursed in Russian, low and sharp. Shane got the crutches and stayed close while Ilya made it to the bathroom. When he came out, his face was pale. Shane made eggs because it was the only thing in the fridge that looked edible. They ate at the counter. Ilya’s appetite was small. Shane’s was worse.

“I should go,” Shane said when the dishes were done.

Ilya looked at the crutches. “If you go, I will try to walk without them and make it worse.”

“That’s blackmail.”

“It is practical.”

Shane stayed.

By the third evening the rain had stopped. The loft felt smaller. Shane had run out of things to do with his hands—laundry, groceries, answering emails that didn’t matter. Ilya had been quiet most of the day, reading something on his phone with his leg elevated. When Shane came back from the corner store with ice packs and ginger ale, Ilya was watching him.

“You are good at this,” Ilya said.

“At what?”

“Taking care of people who don’t ask for it.”

Shane set the bags down. “You asked.”

“I said the knee was shit.”

“Same thing,” Shane said again. It felt thinner this time.

Ilya shifted. The movement made him wince. Shane crossed the room and adjusted the pillow without being asked. His hand stayed on Ilya’s calf for a second longer than necessary. The muscle was warm under his palm. Ilya didn’t pull away.

“If someone saw you here,” Ilya said, voice low, “it would be bad.”

“I know.”

“Worse for you. You have the clean reputation. The parents who come to every game. The foundation.”

Shane’s throat felt tight. “Worse for you too.”

“I am already the Russian who fights. They expect me to be difficult.” Ilya looked at Shane’s hand on his leg. “They do not expect this.”

Shane lifted his hand. The absence of contact felt louder than the touch had. “What is this?”

Ilya didn’t answer right away. He reached for the ginger ale, opened it, set it down without drinking. “I think about you when I am not supposed to. On the ice. After games. When I am home and the apartment is empty and I know you are three hours away doing the same thing.”

Shane sat on the edge of the coffee table so their knees were almost touching. The bad one stayed elevated. The good one was close enough that Shane could feel the heat of it.

“I do the same,” he said. The words were careful. “I have for a long time.”

Ilya’s eyes closed. When he opened them, something in his face had shifted. Not softer. More exposed. “Then why do we keep pretending the only thing we are is rivals?”

“Because it’s safer.”

“Is it?”

Shane didn’t have an answer. He reached out again, slower this time, and set his hand on Ilya’s good knee. Ilya’s fingers covered his. The grip was tight. Not demanding. Just holding on.

They stayed like that while the light left the room. No one spoke. Outside, the street was quiet. Inside, the only sound was the low hum of the refrigerator and the occasional shift of Ilya’s breath when the pain moved.

Later, Shane helped him to the bedroom. He didn’t stay. He went back to the couch and lay awake listening to the building settle. At some point he heard Ilya’s phone vibrate. Then silence. Then Ilya’s voice, low, speaking Russian. Shane didn’t understand the words. He understood the tone. Careful. Distant. The way someone sounds when they are lying to a person they love.

In the morning Ilya was already up, crutches under his arms, making coffee. His face was drawn. Shane took the mug he offered and didn’t ask about the call.

“My mother again,” Ilya said anyway. “She wants a date. I told her I would think about it.”

Shane nodded. The coffee was too strong. He drank it.

“I don’t want to,” Ilya said. “Think about it. Or go. Or meet anyone.”

“Then don’t.”

“It is not that simple.”

“I know.”

They stood in the kitchen with the morning light cutting across the floor between them. Ilya shifted his weight. The crutches creaked.

“If I told her no,” he said, “she would ask why. She would keep asking. Eventually she would look at me the way she looks at the news when they talk about players who are—” He stopped. The word sat between them, unsaid.

Shane set his mug down. “You don’t have to tell her anything. Not yet.”

“And later?”

“Later we figure it out.” Shane stepped closer. Close enough that Ilya had to tilt his head to keep looking at him. “Or we don’t. But I’m here now. The knee is still shit. You still don’t sleep. Those things are true whether anyone else knows or not.”

Ilya’s mouth twitched. Almost a smile. It didn’t reach his eyes, but it was something. “You are very stubborn.”

“You texted.”

“I know.”

Shane reached up and brushed his thumb along the edge of Ilya’s jaw. The stubble was rough. Ilya leaned into the touch like it cost him nothing and everything at once. They stood there until the coffee went cold and the light moved across the floor and the only thing left between them was the quiet decision to stay in the same room a little longer.

By the end of the week the swelling had gone down enough that Ilya could walk short distances without the crutches. Shane still stayed. They didn’t talk about what came next. They ate. They watched bad television. They stood at the window when the rain came back. On the last night, Ilya slept in the bed and Shane slept beside him, careful of the knee, careful of the space that still existed between their bodies even when their hands found each other under the sheet.

In the morning Shane’s flight was at noon. He packed while Ilya made eggs. At the door, Ilya caught his wrist.

“Text me,” he said. “When you land. And after. Not just when something is wrong.”

Shane nodded. “You too.”

Ilya’s grip tightened, then released. “The knee is better.”

“I know.”

“Still shit, though.”

Shane almost smiled. “Same thing.”

He left. The hallway was bright. The elevator was slow. Outside, Boston smelled like wet pavement and the river. Shane walked to the car he’d called and didn’t look back until he was inside. When he did, Ilya was at the window three floors up, one hand braced on the frame, watching.

Shane lifted a hand. Ilya lifted his in return.

The car pulled away. Shane’s phone stayed in his pocket until he was through security. Then he took it out and typed, *Landed. Knee?*

The reply came before the plane pushed back.

*Still shit. Come back when you can.*

Shane read it twice. Then he put the phone away and closed his eyes as the engines started, already counting the weeks until the next time the ice would bring them into the same building and the rest of the world would only see the rivalry they had spent years perfecting.

Under the noise, something quieter held.
