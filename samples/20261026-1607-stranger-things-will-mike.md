The basement still smelled like old carpet and the detergent Mrs. Wheeler used on the blankets. Will sat on the edge of the couch with his hands between his knees, watching Mike tape a new poster over the dent in the drywall. The poster was for a band neither of them had heard of; Mike had bought it because the colors were the same as the ones Will had used in his last painting.

“You don’t have to stay,” Will said. His voice came out thinner than he wanted. “The others are upstairs.”

Mike smoothed the last corner with the flat of his hand. “I know.” He stepped back, studied the poster, then sat on the opposite end of the couch. The space between them was the width of a throw pillow. “Dustin’s explaining some radio thing to Lucas. They’ll be at it for a while.”

Will nodded. Outside, the late-October light was already gone. The only sound was the low hum of the furnace and the occasional creak of the house settling. He flexed his fingers. The left ones still ached when the temperature dropped; the doctors had said the cold would do that for a while. He didn’t mention it. Mike already watched his hands too carefully.

They had been careful with each other since the hospital. Mike brought him sketchbooks and stayed while he filled them. Will let him. Neither of them said what they were doing, only that it was easier when the other was in the room.

“Your mom made soup,” Mike said after a minute. “Chicken. The kind with the little stars.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“You said that yesterday.”

Will looked at the poster. The band’s logo was a jagged line that almost looked like a scar. “I ate breakfast.”

Mike didn’t argue. He reached for the remote, turned the television on, and left the volume low. A nature documentary played without sound. Will watched a bird of prey turn in a slow circle over a field. His shoulders loosened a fraction.

They sat like that until the documentary ended and another began. Will’s head tipped sideways until it rested against the back of the couch. He didn’t mean to close his eyes. When he opened them again the room was darker and Mike was still there, one arm stretched along the back of the couch so that his fingers nearly brushed Will’s shoulder.

“You fell asleep,” Mike said. He didn’t move his arm.

“Sorry.”

“Don’t be.” Mike’s thumb tapped once against the fabric. “You looked less—I don’t know. Less like you were waiting for something to happen.”

Will sat up slowly. The ache in his fingers had settled into a dull pull. He rubbed his left palm with his right thumb. Mike’s eyes followed the motion and then moved away.

“I keep thinking it’ll come back,” Will said. He kept his voice even. “Not the whole thing. Just the part where I can’t tell if what I’m feeling is mine.”

Mike was quiet long enough that Will thought he wouldn’t answer. Then he said, “When you were in the hospital, the nurse asked me what your favorite color was. I told her blue. Then I thought maybe I was wrong and it was green, or the color of that jacket you used to wear. I sat there for twenty minutes trying to remember if you’d ever actually said it out loud.”

Will turned his head. Mike was looking at the television, but his jaw was tight.

“It’s blue,” Will said. “The dark kind. Like the sky right before it gets black.”

Mike’s mouth moved, almost a smile. “Okay.”

They watched the rest of the second documentary. When the credits rolled, Will stood and stretched. His left side pulled. He ignored it. Mike stood too, and for a second they were close enough that Will could see the place where Mike had bitten the inside of his lip.

“I’m going to get water,” Will said.

“I’ll come with you.”

In the kitchen the overhead light was bright. Will filled a glass and drank half of it. Mike leaned against the counter with his arms crossed. Neither of them turned the light off.

“You can stay tonight,” Will said. “If you want. The couch is fine.”

Mike looked at him. “Your mom already said I could.”

“I know. I’m saying it too.”

Mike nodded. He uncrossed his arms. One hand rested on the counter between them, close enough that Will could have reached for it if he moved his own. He didn’t. He finished the water and set the glass in the sink.

They went back downstairs. Mike turned the television off and folded the blanket Will had been using. Will sat on the floor with his back against the couch and pulled a sketchbook into his lap. He opened it to a blank page and held the pencil without drawing.

Mike sat on the floor beside him. Their knees touched. Neither of them moved away.

“I don’t know how to do this,” Mike said. The words were quiet. “The part where I stop waiting for you to tell me you’re fine and start believing it when you say you’re not.”

Will turned the pencil between his fingers. “I’m not fine.”

“I know.”

“I’m better when you’re here.” Will kept his eyes on the page. “That’s not the same thing.”

Mike’s knee pressed a little harder against his. “It’s something.”

Will drew a single line, then another. The lines didn’t make a shape yet. Mike watched them appear. After a while he reached over and took the pencil. Will let him. Mike drew a small circle at the end of one of the lines and handed the pencil back.

“Star,” he said.

Will added two more lines. “Constellation.”

They worked on the page until the furnace kicked on again. The sound covered the small noise Will made when his hand cramped. Mike set the pencil down and closed his fingers around Will’s wrist, gentle, just above the place that hurt. He held it until the cramp eased. Will didn’t pull away.

When Mike let go, his hand stayed on the floor between them, palm up. Will looked at it. Then he turned his own hand over and set it, lightly, against Mike’s. Their fingers didn’t lace. They just rested there, warm and still.

Upstairs, someone laughed. The sound traveled down the stairs and faded. Mike’s thumb moved once across Will’s knuckles and stopped.

“We don’t have to figure the rest out tonight,” Mike said.

“I know.” Will’s voice was steady. “But we can start.”

Mike’s fingers curled, just enough to hold. Will curled his back. Outside, the wind moved through the bare trees. Inside, the basement stayed warm, and the space between them stayed closed.
