The rain had stopped just after midnight, leaving the parking lot of the Hawkins Community Center slick and black under the sodium lights. Steve Harrington sat on the tailgate of his truck with a half-empty thermos of coffee between his knees, watching the side door. Inside, the Thursday night adult swim class was letting out. He could hear the low thump of music and the occasional splash of someone still reluctant to leave the warm water.

Eddie Munson came out last, as he always did. His hair was wet and tied back in a loose knot, a towel slung over one shoulder, flannel shirt unbuttoned over a tank top. He paused under the overhang to light a cigarette, the flame briefly illuminating the scar that cut through his left eyebrow. When he spotted Steve, he didn’t smile, but the corner of his mouth did something that was close enough.

“You again,” Eddie said, walking over. His boots left wet prints on the asphalt.

“Me again.” Steve held out the thermos. “Coffee’s still warm.”

Eddie took it without comment, drank, and handed it back. They did this most Thursdays now. Steve had started coming three months ago after a late shift at the video store left him restless and the apartment felt too quiet. He’d told himself he was just checking on the community center’s new security lights—Robin’s idea, something about grants and public safety—but the lights had been working fine for weeks and he was still here.

“Class go okay?” Steve asked.

“Old lady Henderson almost drowned me with her freestyle. I think she does it on purpose.” Eddie exhaled smoke toward the street. “You working tomorrow?”

“Opening. You?”

“Interview at the garage on Cherry. Uncle Wayne knows the owner.” Eddie’s voice stayed even, but his fingers tapped once against the thermos when Steve offered it again. “If it pans out, I might actually make rent without selling plasma.”

Steve nodded. He knew better than to say anything that sounded like encouragement. Eddie hated that. They sat in the quiet for a while, the sound of water dripping from the building’s gutters filling the space between them.

A car pulled into the lot too fast, tires hissing. It stopped at an angle across two spaces, headlights still on. Two guys got out—early twenties, jackets with faded high school patches. One of them spotted Eddie and stopped.

“Well, shit,” the taller one said. “Munson. Thought you crawled back under whatever rock they found you under.”

Eddie didn’t move. Steve felt the shift in him anyway, the way his shoulders settled lower, the cigarette held a little tighter between two fingers.

“Evening, gentlemen,” Eddie said. “Parking lot’s full. Try the street.”

The shorter one laughed. “You still doing the freak act? Thought the demogorgon shit would’ve cured you of that.”

Steve stood. The tailgate creaked. He didn’t step forward, just put himself between Eddie and the open lot, hands in his jacket pockets. The two guys looked at him, then at each other. The taller one’s mouth twisted.

“Harrington. Figures. You two running a club now?”

“Just waiting for the rain to stop,” Steve said. His voice was the same one he used when customers tried to return opened tapes. “You should too. Roads are slick.”

They stared a moment longer. Then the taller one spat on the ground, not quite at their feet, and jerked his chin at his friend. They got back in the car. The engine revved once before they pulled out, taillights disappearing around the corner.

Eddie was quiet for a long time after. He finished the cigarette and ground it out under his boot.

“You didn’t have to do that,” he said.

“I know.”

“They won’t forget it.”

“They never do.” Steve sat back down. The metal was cold through his jeans. “You okay?”

Eddie looked at him then, really looked, the way he sometimes did when he thought Steve wasn’t paying attention. The scar on his eyebrow caught the light. “Yeah,” he said. “I’m okay.”

They stayed until the thermos was empty and the last of the class lights went dark inside. Eddie walked Steve to his truck like he always did, even though it was only ten feet away. At the driver’s door he stopped, one hand on the frame.

“Thursday,” he said.

“Thursday,” Steve answered.

Eddie nodded once and headed for his own van, the one that still had a dent in the sliding door from last winter. Steve watched until the taillights turned onto the main road, then started the engine and drove home with the radio off.

---

The interview went well. Eddie called on Friday from the payphone outside the garage, voice low so the guys in the bay wouldn’t hear. They wanted him to start Monday. Wayne had already heard and left a message on Steve’s answering machine that was mostly static and the sound of a beer opening.

Steve replayed it twice before he went to work.

On Saturday he drove out to the trailer park with a six-pack and a bag of the cheap pretzels Eddie liked. Wayne answered the door in a flannel that had seen better decades and waved him in without ceremony. Eddie was at the kitchen table with a stack of paperwork and a calculator that kept beeping at him.

“Harrington,” Wayne said. “You stay for dinner, you’re peeling potatoes.”

“I can peel potatoes,” Steve said.

Eddie glanced up. There was grease under his fingernails already, even though he hadn’t started yet. “You don’t have to—”

“I know,” Steve said, and took the peeler Wayne handed him.

They worked in the small kitchen while the afternoon light moved across the linoleum. Wayne talked about the garage owner, a man named Ruiz who’d lost a son in the same year the gates opened and didn’t ask questions about the years after. Eddie mostly listened, correcting the numbers on the forms when Wayne got them wrong. When the potatoes were done and the beer was half gone, Wayne stepped outside to smoke and left the door cracked.

Eddie set the calculator aside. “You keep showing up,” he said.

“Yeah.”

“People notice.”

“Let them.” Steve rinsed his hands at the sink. The water ran cold. “You want me to stop?”

Eddie was quiet long enough that Steve turned around. He was looking at the table, thumb moving over a crease in one of the forms.

“No,” he said finally. “I don’t.”

Outside, Wayne’s lighter clicked. Inside, the refrigerator hummed. Steve dried his hands on a dish towel that smelled like laundry soap and old coffee and didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to.

---

Monday came and went. Eddie started at the garage. Steve opened the video store and spent most of the shift rewinding tapes that customers had left half-finished. At four-thirty Robin stuck her head in the back room and told him he looked like he was waiting for a phone call that wasn’t coming.

“I’m not,” he said.

“Sure.” She tossed him a bag of gummy worms. “Eddie’s uncle called the store earlier. Said if you were free after close, there was leftover chili and Eddie hadn’t eaten since breakfast.”

Steve closed at six. He was at the trailer by six-forty.

Eddie answered the door in a work shirt with the sleeves rolled up, hair still damp from a shower. There was a bandage on his left forearm, neat and white.

“It’s nothing,” he said before Steve could ask. “Caught it on a manifold. Ruiz made me go to the clinic. Four stitches. I’m fine.”

“Chili’s on the stove,” Wayne called from the living room. The television was on low. “Don’t let him tell you he’s fine if he’s not.”

They ate at the table. Eddie kept his arm carefully clear of the bowl. Steve watched the way he held the spoon, the slight hitch when he reached for the salt, and didn’t comment. Afterward Wayne took his plate to the sink and stayed there, running water longer than necessary.

Eddie walked Steve out to the truck again. The night was clear, colder than it had been. At the door Eddie stopped, the same way he always did, one hand on the frame.

“You don’t have to keep doing this,” he said.

“I know.”

“I’m not—” Eddie stopped. His fingers tightened on the metal. “I’m not good at this. The part where someone sticks around after the interesting shit is over.”

Steve looked at the bandage, at the scar on Eddie’s eyebrow, at the way the porch light caught the ends of his hair. “I’m not here for the interesting shit,” he said.

Eddie huffed something that might have been a laugh. “Then what are you here for?”

“Thursday,” Steve said. “And whatever’s left of the chili. And this.”

He didn’t move. Neither did Eddie. The truck’s engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the row of trailers a dog barked once and went quiet.

Eddie let go of the doorframe. His hand found Steve’s wrist, thumb resting over the pulse point, light enough that Steve could have pulled away. He didn’t.

“Okay,” Eddie said.

“Okay,” Steve answered.

They stood there until the cold got into their jackets and Wayne turned the porch light off and on again, the universal signal that it was time to come in or go home. Eddie’s thumb moved once, a small press, and then he stepped back.

“Thursday,” he said.

“Thursday,” Steve said, and got in the truck.

He drove with the radio on this time, low, some station out of Indianapolis playing songs he half-remembered from high school. The roads were dry. The lights of Hawkins spread out ahead of him, ordinary and small. In the rearview the trailer park disappeared behind a stand of trees.

He would be back on Thursday. He already knew that. And Eddie would be there, hair wet from the pool, thermos waiting, whatever came next still unsaid between them and, for once, not needing to be said yet.

That was enough. For now, it was enough.
