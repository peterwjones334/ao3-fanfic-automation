The apartment was quiet except for the low hum of the refrigerator and the occasional creak of the building settling. Buck sat on the edge of the couch, elbows on his knees, watching the streetlights flicker through the half-closed blinds. His left wrist still ached when he turned it too fast, the residual stiffness from the cast that had come off three weeks ago. The doctor had said the bone was solid. Buck still caught himself favoring it.

Eddie moved around the kitchen with the careful economy of someone who had learned not to waste motion. He set a mug on the coffee table without speaking, steam rising from the tea. Buck looked at it, then at Eddie’s hands—steady, a faint scar across the knuckle from a call last month—and didn’t reach for the mug yet.

“Chris is asleep,” Eddie said. His voice was low. “He asked if you’d still be here in the morning.”

Buck nodded. He had been staying on the couch since the accident. Three nights. Eddie hadn’t asked him to leave. Buck hadn’t offered to go back to his own place, which was still half-unpacked boxes and a bed he hadn’t made. The silence between them had weight, but it wasn’t the old kind of silence. It didn’t press.

Eddie sat in the armchair across from him. He had changed out of his uniform into a gray t-shirt and soft jeans. There was a smudge of dirt near the collar from when he’d carried Christopher in from the car. Buck watched the smudge and thought about the way Eddie’s shoulders had curved around the boy, protective even in the ordinary act of getting him home.

“You don’t have to stay,” Eddie said. “If your wrist is bothering you, the couch—”

“It’s fine.” Buck flexed his fingers. The ache was dull, manageable. “I can sleep on my side.”

Eddie studied him. The lamp behind the chair put half his face in shadow, but Buck could still read the line of his mouth. Careful. Measuring. Eddie had always measured things before he said them.

“You could take the bed,” Eddie said. “I’ll take the couch.”

Buck shook his head. “I’m good.”

They sat with that. Outside, a car passed, headlights sliding across the wall and then gone. Christopher’s door was cracked; a thin strip of night-light spilled into the hallway. Buck had helped hang the glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling last year. They were still there. He had checked.

Eddie leaned forward, elbows on his knees, mirroring Buck without seeming to notice. “The physical therapist said you shouldn’t push the wrist yet.”

“I’m not pushing it.”

“You’re still reaching for things with it.”

Buck almost smiled. “You’re watching me reach for things.”

“Someone has to.” Eddie’s mouth twitched, not quite a smile either. He looked down at his own hands. “You scared him. When they brought you in. He heard the radio.”

Buck’s throat tightened. He had known that. Bobby had told him later, careful and flat, that Christopher had been in the station when the call came through. Eddie had been on the other side of the city. Buck had been the one who went through the floor. The memory of the fall was short: the sound of the beam, the drop, the way the air left him all at once. After that, mostly voices and the bright blur of the hospital lights.

“I’m okay,” Buck said. It was true in the way that mattered for a report. The bone had knitted. The bruises were fading to yellow. He could walk without limping if he concentrated.

Eddie nodded, but he didn’t look convinced. He stood and went back to the kitchen. Buck heard the soft clink of a glass, the tap running. When Eddie returned he set the water beside the tea. He didn’t sit again. He stood with his hands in his pockets, looking at the blinds as if the street outside required his attention.

“I keep thinking about the loft,” Eddie said. “Before. When you were still living there. Chris used to ask why you never stayed for breakfast.”

Buck remembered those mornings. He would leave before dawn most nights, careful not to wake them. He had told himself it was easier. That Eddie needed space. That he didn’t want to make anything complicated for Christopher. The excuses had been neat. They had also been thin.

“I didn’t want to assume,” Buck said.

“You never assumed.” Eddie turned. His expression was open in a way Buck wasn’t used to seeing when they were both awake and the shift was over. “That’s the problem, sometimes.”

Buck reached for the water. His right hand. The left stayed on his knee. He drank. The water was cold. When he set the glass down, Eddie was still watching him.

“You can stay,” Eddie said. “Not just tonight. Until the wrist is solid. Or longer, if you want. Chris likes having you here. He sleeps better when he knows you’re in the living room.”

Buck looked at the hallway. The night-light made a soft rectangle on the floor. He thought about the way Christopher had climbed onto the couch with him earlier, careful of the wrist, and shown him a drawing of the three of them at the beach. The drawing had been folded twice. Christopher had explained the folds were so it would fit in Buck’s pocket if he needed it.

“I don’t want to make it harder for you,” Buck said.

Eddie was quiet for a long moment. Then he crossed the room and sat on the couch, not close enough to touch, but close enough that Buck could feel the heat of him through the cushion. Their knees were a hand’s width apart.

“It’s not harder,” Eddie said. “It’s quieter when you’re not here. Chris notices. I notice.” He exhaled, a short sound that wasn’t quite a laugh. “I keep waiting for you to decide you’re done being careful with us. And then you stay anyway.”

Buck turned his head. Eddie’s profile was steady. There was a small cut on his jaw from shaving that morning; Buck had watched him in the bathroom mirror while Christopher brushed his teeth and talked about a science project. Ordinary. The kind of morning Buck had stopped letting himself picture years ago.

“I don’t know how to stop being careful,” Buck said. The words came out low. He hadn’t planned them. “I keep thinking if I move wrong, something breaks. Not the wrist. Something else.”

Eddie nodded. He didn’t look away. “Then we go slow. You stay on the couch. Or the bed, if your back starts complaining. We have breakfast. You help with homework if you want. We don’t decide anything else until you’re ready.”

Buck’s chest felt tight and warm at the same time. He looked at their hands on the cushions. Eddie’s left, Buck’s right. Close. Not touching.

“Okay,” Buck said.

Eddie stood. He picked up the empty water glass and the mug of tea Buck hadn’t finished. In the kitchen the dishwasher clicked open and shut. When he came back he had a folded blanket under one arm and a pillow.

“Get some sleep,” he said. “I’ll wake you if Chris needs anything. Or if your wrist starts acting up.”

Buck took the pillow. Their fingers brushed. Eddie didn’t pull away immediately. The contact was brief, warm, and then gone. Eddie set the blanket on the arm of the couch.

“Good night, Buck.”

“Good night.”

Eddie turned off the lamp. The room went dim, lit only by the streetlights and the thin line from Christopher’s door. Buck heard the soft click of Eddie’s bedroom door. He lay back on the couch, the pillow under his head, and listened to the quiet. His wrist ached, but less than it had an hour ago. Somewhere in the apartment, water ran briefly in the bathroom and then stopped.

In the morning, Christopher would come out first. He always did. He would check that Buck was still there, then climb up carefully and show him whatever he had dreamed about. Eddie would make coffee. The three of them would eat at the small table by the window. Buck would reach for the milk with his right hand. Eddie would pass it without comment. The day would move forward in small, ordinary pieces.

Buck closed his eyes. Outside, the street was still. Inside, the refrigerator hummed. He stayed.
