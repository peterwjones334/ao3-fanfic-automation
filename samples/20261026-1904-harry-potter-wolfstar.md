**The Flat Above the Bookshop**

The bell over the door of Lupin’s Books rang at half past four, the same thin note it had made for three years. Remus did not look up from the invoice he was checking. Most customers at that hour wanted something specific and cheap; the ones who browsed stayed longer and rarely bought.

“Moony.”

He set the pen down. Sirius stood just inside the threshold, hands in the pockets of a coat that was too large across the shoulders and too short at the wrists. The coat was new. The rest of him looked the same as it had the day the Ministry returned his wand and told him he could go: thinner than he should be, hair longer than was practical, eyes the colour of a sky that had not decided whether to clear.

“I thought you were still in Scotland,” Remus said.

“Was. Board’s finished with me. Told me to find somewhere quieter than the Leaky.” Sirius glanced at the shelves. “You have a room upstairs.”

“I have a room upstairs that is currently full of stock.”

“I can shift boxes.”

Remus considered the invoice, then the empty chair behind the counter, then the man who had once known the exact distance between their beds at Hogwarts. “The kettle’s on. Come through.”

They moved the boxes that evening. Sirius worked without talking much, which was new. When the last crate was stacked against the wall of the small bedroom that had been Remus’s office, he stood in the middle of the cleared floor and turned once, as if measuring the space against something only he could see.

“It’s fine,” he said. “Better than a cell.”

Remus did not answer that. He made tea instead and set two mugs on the low table in the sitting room that doubled as a kitchen. The table had one leg shorter than the others; Sirius wedged a folded piece of cardboard under it without being asked.

They drank in silence for a while. Outside, rain started against the window above the shop sign. Remus watched the water run in thin lines and tried to decide whether this was a good idea. Sirius had never been good at staying still. Remus had never been good at asking him to leave.

“I’ll pay rent,” Sirius said.

“You don’t have to.”

“I do, actually. The solicitors were very clear about that. Monthly. In writing.” He pulled a folded letter from the coat and set it on the table. “Also, I can cook. Not well, but better than whatever you’re doing with those tins.”

Remus looked at the open tin of beans still sitting on the counter from lunch. “The beans are fine.”

“The beans are a cry for help.”

The corner of Remus’s mouth moved. He covered it with the mug.

The first week passed in small adjustments. Sirius rose early and ran the length of the street before the shop opened, then came back with his hair damp and a paper under his arm. He read at the table while Remus sorted stock. Sometimes he asked about a title; sometimes he simply listened to the scratch of Remus’s pen and the occasional customer’s voice from below. At night he took the sofa until Remus told him the second bed was clear. After that he slept with the door open an inch, which Remus noticed and did not mention.

On the eighth night the rain came hard enough to drum on the roof. Remus was still downstairs closing the till when he heard the scrape of a chair upstairs, then silence. He locked the front door, turned off the lights, and climbed the stairs with the day’s takings in one hand.

Sirius sat at the table with both palms flat against the wood. His shoulders were drawn tight. When Remus stepped into the room he did not startle, but his fingers pressed harder against the surface.

“Bad day,” Remus said. It was not a question.

“Nothing happened.” Sirius’s voice was level. “Just the usual. Walls. Voices that aren’t there. I keep expecting someone to tell me the time is up.”

Remus set the money tin on the counter and filled the kettle again. He did not turn on the main light. The small lamp over the sink was enough. He watched the water come to a boil and thought of the last full moon, three nights ago, when he had locked himself in the back room of the shop with the usual precautions and Sirius had stayed upstairs without comment. In the morning there had been a plate of toast and a note that said the shop needed new shelves in the poetry section. The note was still in Remus’s desk drawer.

He poured two mugs and carried them to the table. Sirius took his without looking up. Their fingers brushed. Neither of them moved away.

“You can stay,” Remus said. “As long as you want. The rent letter can wait.”

“I don’t want charity.”

“It isn’t.” Remus sat. The chair creaked. “I have the space. You have nowhere else that isn’t louder than this. That’s practical.”

Sirius lifted his head. In the low light the lines around his eyes looked deeper than they had at twenty-one. “You always did prefer practical.”

“It keeps the books balanced.”

They finished the tea. Sirius washed the mugs and set them upside down on the draining board. Remus locked the till money in the small safe under the counter and came back upstairs to find the light off and Sirius already on the sofa, coat folded under his head like a pillow. The bedroom door stood open.

Remus turned down the covers on the second bed and left the door as it was.

Two weeks later the full moon fell on a Thursday. Remus closed the shop at noon and told the regulars he would reopen on Saturday. Sirius watched him move the heavy bolts into place on the back-room door and said nothing until Remus turned the key.

“I’ll be here,” Sirius said.

“You don’t have to stay in the flat. There’s a pub two streets over that stays open late.”

“I know where the pub is.” Sirius leaned against the counter. The old scar that ran across his left knuckles stood out pale against the skin. “I’ll be here when you come back.”

Remus nodded once and went upstairs to change. He left the bedroom door open again.

The night was long. When the worst of it passed he lay on the floor of the back room with his cheek against the cool boards and listened to the sound of water running in the sink upstairs. Footsteps crossed the sitting room, paused, then continued. A chair scraped. The kettle clicked. Remus closed his eyes and counted the minutes until the transformation eased enough for him to stand.

He washed in the small sink behind the counter, dressed in the clothes he had left folded on the lowest shelf, and climbed the stairs. The flat smelled of toast and something sharp that might have been lemon. Sirius sat at the table with a newspaper spread in front of him and a second plate waiting. He did not look up until Remus had sat down.

“Eat,” he said. “Then sleep. The shop can wait until noon.”

Remus picked up the toast. His hands were steady enough. Across the table Sirius turned a page of the paper without reading it. Their knees touched under the short leg of the table and stayed there.

In the third month Sirius began bringing records home from a second-hand shop on the corner. He played them low in the evenings while Remus worked on the accounts. One night the needle stuck on a track neither of them knew. Sirius lifted the arm, set it down again, and the same three notes repeated. He swore under his breath and reached for the tonearm. Remus caught his wrist.

“Leave it,” he said. “It’s fine.”

Sirius looked at the place where Remus’s fingers rested. The skin there was warm. Neither of them moved for a count of five. Then Sirius turned his hand, slow enough that Remus could have pulled away, and their palms met. The record kept skipping. Outside, a bus passed and the window rattled once.

“I keep thinking you’ll tell me to go,” Sirius said. His voice was quiet. “Or that you’ll decide this is too much trouble. The moons. The nights I don’t sleep. All of it.”

“I haven’t.”

“You might.”

Remus tightened his grip, not hard. “I won’t.”

They stayed like that until the record ended and the needle lifted on its own. Sirius did not let go. Remus did not ask him to.

On a clear evening in late spring they walked to the end of the street and stood under the streetlamp that never quite worked. Sirius had his hands in his pockets again. Remus watched the light flicker and thought of all the years they had spent not saying certain things because there had always been someone else in the room, or a war, or a door that locked from the outside.

“I want to stay,” Sirius said. “Not just the flat. Here. With you. If that’s—”

“Yes,” Remus said.

Sirius turned. The streetlamp buzzed. Remus stepped closer until their coats brushed and the cold air between them narrowed to nothing. When Sirius kissed him it was careful at first, then less so. Remus’s hand found the back of Sirius’s neck and stayed there. They stood under the faulty light until a car turned the corner and its headlights swept across them. Sirius laughed once, short and surprised, and rested his forehead against Remus’s.

“Practical,” he said.

“Extremely,” Remus answered.

They walked back to the shop. Remus unlocked the door. Sirius followed him inside and up the stairs. The second bed stayed made. The sofa stayed empty. In the morning the kettle boiled at the usual time and the short leg of the table held, cardboard and all.
