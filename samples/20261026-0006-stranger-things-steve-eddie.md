**The Store After Closing**

Eddie Munson locked the front door of Vinyl & Voltage at ten-seventeen and killed the neon sign. The M behind the counter still glowed faint red through the glass. He left it. Wayne said the light kept the kids from thinking the place was empty and worth trying.

He was restacking the metal section when the knock came. Three short raps, the same pattern every time.

Steve Harrington stood on the sidewalk with his hands in his jacket pockets and his hair doing that thing it did when the wind got at it. Behind him the Family Video sign was dark. Robin had closed early for a date with a girl from the community college.

Eddie flipped the deadbolt.

“We’re closed,” he said.

“I know.” Steve stepped in and the door shut behind him with the little bell that never quite rang right. “Robin told me to get the new Dio before it was gone. I didn’t want to explain to Dustin why I failed.”

Eddie went back to the bin. “It’s not new. It’s a reissue. And it’s already gone. Kid from the high school bought the last one at six.”

Steve made a face. “Figures.” He drifted toward the counter and stopped. “You still got that list you made me?”

“The one that wasn’t all Priest and Sabbath?” Eddie pulled a folded sheet from under the register. “It’s got a couple of things that won’t make your ears bleed. Fair warning.”

Steve took it. Their fingers didn’t touch. Eddie noticed that they didn’t.

They stood like that for a minute. The only sound was the cooler humming in the back and the occasional car on the road. Steve read the list like it required concentration. Eddie watched the way the overhead light caught the scar on Steve’s temple, the one that never quite faded.

“You working the morning shift tomorrow?” Steve asked without looking up.

“Wayne’s covering. I got a thing.”

“Right.” Steve folded the paper and put it in his pocket. “Thanks.”

He left. The bell half-rang. Eddie locked the door again and stood with his hand on the bolt until the red of Steve’s taillights disappeared.

---

Three nights later Steve came back with a paper bag and two coffees from the place by the high school that stayed open late. He set one on the counter without asking.

Eddie was on the floor with a box of returns. His knee was wrapped in athletic tape that had already started to peel at the edges. He’d missed a step coming out of the trailer and hit the gravel harder than he wanted to admit. Wayne had iced it and said nothing.

Steve looked at the knee, then at Eddie’s face.

“You fall off a stage or something?”

“Trailer steps,” Eddie said. “They’re a hazard. Should put up a sign.”

Steve crouched. He didn’t touch the bandage. He just looked at the swelling above the tape and the way Eddie was holding his weight off that leg.

“You got ice?”

“In the back.”

Steve found it. He wrapped the bag in a clean rag from the stock room and handed it over. Eddie pressed it against the knee and tried not to hiss. The cold went straight through.

They drank the coffee. Steve sat on an overturned milk crate and didn’t leave when the clock hit eleven. Eddie put on a record low enough that the bass didn’t rattle the windows. Steve didn’t comment on the music. He just listened, one knee up, the other stretched out so his foot rested near Eddie’s good leg.

“Robin says you’ve been sleeping here some nights,” Steve said.

Eddie kept his eyes on the ice. “Wayne’s got the night shift at the plant. Place is quiet. I like the quiet.”

“Yeah.” Steve turned the coffee cup in his hands. The lid made a small plastic sound. “I get that.”

They didn’t talk about the quiet that came after the gates closed. They didn’t have to.

---

The next week Steve brought a tape he’d made. He’d written the track list on the inside of the case in the same careful print he used for the kids’ notes. Side A was stuff Eddie had put on the list. Side B was things Steve liked that he thought Eddie might tolerate. There was a blank space at the end of Side B labeled *your call*.

Eddie played it after Steve left. He sat on the counter with the lights off except the neon and the glow from the stereo. When the last song ended he rewound it and started again.

He didn’t give the tape back.

---

It rained on a Thursday. The kind of rain that turned the parking lot into a shallow lake and made the neon smear across the glass. Eddie was inventorying the back room when he heard the door. He hadn’t locked it yet. He went out with a box cutter still in his hand.

Steve was soaked from the shoulders down. His hair was plastered to his forehead. He held a plastic bag that had split at the bottom; the corner of a VHS case stuck out.

“Power’s out at my place,” he said. “Tree took a line on Maple. Robin’s with her parents. I figured—” He stopped. Water dripped off his chin onto the mat. “You got power.”

Eddie looked at the box cutter, then at Steve, then put the cutter down.

“Yeah,” he said. “Come on.”

He locked the door. They went to the back room where the space heater lived and the old couch Wayne had dragged in years ago still smelled like cigarettes and dust. Eddie found a clean towel. Steve used it on his hair and didn’t look in the mirror.

They ate the sandwiches Steve had managed to keep dry. Outside the rain kept hitting the metal roof. Steve’s shoulder brushed Eddie’s when he reached for the second half of his sandwich. Neither of them moved away.

“You ever think about leaving?” Steve asked. His voice was quiet enough that the rain almost covered it.

“Hawkins?” Eddie said. “Every day. Then I remember the rent on a place in Indianapolis and Wayne’s knee and the fact that I still owe the store for the last order that got lost in the mail. So. Not yet.”

Steve nodded like that made sense. He was still damp at the collar. Eddie could see the pulse in his throat.

“I almost left,” Steve said. “After. Robin wanted to. I told her I had to stay for the kids. Truth is I didn’t know what else to do with myself. Still don’t, some days.”

Eddie didn’t answer. He reached over and turned the heater up one notch. The element clicked and the air got warmer against their shins.

Steve’s hand was on the cushion between them. Eddie’s was on his own knee. The space was small enough that if either of them shifted the right way their fingers would meet.

Neither of them shifted.

---

The power came back on at the Harrington house around midnight. Steve didn’t leave until one. Eddie walked him to the door. The rain had eased to a mist. Steve stood on the sidewalk with his hands in his pockets again and looked at the wet asphalt like it might give him a line.

“Thanks,” he said.

“Anytime,” Eddie said, and meant it more than he wanted to.

Steve nodded once. He got in the car. The engine turned over. He didn’t drive away right away. Eddie watched the taillights stay on for a full minute before they finally pulled out.

---

Two nights later Eddie was restocking when the phone rang. It was Steve. The line was quiet for a second after Eddie said the store’s name.

“I made another tape,” Steve said. “Side B’s full this time. Thought you might want it.”

“Bring it by,” Eddie said. “We’re open till ten.”

“I know.”

Steve showed up at nine-forty with the tape and a paper bag of fries from the diner. They ate them standing behind the counter while the last customer of the night flipped through the dollar bin and left without buying anything. Eddie locked up. They put the tape on.

Halfway through Side B Steve reached over and turned the volume down.

“I keep thinking about that night the power went out,” he said. “I keep thinking I should’ve said something then.”

Eddie stayed where he was, one hip against the counter, arms crossed so his hands wouldn’t do anything stupid.

“Said what?”

Steve looked at him. The neon painted the edge of his jaw red.

“That I come here because you’re here,” he said. “Not because of the music. Not because Robin told me to. I come here and I stay past close and I make excuses because I want to be in the same room as you and I don’t know how to say that without sounding like an idiot.”

The stereo clicked as the tape reached the end of a song. Neither of them moved to flip it.

Eddie uncrossed his arms. His hands were steady. He stepped closer until the toes of his boots nearly touched Steve’s sneakers.

“You don’t sound like an idiot,” he said.

Steve’s breath caught. Eddie watched it happen. He lifted one hand and set it, carefully, against the side of Steve’s neck. The skin was warm. Steve leaned into the touch like he’d been waiting for permission.

“Okay,” Steve said. His voice was rough. “Good.”

Eddie kissed him. It was short and a little off-center because Steve moved at the same time. Their teeth clicked. They both laughed, quiet and close, and then Eddie tried again and got it right. Steve’s hands came up and settled on Eddie’s waist, thumbs pressing just above the waistband of his jeans. The neon hummed. Outside a car went by and its headlights swept across the closed blinds and were gone.

When they broke apart Steve rested his forehead against Eddie’s.

“I’m not good at this,” he said.

“Neither am I,” Eddie said. “We’ll figure it out.”

Steve nodded. His fingers tightened once, then relaxed. Eddie kept his hand where it was, feeling the steady beat under his palm, and didn’t let go.

---

They flipped the tape. Side A started again. They stayed behind the counter with the lights low and the door locked and the neon still glowing through the glass, red against the dark street, until the last song ended and the machine clicked off.

Neither of them reached to turn it back on. They didn’t need to.
