The site was quieter after five. Scaffolding creaked in the wind off the river, and the old brick warehouse still held the day’s heat. Shane Hollander stood on the second-floor walkway with a clipboard and a pencil, marking the places where the new load-bearing beams would sit. He liked the numbers. They didn’t argue.

Below him, Ilya Rozanov’s crew was packing up. Tools clanged into metal boxes. Someone laughed. Ilya himself was still on the ground floor, sleeves rolled, chalk in one hand, arguing with the structural engineer about a support that Shane had already approved twice.

“It is too much steel,” Ilya said. His accent made the words blunt. “The building does not need all this. You are making it heavy for nothing.”

Shane leaned on the railing. “The calculations say otherwise. If you ignore them, the floor above you drops six centimeters in the first winter.”

Ilya looked up. Dust streaked one cheekbone. He smiled, small and sharp. “You always assume the worst, Hollander. Maybe the building wants to lean a little.”

“Buildings don’t want things.”

“People do.”

Shane went back to his notes. He had been doing this for three weeks—revising drawings after Ilya’s crew found something unexpected in the walls, then defending those revisions in front of the client who had hired both of them because neither firm would work under the other. The client called it collaboration. Shane called it a slow way to lose a commission. Ilya called it fun, usually while standing too close and smelling like sawdust and the cheap coffee from the truck on the corner.

By the time the others left, the light had gone thin. Shane was packing his laptop when he heard the sharp crack and the shorter sound that followed—a body hitting concrete.

He was down the stairs before the echo finished. Ilya sat on the floor with one leg bent wrong under him, one hand braced on a stack of bricks. Blood ran from a cut above his eyebrow, bright against the dust. A length of pipe lay nearby, still rocking.

“Don’t,” Ilya said when Shane dropped to a knee. “It is fine. I moved too fast.”

“You didn’t.” Shane looked at the angle of the ankle and felt his stomach turn. “Stay still.”

“I am not a building.”

“Then stop arguing with the engineer.” Shane already had his phone out. The nearest clinic was ten minutes if traffic held. He put the call on speaker while he checked the cut with careful fingers. Ilya’s skin was warm. He flinched once, then stayed still under the touch.

They argued in the waiting room about who would drive the truck back. Ilya lost. The ankle was a bad sprain and a hairline fracture; the cut needed three stitches. Shane drove. Ilya sat in the passenger seat with his boot cut open and a plastic bag of ice melting against the joint, eyes closed against the streetlights.

“You did not have to stay,” Ilya said when they pulled up outside the temporary rental the firm had given him. A second-floor walk-up with a narrow stairwell.

“You can’t do stairs.”

“I can.”

Shane got out anyway and came around to open the door. Ilya let him. That was new. Upstairs, the apartment was mostly empty—a mattress on the floor, a kettle, two mugs. Shane found a chair and set it so Ilya could sit with the leg up. He rinsed the blood out of the cloth he had used in the truck and handed it over.

Ilya took it. Their fingers brushed. Neither of them moved for a second.

“You always look at me like you are measuring something,” Ilya said.

“I measure everything.”

“Not everything.” Ilya’s mouth curved. The stitches pulled. “Sometimes you just look.”

Shane stepped back. The kitchen was small. He filled the kettle because his hands needed a job. Outside, a bus sighed at the corner. Ilya watched him from the chair, the ice pack dripping slowly onto a folded towel.

They ate takeout on the floor because the table was still in boxes. Shane sat with his back against the wall and his knees drawn up. Ilya’s injured leg rested across the only cushion. The food was too salty. Neither of them said so.

“You could have called one of your guys,” Shane said.

“They would make jokes. You do not make jokes.”

“I make jokes.”

“When?”

Shane thought about it. “In university. Once.”

Ilya laughed, short and surprised, then stopped when it pulled at the stitches. He pressed the heel of his hand under the bandage. Shane watched the motion and did not look away.

Later, after the lights were off except for the one over the sink, Shane stood in the doorway with his keys in his hand. Ilya was on the mattress, the bad ankle propped on a pillow made of folded shirts. The room smelled like antiseptic and the river air that came through the open window.

“Stay,” Ilya said. Not a question. His voice was quieter than it had been all day.

Shane looked at the narrow strip of floor between the mattress and the wall. He looked at Ilya’s hand resting on the blanket, fingers loose. He set the keys on the counter.

In the morning the ankle was worse. Swollen, dark at the edges. Shane drove them back to the clinic for the boot the doctor had ordered. Ilya sat in the passenger seat again and did not complain about the speed or the route. When they stopped at a light he reached over and adjusted the vent so the air hit Shane’s hands on the wheel instead of his own face.

“You are cold,” he said.

“I’m fine.”

“You always say that.”

On the site the next day Shane worked from the drawings while Ilya directed from a folding chair someone had brought up. The crew pretended not to notice the boot or the way Shane checked the height of the new supports twice before he let anyone cut. At lunch Ilya sat on the tailgate of the truck and ate half of Shane’s sandwich without asking. Shane let him.

They did not talk about the night. They talked about load calculations and the client’s insistence on original brick wherever possible. When Ilya’s voice went tight with pain, Shane handed him the water bottle and waited until he drank. The crew finished early. Shane walked Ilya to the truck and opened the door.

“You do not have to keep doing this,” Ilya said.

“I know.”

“Then why.”

Shane looked at the boot, at the scrape still visible under the edge of the bandage on Ilya’s forehead, at the way Ilya held himself carefully even sitting down. “Because the numbers say the floor holds if we do it right. And because you keep trying to stand on it anyway.”

Ilya was quiet for the drive. At the apartment he let Shane help him up the stairs without the usual commentary. Inside, he sat on the mattress and watched Shane move around the small kitchen like he had been there longer than one night.

“I keep thinking about the way you looked at the pipe,” Ilya said. “After it fell. Like you already knew where it would land.”

“I didn’t.”

“You moved like you did.”

Shane set two mugs on the counter. The kettle clicked off. He poured and carried one over. Their hands met around the ceramic. Ilya’s fingers stayed where they were.

“I don’t want the building to lean,” Shane said.

“I know.”

“I don’t want you to either.”

Ilya’s thumb moved once against the side of Shane’s hand. Outside, the river wind moved the scaffolding. Inside, the only sound was the low tick of the cooling kettle and the slower sound of both of them breathing in the same small room.

Shane sat on the edge of the mattress. The boot rested between them. Ilya shifted until their shoulders touched. Neither of them spoke for a long time. When Shane finally turned his head, Ilya was already looking at him, the usual sharpness gone from his face, replaced by something quieter and more careful.

“Stay,” Ilya said again.

This time Shane answered.

“All right.”

He stayed. The drawings could wait until morning. The numbers would still be there. For now the floor under them held, and that was enough.
