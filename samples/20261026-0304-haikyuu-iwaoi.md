The gym smelled like resin and old sweat. Iwaizumi had the key because the university coach trusted him not to leave the lights on overnight, and because no one else wanted to stay past nine. He was halfway through taping the net when the door opened behind him.

Oikawa stood in the entrance in a jacket that was too thin for March, hair longer than it had been at the airport three days ago. The flight from Buenos Aires had left him with shadows under his eyes that the hallway light did not soften.

“You’re still here,” Oikawa said. His voice was the same. Lighter than it needed to be.

“Someone has to fix the tape after you lot leave it crooked.” Iwaizumi kept his back turned. The tape roll was sticky against his thumb. “Practice ended two hours ago.”

“I know.” Oikawa stepped inside. The door closed. “I walked past the building twice. Then once more.”

Iwaizumi finished the knot. He turned. Oikawa had not moved from the painted line that marked the end of the court. The distance between them was the length of a good serve.

They had not spoken for eight months. Not since the phone call where Oikawa had said he was staying in Argentina another year and Iwaizumi had said fine and then nothing else. The silence after that had been deliberate. Iwaizumi had filled it with shifts at the clinic and with the quiet of the apartment he shared with no one.

“Your shoulder,” Iwaizumi said. “The trainer in Sendai sent the notes.”

“It’s fine.”

“It isn’t. You still drop your elbow on the jump.”

Oikawa’s mouth twitched. “You watched the tape.”

“They sent it.” Iwaizumi walked to the ball cart. He picked one up, turned it once in his hands, set it down. “You want to hit or you want to talk.”

“Both,” Oikawa said. “If you’ll stay.”

They started with serves. Oikawa stood at the line. Iwaizumi waited at the other end with a cart of balls and the whistle he did not use. The first three went long. The fourth clipped the tape and spun out. On the fifth Oikawa stopped, rolled his shoulder once, and tried again. The ball cleared. Iwaizumi caught it clean and rolled it back without comment.

They did not talk about the phone call. They did not talk about the letters Oikawa had written and not sent, which Iwaizumi had found later in a drawer when he went to collect a box of old jerseys. The letters were still there. Iwaizumi had not read past the first line of the second one.

After twenty minutes Oikawa’s shirt stuck to his back. He pulled it off between sets and tied it around his waist. The scar on his left shoulder was new. Thin. Surgical. Iwaizumi looked at it once and then looked at the floor.

“You didn’t say it was that bad,” he said.

“It healed.”

“That’s not the same.”

Oikawa served again. The ball hit the back line and bounced high. Iwaizumi let it go. He walked across the court. Oikawa did not step back. They stood close enough that Iwaizumi could see the pulse at the base of Oikawa’s throat.

“You could have called,” Iwaizumi said.

“You told me not to.”

“I told you to decide what you wanted.”

“I did.” Oikawa’s voice dropped. “I wanted both. The team there and you here. That isn’t a decision.”

Iwaizumi’s hand lifted. He stopped it halfway to Oikawa’s shoulder and let it fall. The scar looked raised under the lights. He wanted to press his thumb against it and feel the heat of the skin underneath. He did not.

“Come over,” he said. “There’s leftover rice. You look like you haven’t eaten.”

Oikawa nodded. He put the shirt back on. They locked the gym together. On the walk to the station Oikawa stayed half a step behind. Iwaizumi did not slow down until the platform, when the train was already pulling in and there was nowhere else to stand.

The apartment was smaller than the one they had shared for a month after high school, before Oikawa left. Iwaizumi heated the rice. Oikawa sat at the table with his hands flat on the wood and watched the kettle. Steam fogged the window above the sink. Outside, the street was empty.

They ate. Oikawa spoke about the flight and about the coach who had told him to rest. Iwaizumi listened and did not ask the questions that sat behind his teeth. When the bowls were empty Oikawa stood to wash them. Iwaizumi took the sponge from his hand.

“Sit.”

Oikawa sat. He watched Iwaizumi’s shoulders move under the old practice shirt. The silence stretched. It was the same silence as the phone call, only this time neither of them hung up.

“I read one of the letters,” Iwaizumi said. He kept his back to the table. Water ran over his hands. “The one that started with the date of the final.”

“I wrote that the night after.”

“I know.” Iwaizumi turned the tap off. He dried his hands on the towel and stayed where he was. “You said you kept looking for me in the stands even though you knew I wasn’t there.”

Oikawa’s chair scraped. He stood. The distance between them was the length of the kitchen. Shorter than the court. Easier to cross and harder.

“I still do that,” Oikawa said. “In Argentina. When the lights come up. I look for the place where you used to stand.”

Iwaizumi faced him. The overhead light made Oikawa’s eyes darker than they were in daylight. He looked tired in a way that sleep would not fix. Iwaizumi crossed the kitchen. He stopped when their shoes touched.

“Show me the shoulder,” he said.

Oikawa pulled the shirt off again. The scar ran from the front of the joint to the back, pale against the rest of his skin. Iwaizumi touched the edge of it with two fingers. Oikawa’s breath caught and then evened out. The muscle underneath was still solid. The skin was warm.

“It hurts when I serve,” Oikawa said. Quiet. “Not all the time. Only when I think about whether it will hold.”

“It will hold.” Iwaizumi kept his hand where it was. “You just have to stop trying to prove it every time you jump.”

Oikawa’s forehead dropped against Iwaizumi’s. Their noses touched. Neither of them moved for a long moment. The kettle clicked as it cooled. Somewhere below them a neighbor’s television murmured through the floor.

“I don’t know how to come back,” Oikawa said. “Not all the way. The contract is signed through next season.”

“Then don’t come back all the way.” Iwaizumi’s free hand found Oikawa’s wrist. The pulse there was steady. “Come back when you can. Call when you can’t. I’ll answer.”

Oikawa made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost something else. He turned his face. Their mouths met. The kiss was careful at first, then less so. Iwaizumi tasted rice and the mint of the gum Oikawa had chewed on the train. Oikawa’s hands settled on Iwaizumi’s waist and stayed there.

They stayed in the kitchen until the light outside the window changed from streetlamp yellow to the first grey of morning. Oikawa slept on the couch because the bed was narrow and because neither of them had decided yet what the bed would mean. Iwaizumi sat on the floor beside it with his back against the cushions and Oikawa’s hand resting, loose, in his hair.

In the morning Oikawa’s flight was at noon. They walked to the station again. At the ticket gate Oikawa stopped. He looked at Iwaizumi the way he used to look at the court before a match he was not sure he could win.

“I’ll call when I land,” he said.

“I’ll be here.”

Oikawa nodded. He stepped through the gate. Halfway down the platform he turned. Iwaizumi raised one hand. Oikawa raised his in answer. Then the train came and the doors closed and the platform was empty except for the people who had places to be.

Iwaizumi walked back to the apartment. He opened the drawer where the letters were and read the rest of them. When he finished he put them back. The phone on the table was quiet. He left it there and went to the clinic. The first patient of the day was a second-year with a strained ankle. Iwaizumi taped it and told him to ice it and to stop jumping until it stopped hurting. The student nodded. Outside the window the sky was clear.

At four the phone rang. Iwaizumi answered on the second ring.

“Landed,” Oikawa said. The line crackled. “The shoulder feels better.”

“Good.”

“Iwa-chan.”

“Yeah.”

“I’ll call again tomorrow.”

“I’ll answer,” Iwaizumi said. He kept the phone to his ear after the line went quiet. The clinic was empty. The tape on the table was the same brand they used at the university gym. He rolled it once between his fingers and set it down. Outside, the afternoon light moved across the floor in a slow line. He watched it until it reached the door.
