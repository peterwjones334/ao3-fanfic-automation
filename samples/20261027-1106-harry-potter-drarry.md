**The Weight of Paper**

The bookshop closed at six. Draco had learned the exact weight of the silence that followed. It settled against the windows once the last customer left and the streetlights outside began their slow climb. He wiped the counter with a cloth that smelled of lemon oil and old paper, the motion automatic. The bell above the door had not rung in twenty minutes.

When it did, the sound was sharp.

Harry Potter stood in the doorway with snow melting on the shoulders of his coat. His glasses were fogged. He pushed them up with one finger and looked around the narrow aisles as if he expected something to step out from between the shelves.

“We’re closed,” Draco said. His voice came out quieter than he intended.

Harry turned. Recognition moved across his face and settled. “Malfoy.”

“Potter.”

They had not spoken in three years. The last time had been in a Ministry corridor after Draco’s probation hearing. Harry had nodded once and kept walking. Now he stepped inside and closed the door behind him. The bell chimed again.

“I’m not here for books.”

“I gathered.”

Harry pulled a folded photograph from his pocket. The image showed a silver snuffbox with a crest Draco recognised at once. The Black family. “This was taken from a private collection two nights ago. The trail led here. Someone sold it to you.”

Draco looked at the photograph, then at Harry. The coat was Muggle, dark and practical. There was a faint scar at Harry’s temple that had not been there the last time they met. “I buy estates. I don’t ask questions about the previous owners if the provenance looks clean.”

“This one doesn’t.”

Harry set the photograph on the counter. The paper was damp at the edges. Draco studied the snuffbox again. He had bought it three weeks earlier from a man who claimed it came from a cleared house in Kent. The man had smelled of cigarettes and rain. Draco had paid in cash because the man had asked for it.

“I still have the ledger,” he said.

They sat in the back room with the ledger open between them. The tea Draco made was too strong. Harry drank it anyway. He asked about dates and descriptions. Draco answered. Outside, snow continued to fall against the glass. Neither of them mentioned the war. When Harry stood to leave, he paused with his hand on the doorframe.

“I’ll need to come back tomorrow,” he said. “To check the rest of the entries.”

Draco nodded. He locked the door after Harry left and stood for a moment with his palm against the wood. The shop smelled of paper and lemon oil. He did not turn the lights off for another hour.

Harry returned the next afternoon. Then the afternoon after that. Sometimes he brought a new question about a different item. Sometimes he claimed he was only checking whether Draco had remembered anything else. They drank tea in the back room. Draco noticed the way Harry’s hands stilled when he listened. Harry noticed the careful way Draco held his cup, as if the porcelain might crack if he gripped it too tightly.

On the sixth visit, Harry arrived as Draco was locking the front door. A storm had been forecast. The sky was already dark.

“Walk with me,” Harry said. “There’s a café two streets over. They stay open late.”

They walked. The rain started before they reached the corner. Harry offered his coat. Draco refused. They sat under the café’s narrow awning with two cups of coffee between them and watched the water run in the gutters. Harry’s knee rested against Draco’s under the table. Neither of them moved it.

“You live above the shop,” Harry said after a while.

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

Harry looked at the rain. “I have a flat in Clerkenwell. It’s quiet. Too quiet, most nights.”

Draco turned his cup in a slow circle. The ceramic was warm against his palms. “I thought Aurors kept odd hours.”

“We do. The quiet is still there when I get home.”

They stayed until the rain eased. Harry walked him back to the shop. At the door, he stopped. His hand lifted as if he meant to touch Draco’s sleeve, then dropped again.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“Tomorrow,” Draco answered.

The break-in happened on a Thursday. Draco had stayed late to catalogue a new box of letters. He locked the door at half past eight and stepped into the alley behind the shop. Two men were waiting. One of them held a length of pipe. The other grabbed for the satchel Draco carried. The satchel held nothing but ledgers. Draco twisted, and the man with the pipe swung. The blow caught him across the ribs and drove him into the brick wall. Pain flared white along his side. His wrist twisted under him when he fell.

Harry came out of the dark at the mouth of the alley. He moved faster than Draco expected. The pipe clattered across the cobbles. One man ran. The other stayed long enough to take a punch that split his lip, then fled as well. Harry crouched beside Draco. His hands were careful when he checked the wrist.

“Can you stand?”

“Yes.”

Harry helped him up. They walked the short distance to the flat above the shop. Inside, Harry turned on the lamp by the sofa. Books covered the cushions. Draco sat on the floor instead. Harry found the first-aid kit in the bathroom and wrapped the wrist with steady fingers. He pressed a cold cloth to the bruise forming along Draco’s ribs. Neither of them spoke until the wrapping was finished.

“I was across the street,” Harry said. “I’ve been watching the shop in the evenings. Not for the case. I just—” He stopped. His thumb rested against the inside of Draco’s wrist, just above the bandage. “I didn’t like the idea of you locking up alone.”

Draco looked at the place where Harry’s hand touched him. The skin there felt warmer than it should have. “You didn’t have to.”

“I know.”

They sat on the floor until the kettle boiled. Harry made tea. He brought both cups and sat opposite Draco with his back against the sofa. The bruise along Draco’s ribs pulsed in time with his heartbeat. He drank the tea and watched the way the lamplight caught the edge of Harry’s glasses.

“I used to think,” Draco said, “that if I stayed quiet enough, everything else would stay quiet too.”

Harry’s mouth moved, almost a smile. “How’s that working?”

“Not especially well.”

Harry set his cup down. He shifted closer. Their shoulders touched. Draco felt the solid line of Harry’s arm through the wool of his jumper. Outside, a car passed. The sound faded. Harry’s hand found Draco’s uninjured one. Their fingers fitted together without effort.

“I’m not going to disappear this time,” Harry said. His voice was low. “If you don’t want me to.”

Draco turned his head. Harry was close enough that Draco could see the faint freckles across the bridge of his nose. He had not noticed them before.

“Stay,” Draco said.

Harry stayed. He slept on the sofa with a blanket Draco found in the cupboard. In the morning, Draco came into the kitchen wearing a jumper that had once belonged to his father and was now soft from washing. Harry stood at the counter with two mugs of coffee. He looked at Draco for a long moment, then set the mugs down and crossed the small space between them.

The kiss was careful. Harry’s hand rested at the side of Draco’s neck. Draco’s uninjured hand settled against Harry’s chest. They stayed like that until the coffee cooled. When they parted, Harry’s forehead rested against Draco’s.

“Tomorrow,” Harry said again.

Draco’s mouth curved. The expression felt unfamiliar and not unpleasant. “Tomorrow.”

The snuffbox was returned to its owner three weeks later. Harry closed the file. He kept coming to the bookshop. Sometimes he brought sandwiches. Sometimes he simply sat in the back room while Draco worked. The silence between them changed. It no longer pressed against the windows. It settled, instead, around the two of them, light enough to leave room for whatever came next.
