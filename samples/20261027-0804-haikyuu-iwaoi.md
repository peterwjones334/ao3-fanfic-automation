Iwaizumi’s apartment in Irvine smelled like the eucalyptus he’d hung by the window and the leftover curry he’d forgotten on the stove. The clock on the microwave read 1:14 a.m. He sat on the floor with his back against the couch, phone balanced on his knee, and watched the typing bubble appear and vanish three times before the message finally landed.

*Flight lands at LAX 4:40. Don’t wait if you’re working.*

He typed back: *I’m not working. I’ll be there.*

The bubble returned, then: *You still drive like you want to die, Iwa-chan.*

Iwaizumi set the phone face-down on the rug. Outside, a car passed and its headlights swept across the ceiling. He counted the seconds until the sound faded. Four years since Oikawa had signed with the Argentine league. Two years since the last time they’d been in the same country for more than a layover. The last photo Oikawa had sent showed him on a balcony in Buenos Aires, hair longer, shoulders broader, a plastic cup of something pink in his hand. Iwaizumi had stared at it until the screen timed out, then put the phone in a drawer for three days.

He stood, rinsed the pot, and left the light on in the kitchen so the place wouldn’t feel empty when he came back.

At the terminal he stood under the arrivals board with his hands in the pockets of his jacket. People streamed past with rolling bags and paper cups. When Oikawa appeared, he was taller than Iwaizumi remembered and thinner through the face. The navy tracksuit jacket was unzipped over a white shirt. He stopped three feet away and looked at Iwaizumi the way he used to look at a serve he was about to try for the first time—half calculation, half dare.

“You got old,” Oikawa said.

“You got loud,” Iwaizumi answered, and the corner of Oikawa’s mouth lifted.

They didn’t hug. Oikawa took the passenger seat and spent the drive south talking about the team’s setter rotation and a teammate who insisted on playing reggaeton in the locker room. Iwaizumi kept both hands on the wheel and answered when there was a gap. At a red light he glanced over and found Oikawa watching the side of his face instead of the road.

“What,” Iwaizumi said.

“Nothing.” Oikawa turned forward again. “You still do that thing with your jaw when you’re tired.”

They ate takeout at the small table by the window. Oikawa used the chopsticks Iwaizumi kept in the second drawer, the ones with the chip on the left stick. He told a story about a practice match in Mendoza where the gym’s air conditioning had failed and everyone had played in the heat until the floor was slick. Iwaizumi listened and thought about the summer they were sixteen, the two of them alone in the school gym after everyone else had gone home, the way the late light had turned the floor gold. He had wanted to say something then and had not. He still had not said it.

After dinner Oikawa stretched out on the couch with his feet over the armrest. Iwaizumi sat on the floor again, laptop open, pretending to review a training plan. The apartment was quiet except for the low hum of the refrigerator and the occasional click of Oikawa’s phone.

“I almost didn’t come,” Oikawa said.

Iwaizumi closed the laptop. “Why.”

“Because I keep thinking if I stay away long enough the feeling will go somewhere else.” Oikawa’s voice was flat, the way it got when he was describing a lost point he still believed he could have saved. “It doesn’t. It just gets quieter. Then I see a photo of you with the team you work with and it gets loud again.”

Iwaizumi set the laptop aside. His hands felt large and useless in his lap. “You could have called.”

“I did call. Last March. You didn’t pick up.”

“I was in a meeting. I texted back.”

“You texted *sorry, later.* Later never happened.” Oikawa sat up. The tracksuit jacket had slipped off one shoulder. “I don’t need you to fix it. I just need to know if I’m the only one who still notices when the other one goes quiet.”

Iwaizumi looked at the rug. There was a coffee stain near the corner he’d never managed to get out. “You’re not.”

The silence after that was different from the one on the drive. Oikawa’s breathing changed. Iwaizumi heard the soft sound of fabric when Oikawa stood. He stayed where he was until Oikawa’s bare feet stopped in front of him. A hand settled on the top of his head, light, the way Oikawa used to touch him after a match when the score was close and neither of them wanted to talk yet.

“Hajime,” Oikawa said.

Iwaizumi tipped his head back. Oikawa’s face was close and careful. The hand on his hair moved, thumb brushing the place where his hairline met his temple. Iwaizumi reached up and caught Oikawa’s wrist, not to pull it away, just to hold it there. His pulse was steady under Iwaizumi’s fingers.

“I don’t know how to do this without breaking something,” Iwaizumi said.

“Then we start small.” Oikawa’s other hand came down and rested against the side of Iwaizumi’s neck. “I’m here for eleven days. We can be quiet together for eleven days and see if it still feels like something we have to protect from each other.”

Iwaizumi nodded once. He let go of the wrist and stood. They were the same height now. Oikawa’s mouth was slightly open, like he was about to say something else and had decided against it. Iwaizumi leaned in and kissed him, short and dry, the kind of kiss that asked a question instead of answering one. Oikawa made a small sound and kissed him back, one hand settling between Iwaizumi’s shoulder blades, the other still against his neck.

When they stopped, Oikawa rested his forehead against Iwaizumi’s. “Okay,” he said, almost to himself.

They slept in the same bed that night, backs touching, the way they used to on the floor of Iwaizumi’s childhood room when the heat made the second floor unbearable. In the morning Iwaizumi woke first. Oikawa’s hair was across the pillow. One of his hands had found Iwaizumi’s wrist in the night and stayed there. Outside, a neighbor’s sprinkler clicked on. Iwaizumi stayed still and counted the clicks until Oikawa stirred, turned over, and opened his eyes.

“Still here,” Oikawa said, voice rough.

“Still here,” Iwaizumi answered.

They made coffee and drank it at the table. Oikawa’s knee rested against Iwaizumi’s under the wood. Neither of them moved it. The light through the window was pale and ordinary. Iwaizumi thought about the eleven days and the flight that would come after them and the distance that would open again. He thought about the typing bubble and the three times it had appeared and vanished. He reached across the table and covered Oikawa’s hand with his own. Oikawa turned his palm up without looking and laced their fingers together.

“Later,” Oikawa said.

“Later,” Iwaizumi agreed, and meant it.
