Shane had the cabin to himself for three days. That was the plan. No media, no teammates, no parents texting about recovery timelines. Just the lake, the quiet, and the dull throb in his left shoulder that the physio said would fade if he actually rested. He had driven up from Montreal with a bag of groceries and a single suitcase, the rental key still warm in his pocket from the handoff at the office. The place was small: one bedroom, a kitchen that opened onto a living room with a woodstove, and a porch that faced the water. He had chosen it because the listing said “no neighbors within a kilometer.” 

He was halfway through unpacking the groceries when the rental car pulled up the gravel drive. Not the one he’d booked. He set the milk down and watched through the kitchen window as Ilya Rozanov got out, duffel over one shoulder, looking exactly as unbothered as he always did in pressers. The late-afternoon light caught the ends of his hair. Shane stayed where he was for a full ten seconds, hoping the car would reverse. It did not.

Shane opened the door before Ilya could knock.

“Wrong cabin,” Shane said.

Ilya checked the key tag in his hand, then the number on the post. “Same number. Same lake. Maybe they double-booked.”

“They didn’t.”

Ilya’s mouth twitched. “Then someone paid extra for company. I am not complaining yet.”

Shane should have told him to leave. Instead he stepped back. The shoulder protested when he moved the door wider. Ilya’s eyes tracked the motion, then the sling Shane had left on the chair by the table.

“You are hurt.”

“Minor. Dislocated in the last game. It’s fine.”

“You are bad liar.” Ilya dropped the bag by the wall. “I will take the couch. You take the bed. Do not argue.”

Shane argued for ten minutes. He lost. By the time the light started to fade, Ilya had claimed the far end of the couch with a blanket pulled from the linen closet and was scrolling through his phone like this was ordinary. Shane stood in the kitchen doorway and watched him for a moment longer than necessary, then went to finish putting the milk away.

---

They lasted until dinner before the silence got heavy. Shane had made pasta because it was easy one-handed. The sauce came from a jar. Ilya ate most of it standing at the counter, watching the water go dark outside. Every so often his gaze flicked to Shane’s shoulder, then away again.

“Boston played you hard,” Ilya said. “I saw the replay. Dirty hit.”

“Part of the job.”

“Still.” Ilya set the plate down. “You should have stayed down longer. Ref would have called it.”

Shane almost smiled. “Since when do you care about calls?”

“Since it was you.”

The words sat between them. Shane looked at his fork. He had spent years learning how to stand across from Ilya without giving anything away. The skill felt thinner here, in a kitchen that smelled like garlic and lake air. “You don’t have to do this.”

“Do what?”

“The thing where you show up and act like we are friends when no one is watching.”

Ilya was quiet long enough that Shane thought he might leave. The radiator ticked. Outside, something small moved through the underbrush and was gone. Then Ilya said, “I am not acting.”

Shane’s chest tightened. He stood, rinsed his plate, and went to the porch. The air was cold enough that his breath showed. He heard the door open behind him a minute later. Ilya came out with two mugs. He handed one over without comment. The coffee was stronger than Shane usually made it.

They stood at the railing. The lake was still. Somewhere across the water a loon called, long and low.

“My shoulder is not the problem,” Shane said eventually. “The problem is I keep waiting for the season to end so I can stop pretending I do not look for you in every arena.”

Ilya’s hand tightened on his mug. “I know.”

“You do not.”

“I do.” Ilya turned. The porch light caught the edge of his jaw. “I look too. Then I score and I tell myself it is enough. It is not.”

Shane’s pulse kicked. He set the mug on the railing so he would not drop it. The ceramic made a small sound against the wood. “We cannot do this in the open.”

“I know that also.” Ilya stepped closer. Not touching. “But we are not in the open right now.”

Shane looked at him. Really looked. The careful set of his shoulders, the way his fingers flexed like he was holding himself back from something. Shane had seen that look across center ice for years. He had never seen it this close without a helmet between them. Up close, Ilya smelled like the car he’d driven in and the faint trace of the soap from the cabin bathroom.

“If I say yes,” Shane said, “you do not get to vanish after. Not this time.”

Ilya’s breath left him in a short huff. “I will not.”

Shane closed the distance. The first kiss was careful, almost clumsy, because his good arm was the one he had to use to steady himself and Ilya was trying not to jar the shoulder. They figured it out. Ilya’s hand settled at the back of Shane’s neck, warm and sure. Shane leaned into it until the ache in his shoulder was the least important thing he could feel. When they broke apart, both of them were breathing harder than the short walk back inside should have required.

Ilya rested his forehead against Shane’s. “Couch is still mine. You need the bed.”

“Shut up,” Shane said, but his voice was rough.

They went inside. Ilya locked the door. Shane turned off the porch light. The cabin settled around them, small and quiet and, for the first time in a long time, not empty. Shane lay awake longer than he meant to, listening to the low sound of Ilya shifting on the couch in the other room. He fell asleep with the memory of Ilya’s thumb against the side of his neck.

---

Shane woke to the smell of coffee and the sound of someone moving carefully in the kitchen. His shoulder was stiff but not worse. He lay still for a minute, listening. No rush of panic. Just the ordinary creak of floorboards and the low murmur of the radio Ilya must have found. When he got up, the sling was still on the chair. He left it there.

Ilya was at the stove in a T-shirt and the sweatpants he’d slept in, frying eggs. He glanced over when Shane appeared.

“Sit. I will not let you burn yourself.”

Shane sat. The eggs were slightly overdone. He ate them anyway. Ilya poured coffee and stayed standing, leaning against the counter like he had last night. The radio played something low and instrumental. Outside, fog sat on the water.

“We have two more days,” Ilya said.

“Yes.”

“After that we go back.”

Shane nodded. He turned the mug in his hands. The heat soaked into his palms. “I meant what I said. About not vanishing.”

“I heard you.” Ilya looked at the lake through the window. “My contract ends in two years. Maybe sooner if I ask. I have thought about it.”

Shane’s fork paused. “Thought about what?”

“Not playing against you every time.” Ilya’s mouth curved, small. “Or playing with you. Either way, less pretending.”

The idea sat in Shane’s chest, too large and too early and still possible. He reached across the table. Ilya took his hand without hesitation, thumb brushing the inside of Shane’s wrist where the pulse was steady. They stayed like that while the fog burned off and the first clear light hit the water. Neither of them mentioned the plates. Outside, a boat passed far down the lake and the wake reached the shore in thin lines. Inside, the only sound was the radio and the occasional creak of the cabin settling.

Shane thought about the last time they had been in the same city with nowhere to be. Hotel room. Door locked. Lights off. He had left before Ilya woke. This time he stayed at the table until the coffee went cold.

---

They walked the shoreline in the afternoon. Shane kept his pace even so the shoulder would not complain. Ilya matched him without comment, hands in his pockets, stopping when Shane stopped to look at a stretch of exposed rock. The air smelled like pine and wet stone.

“Your parents know you are here?” Ilya asked.

“They know I am resting. They do not know the address.” Shane glanced over. “Yours?”

“No one asks.” Ilya’s tone was flat, then less so. “Is better.”

Shane nodded. He did not push. They reached a flat stretch of shore where the water was clear enough to see the bottom. Ilya skipped a stone. It bounced twice before it sank. Shane tried with his good arm and managed one skip. Ilya made a sound that was almost a laugh.

“Practice,” he said.

“Maybe next year.”

They stood with their shoes just above the waterline. The sun was warm on Shane’s back. He could feel Ilya looking at him and did not turn until he was ready. When he did, Ilya’s expression was open in a way Shane rarely saw outside of locked rooms.

“Two more days,” Shane said again, quieter.

“Then we figure out the rest.”

Ilya stepped closer. This time Shane met him halfway. The kiss was slower. Shane’s free hand found the front of Ilya’s jacket and stayed there. When they pulled back, Ilya’s forehead rested against his for a second longer than necessary.

They walked back in the same silence they had arrived in, but it sat differently between them. At the cabin, Ilya made tea. Shane sat on the couch with his shoulder propped on a pillow and watched the light move across the floor. Ilya brought the mugs over and sat at the other end, close enough that their knees almost touched. Neither of them moved away.

Outside, the lake stayed calm. Inside, the season still waited. For now the only thing that mattered was the weight of the quiet and the fact that, for once, neither of them was counting the minutes until they had to leave.
