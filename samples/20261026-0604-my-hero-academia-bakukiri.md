The kettle clicked off at half past six. Kirishima stood at the counter in the half-dark of the kitchen, one hand braced on the edge, the other wrapping the mug. His shoulder still seized when he reached too high. He kept his left arm low and poured with the right.

Bakugou’s door stayed shut. The light under it had been out since four. Kirishima set the second mug on the table anyway and sat. The steam fogged the window above the sink. Outside, the street was quiet except for the early bus and the scrape of someone’s boots on the pavement two floors down.

They had taken the apartment six months after the last big operation. Kirishima’s agency had offered desk work. He had turned it down and taken the physical-therapy contract instead. Bakugou had walked out of the hospital with a discharge paper and no assignment. He had followed Kirishima to the listing, looked at the two bedrooms and the shared bath, and said the rent was fine if Kirishima stopped hovering. Kirishima had stopped saying he wasn’t hovering.

The door opened at six forty. Bakugou came out in the same shirt he had slept in, hair flat on one side. He looked at the mug, then at Kirishima, then took it and drank standing up.

“Shoulder’s stiff,” Kirishima said.

“It’s fine.”

“You skipped the stretches yesterday.”

Bakugou set the mug down harder than he needed to. “You count them now?”

“I hear the door when you do them.”

Bakugou didn’t answer. He rinsed the mug and left it in the rack. The water ran too long. Kirishima watched the line of his back under the thin shirt and kept his hands on the table.

They ate toast. Bakugou cut the loaf one-handed and didn’t ask for help. Kirishima buttered his own slice and left the knife where Bakugou could reach it without turning. The radio on the counter played the traffic report. Neither of them turned it off.

At seven fifteen Bakugou put on his coat. The zipper caught at the shoulder. He stood still until Kirishima stepped in and eased it free. Their hands met for half a second on the metal. Bakugou didn’t pull away until the zipper was down. He left without saying when he would be back.

Kirishima cleaned the counter. He folded the dish towel twice and hung it straight. The apartment was quiet enough that he could hear the building’s pipes. He sat on the edge of the couch and worked the shoulder through the range the therapist had written down. The pain sat under the bone and stayed there. He counted to thirty and stopped.

The rain started after noon. It came in sheets against the kitchen window and turned the street gray. Kirishima stood at the sink with a mug of tea gone cold and watched the water run in the gutters. His appointment was at three. He left early because the bus ran slow in weather like this and because he did not want to sit in the empty apartment listening for a key that would not turn until after dark.

The clinic smelled like rubber mats and disinfectant. The therapist had him lie on his back and lift a light bar through the same arc he practiced at home. The bar shook. She steadied his wrist with two fingers and did not comment on the tremor. When the set ended she asked about sleep. He said it was fine. She wrote something on the chart and told him to keep the door open at night if the shoulder locked. He nodded and did not tell her the door was already open most nights because the radiator in his room knocked and the sound carried less when the hall was clear.

On the bus home the rain had thinned to mist. He kept his left hand in his pocket so the shoulder would not seize when the bus braked. At the stop nearest the apartment he bought two onigiri from the shop on the corner and put them in his bag. The paper was already damp by the time he reached the building.

Bakugou was in the kitchen when he came in. The lights were on. A pot sat on the stove with the lid half off. Steam rose from whatever was inside. Bakugou looked up, saw the bag, and turned back to the pot.

“You’re late,” he said.

“Clinic ran long.”

“They fix it.”

“Not yet.”

Bakugou stirred. The spoon scraped the bottom. “Sit.”

Kirishima sat. The chair creaked under him. He set the onigiri on the table between them and watched Bakugou plate the rice and the vegetables he had cut small enough to manage with one hand. The bandage on Bakugou’s knuckles was new. The tape edges were neat.

“Door again,” Kirishima said.

“Different door.”

They ate. The rain ticked against the window. Bakugou’s left hand rested on the table edge, fingers loose. Kirishima watched the way the knuckles flexed when he reached for his water glass and did not ask if it hurt.

After the dishes Bakugou stood at the sink longer than the washing required. Kirishima dried the last plate and set it in the rack. Their shoulders were close enough that the fabric of Bakugou’s shirt brushed Kirishima’s sleeve when he shifted his weight.

“You don’t have to wait up,” Bakugou said.

“I’m not.”

“You were.”

“The clinic asked about sleep.”

Bakugou turned off the water. He dried his hands on the towel Kirishima had hung straight that morning. “And.”

“I said it was fine.”

Bakugou looked at him. The light from the fixture over the sink cut across his face and left the rest in shadow. “Is it.”

“Most nights.”

Bakugou nodded once. He folded the towel and hung it where it had been. His hand stayed on the bar for a moment after the towel was in place. Kirishima did not move. The radiator knocked once in the wall between their rooms.

“Show me the stretches,” Bakugou said.

They moved to the living room. Kirishima sat on the edge of the couch. Bakugou stood in front of him and lifted his left arm. The motion stopped short of the height Kirishima had marked that morning. Kirishima stood. He set two fingers under the elbow again and waited. Bakugou breathed in. The arm rose the rest of the way. Their hands stayed in contact longer than the stretch required. When Bakugou lowered the arm he did not step back.

“Tomorrow,” Kirishima said.

“Yeah.”

Bakugou’s hand rested on Kirishima’s good shoulder for the length of one breath. The weight was light. It did not stay. He turned and went to his room. The door stayed open. Kirishima sat on the couch until the light under Bakugou’s door went out, then stood and turned off the kitchen fixture. The apartment settled around the small sound of the radiator and the last of the rain on the glass.

Three days later the weather cleared. Kirishima came home from the clinic with a new set of resistance bands and a note that the range had improved by four degrees. He found Bakugou on the floor in the living room, back against the couch, working the same bands with his left hand. The band was the lightest one. It still trembled at the top of the arc.

Kirishima set his bag down and sat on the floor opposite him. Neither of them spoke. Bakugou finished the set. He let the band fall and flexed his fingers. The bandage was gone. The cut had closed to a thin red line.

“Four degrees,” Kirishima said.

“Better than zero.”

“Yeah.”

Bakugou looked at the band in his lap. “You gonna keep coming home with onigiri.”

“If the shop’s open.”

“They close at eight.”

“I know.”

Bakugou nodded. He coiled the band and set it on the couch cushion between them. His hand stayed there, palm down, close enough that Kirishima could have covered it with his own if he moved. He did not. Outside a car passed. The radiator knocked once, then settled.

“I’m not fine,” Bakugou said. The words were quiet. They sat in the space between the cushions. “Not every day.”

Kirishima kept his eyes on the band. “Me either.”

They stayed on the floor until the light outside the window went blue. When Bakugou stood he offered his right hand. Kirishima took it. The grip was firm and lasted until they were both upright. Bakugou did not let go until Kirishima’s weight was steady.

In the morning the second mug was already poured when Kirishima came out. Bakugou stood at the window with his back to the room. The steam rose between them. Outside the street was dry. The early bus passed. Inside, Bakugou lifted his left arm without being asked. The motion reached the height they had marked. He held it. Kirishima stepped closer and set his fingers under the elbow. The contact stayed until Bakugou lowered the arm of his own accord.

“Again tomorrow,” Kirishima said.

“Yeah,” Bakugou answered, and did not move away.
