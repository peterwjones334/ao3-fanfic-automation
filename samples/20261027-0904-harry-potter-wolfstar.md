The rain had started just after midnight and showed no sign of stopping. Remus stood at the kitchen window of the rented cottage, one hand braced against the frame, the other wrapped around a mug that had gone cold. Outside, the lane was a dark ribbon of standing water. He had stopped checking the wards an hour ago. They would hold or they would not.

The knock, when it came, was three soft raps. Not the pattern he and the others had used. Still, he set the mug down carefully and crossed the room. The door stuck; it always stuck in damp weather. When he pulled it open, Sirius was on the step with his collar turned up and his hair plastered to his forehead.

Neither of them spoke. Remus stepped back. Sirius came in, shaking water onto the mat, and closed the door behind him with the heel of his boot.

“You look like hell,” Remus said.

“Full moon last week.” Sirius’s voice was rough. He toed off his boots and left them in a puddle. “You?”

“Same.”

They stood in the narrow hall. The single lamp on the side table threw their shadows long across the plaster. Remus could smell wet wool and, underneath it, the clean sharp scent of the rain. He had not expected the way his chest tightened at it. He turned toward the kitchen.

“Tea?”

“If you have anything stronger, I won’t refuse it.”

There was half a bottle of firewhisky on the shelf above the cooker. Remus poured two measures and pushed one across the table. Sirius sat without being asked. His hands were steady around the glass, but the skin at his knuckles was split and healing.

“Order business?” Remus asked.

“Not tonight.” Sirius drank. “I needed somewhere that wasn’t full of people watching me to see if I’d crack.”

Remus nodded. He understood that better than he wanted to. For a while they only listened to the rain against the roof. The cottage was small—two rooms and a kitchen, rented under a name that was not his. He had been here six weeks. Long enough for the neighbors to stop noticing when he appeared or vanished.

Sirius set the empty glass down. “I can leave if you want.”

“You can stay,” Remus said. The words came out before he had decided to say them. He cleared his throat. “There’s a chair by the fire. Or the spare bed, if you prefer something horizontal.”

Sirius looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded once and stood.

In the sitting room the fire had burned low. Remus added a log and prodded the embers until the new wood caught. Sirius took the armchair nearest the hearth and stretched his legs toward the heat. His socks were mismatched; one had a hole at the toe. Remus sat on the low stool he used for mending and watched the flames.

“You’re still teaching,” Sirius said after a while.

“When they’ll have me.” Remus kept his eyes on the fire. “It’s not much. Enough to keep the rent paid and the pantry stocked. Most weeks.”

“And the rest of the time?”

“I walk. Read. Try not to count the days until the next moon.” He hesitated. “I sleep when I can.”

Sirius made a low sound that might have been agreement. The silence that followed was not uncomfortable, exactly. It was the silence of two people who had once known each other well enough to leave things unsaid, and who were no longer sure what was still permitted.

Outside, the wind shifted. Rain struck the window in a sudden hard sheet. Sirius flinched at the sound—small, involuntary. Remus saw it and did not comment.

“You can take the bed,” he said. “I’ll stay out here.”

“I’m not putting you out of your own room.”

“It’s not my room. It’s a room I sleep in.” Remus stood. His knee protested; he ignored it. “Come on. The sheets are clean. I changed them yesterday.”

The bedroom was barely large enough for the narrow bed and a chest of drawers. Remus lit the lamp on the nightstand and turned the quilt back. Sirius hovered in the doorway, dripping onto the floorboards.

“There’s a towel on the back of the door,” Remus said. “And if you need anything—”

“I’ll be fine.”

Remus nodded and left him to it. In the sitting room he banked the fire and sat with a book he did not open. He listened to the soft sounds of Sirius moving about, then the creak of the bedframe as he settled. The rain continued. Sometime near three the wind dropped and the only noise was the steady tick of water from the eaves.

He must have dozed. He woke to the sound of a muffled cry from the bedroom—not loud, but sharp enough to pull him upright. For a moment he stayed where he was, listening. The cry did not come again. Instead there was the rustle of sheets and a low, ragged breath.

Remus stood. He crossed the short distance to the bedroom door and stopped with his hand on the latch. He could hear Sirius breathing on the other side—too fast, then deliberately slower, as if he were counting.

“Sirius?” he said, quietly.

A pause. Then: “Yeah.”

Remus opened the door. The lamp was still burning, turned low. Sirius was sitting up against the headboard, one hand pressed flat to his chest. His face was pale in the dim light. He did not look at Remus.

“Sorry,” he said. “Didn’t mean to wake you.”

“You didn’t.” Remus stayed in the doorway. “Bad dream?”

Sirius nodded once. His fingers curled and uncurled against the quilt. “They come and go. Usually I’m alone when they do.”

Remus stepped inside and closed the door behind him. The room felt smaller with both of them in it. He sat on the edge of the bed, careful not to crowd. The mattress dipped under his weight.

“Do you want to talk about it?” he asked.

“No.” Sirius’s mouth twitched. “Not really. It’s the same one, more or less. Azkaban, or the war, or both at once. Sometimes I’m still there. Sometimes I’m watching it happen to someone else.” He exhaled. “It passes.”

They sat in silence. Remus could feel the heat coming off Sirius’s body, close enough that their shoulders almost brushed. He did not move away. Neither did Sirius.

“I used to think,” Remus said at last, “that if I got far enough away from everything, the dreams would stop following me. They didn’t.”

“No,” Sirius agreed. His voice was quieter now. “They don’t.”

Another stretch of quiet. The rain had gentled to a soft patter. Remus became aware of the steady rise and fall of Sirius’s breathing beside him, slower than it had been. He risked a glance. Sirius was watching the far wall, but his posture had eased; one hand rested open on the quilt between them.

Remus looked at that hand. He thought of all the times he had almost reached for it and had not. He thought of the years of letters that had gone unanswered, and the ones he had never sent. His own hand moved before he could stop it. He laid it over Sirius’s, palm to knuckles, nothing more.

Sirius went still. For a moment Remus thought he would pull away. Then his fingers turned, slowly, and their hands settled together, loosely linked.

Neither of them spoke. The contact was warm and ordinary and more than Remus had allowed himself to want in a long time. He kept his eyes on their joined hands. Outside, the first thin light of morning was beginning to gray the edges of the curtains.

“You can stay,” Remus said again, softer this time. “As long as you need.”

Sirius’s thumb moved once across the back of his hand. “I might take you up on that.”

They stayed where they were until the lamp burned itself out and the room filled with the pale, rain-washed light of dawn. Remus did not let go. Sirius did not ask him to.
