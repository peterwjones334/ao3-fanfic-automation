The cabin was smaller than the pictures. Shane stood on the gravel with his duffel and watched the chimney smoke thin against the grey sky. His phone still had the pin Ilya had sent—three days ago, no explanation, just the coordinates and “come if you want.” The rental car idled behind him, engine ticking as it cooled. He killed it and walked up the steps.

Ilya opened the door before Shane could knock. He wore a dark sweater, sleeves pushed to his elbows, and his hair was longer than it had been at the end of the season. He looked at Shane’s bag, then at his face.

“You came.”

“You asked.” Shane stepped past him. The heat inside was immediate, woodstove and something cooking. “You said training.”

“I said come,” Ilya corrected. He took the bag without asking and set it by the couch. “Training is optional.”

Shane looked around. One main room, kitchen along the back wall, stairs leading up to what was probably a loft. The windows faced the lake. Snow had already started, light and steady. “How long have you been here?”

“Two days. I wanted to see if the stove worked.” Ilya moved to the kitchen. “There’s coffee. Or tea. Or nothing.”

Shane took the coffee. They sat at the small table while the snow thickened. Ilya asked about the flight the way someone asks when they already know the answer. Shane answered the way someone answers when they’re still deciding why they’re here. Outside, the lake stayed flat and white.

They cooked dinner together because the kitchen was too narrow for one person to work without the other moving. Ilya chopped onions with the careful rhythm he used on the ice when the game was close. Shane peeled potatoes and tried not to watch the way Ilya’s left hand paused every few cuts. The scar from last March was thin and pale against his wrist. It still pulled when the temperature dropped. Shane had seen the reports. He had also seen the way Ilya taped it during the playoffs and said nothing.

“You can stop staring,” Ilya said without looking up.

“I’m not.”

“You are.” Ilya set the knife down. “It’s fine. It only hurts when I think about it.”

Shane rinsed the potatoes. The water ran cold over his hands. “Then stop thinking.”

Ilya’s mouth did something small. “Helpful.”

They ate at the table because the couch faced the wrong way. The stew was plain and hot. Shane watched the fire in the stove through the glass door and listened to Ilya talk about the trails he had marked. Neither of them mentioned the team or the city or the fact that they usually only saw each other when someone was keeping score. The quiet between them had edges, but it held.

After, they played cards on the floor. The table was covered in maps. Ilya dealt without speaking. Shane lost the first two hands and folded the third. Their knees touched when Ilya leaned in to take the pot. Neither moved. The fire popped. Outside the snow kept falling. Shane watched the way Ilya’s fingers handled the cards, precise, the same way he handled a stick when the play was tight. He looked away before it became obvious.

Shane woke on the second morning to the sound of the door. Ilya was already dressed, boots by the mat, jacket half-zipped. He looked over when Shane came down the stairs.

“Lake’s frozen enough,” he said. “If you want.”

They walked. The path was narrow and the trees held the quiet. Ilya went first and tested the ice with a long stick before he stepped onto it. Shane followed because the alternative was standing on the shore watching. Halfway out, Ilya stopped. The wind moved across the surface and carried the sound of nothing.

“My mother used to bring me here,” Ilya said. His voice was low. “Before everything.”

Shane didn’t ask what everything meant. He stood close enough that their jackets brushed. Ilya’s breathing changed—short, then careful. Shane put a hand between his shoulder blades and left it there. The cold came through the gloves. They stayed until Shane’s fingers started to ache, then turned back without another word. On the way in, Ilya’s steps were slower. Shane matched them.

That afternoon the shoulder locked while Ilya was splitting kindling on the porch. Shane heard the sharp inhale and the clatter of the axe against the boards. He came outside in socks. Ilya was braced against the wall, left arm held tight to his side, face drawn.

“Don’t,” Ilya said.

Shane ignored him. He stepped in, worked his fingers along the muscle the way the trainers did, slow pressure from the base of the neck down. Ilya’s head dropped. His breath fogged the space between them. Shane kept going until the knot eased under his palm. When he stepped back, Ilya caught his wrist. The grip was light. His thumb rested over the pulse point.

“I didn’t know how to say it,” Ilya said. “That I wanted you here. Not for training.”

Shane looked at their hands. The snow had stopped. Light from the cabin window cut a long rectangle across the porch.

“You could have just said that,” he said.

Ilya’s mouth twitched. “Next time.”

They went inside. Shane closed the door. The fire was still going. Ilya poured two cups of tea and set one in front of Shane without asking. They sat on the couch with their shoulders touching. Outside, the lake stayed frozen. Inside, neither of them reached for anything else. Shane turned his hand over, palm up on his thigh. After a minute Ilya’s fingers settled into it, warm and sure.

On the third morning Shane woke to the smell of coffee and the sound of the stove door. Ilya was at the counter in the same sweater, hair still damp from a shower. He looked over when Shane came down.

“Storm’s supposed to clear by afternoon,” he said. “We can leave tomorrow if you want.”

Shane took the mug Ilya held out. Their fingers brushed. “Or we can stay.”

Ilya’s eyes stayed on the coffee. “If you want.”

They spent the day inside. Shane fixed a loose hinge on the cupboard while Ilya watched from the table and offered commentary that was mostly useless. When Shane’s phone buzzed with a message from his agent, he turned it face-down. Ilya noticed and didn’t ask. Later they walked the short loop of the property, boots leaving fresh tracks in the new snow. At the edge of the trees Ilya stopped again. This time he didn’t speak. He just stood with his hands in his pockets and looked at the cabin like he was memorizing it.

Shane stepped up beside him. “You don’t have to do this alone.”

“I know.” Ilya’s voice was quiet. “That’s why I asked you to come.”

They went back. Dinner was leftovers. After, they sat on the floor again because the couch felt too small for the distance they were still keeping. Shane dealt this time. Ilya won two hands and folded the third without looking at his cards. When Shane reached for the deck, Ilya’s hand covered his.

“I keep thinking about last March,” Ilya said. “In the hospital. You stayed.”

“You were concussed. Someone had to.”

“Not someone.” Ilya’s thumb moved once across Shane’s knuckles. “You.”

The fire had burned down to coals. The room was dim. Shane turned his hand under Ilya’s and laced their fingers. The grip was careful on both sides. Outside the wind moved through the trees and the lake stayed still under the ice. Inside, the quiet held until Ilya leaned in, slow enough that Shane could have moved, and rested his forehead against Shane’s temple. Neither of them spoke. The contact was enough.

They stayed like that until the coals settled and the cabin cooled. When they finally moved, it was only to add another log and sit closer. Shane’s shoulder fit under Ilya’s. Ilya’s hand stayed where it was. Tomorrow they could decide about the roads and the flights and everything waiting in the cities. Tonight the snow had stopped and the door was closed and neither of them was keeping score.

Shane woke once in the night to the sound of the wind against the windows. Ilya was still beside him on the couch, one arm draped over the back, fingers loose near Shane’s shoulder. The fire was low. Shane watched the rise and fall of Ilya’s chest and didn’t move. In the morning the roads would be clear enough. They could pack and drive and pretend the three days had been only what they said they were. Or they could stay another night and see what the quiet did when no one was trying to fill it. Shane closed his eyes again. Ilya’s breathing stayed even. The cabin held.
