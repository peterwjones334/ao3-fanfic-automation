The porch light at the Munson trailer flicked on at 11:42 every night Steve drove past. He never stopped, just slowed enough to confirm the glow, then kept going until the dark swallowed the taillights. Wayne kept odd hours at the plant. Eddie kept later ones. Steve told himself he was only checking that the place still stood.

He parked three lots down from the new Family Video and waited until Eddie came out of the back room with a stack of returned tapes. Eddie’s hair was longer than it had been in the spring, half-tied with a rubber band that looked like it had been stolen from Robin’s wrist. The denim vest was gone. In its place he wore a faded black shirt with the sleeves cut off, the scars across his collarbones and ribs visible when he reached for a higher shelf. Steve watched the way the skin pulled and didn’t look away fast enough.

“Help you find something, Harrington?” Eddie asked without turning around. “Or are you just here to glare at the horror section again?”

“Robin needs the returns counted,” Steve said. The lie sat flat between them. Robin had left twenty minutes ago.

Eddie set the stack down and finally looked at him. The fluorescent lights made the circles under his eyes look bruised. “You drive past the trailer every night.”

Steve’s fingers tightened on the counter. “Wayne’s schedule changed. Figured someone should—”

“Someone should what? Make sure I haven’t gotten myself eaten by something that doesn’t exist anymore?” Eddie’s mouth twitched, not quite a smile. “I’m still here. Still ugly. Still working the late shift because nobody else will.”

“You’re not ugly.”

The words came out before Steve could stop them. Eddie went still. For a second the only sound was the low hum of the store’s freezers. Then Eddie laughed, short and sharp, and reached for another tape.

“Go home, Steve.”

Steve didn’t. He stayed until closing, restocking the new releases while Eddie counted the drawer. They didn’t talk. When the lights went out, Eddie locked the front door and handed Steve the spare key without comment. The metal was warm from his pocket.

“Trailer’s unlocked if you need somewhere to go,” Eddie said. “Wayne’s gone till Thursday. Just don’t touch the guitar.”

Steve nodded. He drove past the trailer once more on the way home. The porch light was still on.

---

The rain started just after midnight three nights later. Steve was already on the road when the first fat drops hit the windshield. He told himself he was only going to the gas station. He ended up parked behind Eddie’s van instead, engine off, listening to the water hammer the roof.

The door opened before he could talk himself out of it. Eddie stood in the doorway in sweatpants and a holey shirt, barefoot, hair loose and damp at the ends. He didn’t look surprised.

“Nightmare?” Steve asked.

“Same one.” Eddie stepped back. “Come in or stay out. The porch floods.”

Inside, the trailer smelled like old cigarette smoke and the cheap coffee Wayne bought in bulk. Steve sat on the edge of the couch. Eddie dropped into the chair across from him and lit a cigarette with hands that shook just enough to notice. The scar on his throat was a thin white line that disappeared under the collar of his shirt.

“They don’t stop,” Eddie said after a while. “The bats. Even when I know I’m awake. I can still feel them.”

Steve looked at the cigarette, then at the ashtray already half full. “You could call someone. Robin. Dustin. Me.”

“And say what? That I’m still scared of something we already killed?” Eddie exhaled smoke toward the ceiling. “You’re the one who keeps driving past. You don’t call either.”

“I don’t know what to say.”

“Try the truth. Just once.”

Steve rubbed his palms against his jeans. The denim was damp from the rain. “I keep thinking if I stop checking, something will happen and I won’t know until it’s too late. And I keep thinking if I do stop, I’ll stop for good, and then I won’t have a reason to see you.”

Eddie was quiet long enough that Steve heard the rain change pitch against the tin roof. Then he stubbed the cigarette out and stood. He crossed the small space between them and sat on the couch, close enough that their knees touched. His hand settled over Steve’s, scarred fingers warm and careful.

“I don’t sleep much either,” Eddie said. “Not since the hospital. Not since I woke up and the first thing I saw was you sitting in that plastic chair like you were waiting for a bus that wasn’t coming. You stayed three days. Wayne told me. You didn’t say anything about it.”

“Didn’t seem important.”

“It was.” Eddie’s thumb moved once across the back of Steve’s hand, slow. “I kept waiting for you to decide I wasn’t worth the trouble. Most people do. Eventually.”

Steve turned his hand over so their palms met. He didn’t squeeze. He just held still and let Eddie decide how long it lasted.

“I’m not most people,” he said.

They sat like that until the rain eased. Eddie’s head tipped against Steve’s shoulder somewhere around two in the morning. Steve stayed awake and listened to the steady drag of his breathing. When the porch light timer clicked off, neither of them moved to turn it back on.

---

The next morning Steve made eggs in Wayne’s skillet while Eddie slept. He burned the first batch and scraped them into the trash. The second batch was only slightly overdone. He left them on a plate with a note that said he’d be back after his shift and that the spare key was under the mat where Eddie had put it.

Eddie was waiting on the steps when Steve pulled up that evening. He had a guitar in his lap but wasn’t playing it. The porch light was already on, early.

“Wayne called,” Eddie said when Steve sat down beside him. “Told him you stayed. He asked if you were the one who keeps driving past or if that was still a secret.”

“What did you tell him?”

“That you make bad eggs and that I’m keeping you anyway.” Eddie set the guitar aside. His shoulder brushed Steve’s. “He said to tell you the light stays on. And that if you break the guitar he’ll break your hands. I think he was joking.”

Steve huffed a laugh. The sound felt strange in his own mouth. “I’ll be careful.”

They sat until the mosquitoes drove them inside. Eddie put on a record low enough that the neighbors wouldn’t complain. Steve washed the breakfast dishes. When the needle reached the end of the side, neither of them flipped it. Eddie’s hand found Steve’s wrist again, thumb resting over the pulse.

“Slow,” Eddie said. Not a question.

“Yeah,” Steve answered. “If you want.”

Eddie nodded once. Outside, the porch light stayed on. Steve didn’t drive past it that night. He was already home.
