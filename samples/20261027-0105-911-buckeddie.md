The elevator in 4B stuck again on a Tuesday. Buck heard the metallic grind from his doorway, keys still in his hand, and crossed the hall before the thought finished forming. Eddie stood inside with one hand on the emergency panel and the other braced against the wall, Christopher balanced on his hip.

“Third time this month,” Eddie said. He didn’t look up. The fluorescent strip above them flickered and settled. “Maintenance said they’d fix the sensor.”

“They said that last month.” Buck wedged his shoulder against the door to keep it from sliding shut. The car smelled like wet concrete and the orange cleaner someone had used on the buttons. Christopher’s backpack dug into Eddie’s ribs; the boy’s casted arm rested carefully across Eddie’s chest. “You want me to take him?”

Eddie hesitated. The hesitation was small and familiar. “He’s fine.”

“I know. Offer stands.”

They rode the rest of the way in silence after the doors finally closed. Christopher counted the floors under his breath. When the doors opened on three, Buck held them and watched Eddie step out. The cast was purple with silver stars. Christopher had drawn one of the stars himself with a marker that bled.

“Thanks,” Eddie said. The word sat between them the way it always did, heavier than the elevator ride.

Buck nodded. “Anytime.”

He didn’t go back to his apartment. He went down to the basement instead and found the building manager’s number on a laminated card taped to the boiler. He left a message about the sensor. When he came back up, Eddie’s door was closed. Buck stood in the hallway long enough to hear the low sound of the television and Christopher’s laugh, then he went home and made pasta he didn’t finish.

They had met in the laundry room two months earlier. Eddie had been feeding quarters into a machine that kept rejecting them. Buck had the right change and an old habit of helping people who looked like they were one more small failure away from snapping. Eddie had not snapped. He had said thank you in the same careful way he said it now, and then he had asked if Buck knew which dryer actually dried instead of just spinning. Buck had known. He still knew.

The pining arrived later, after the third time Eddie knocked to borrow a cup of rice and stayed for the twenty minutes it took the rice to cook. Christopher had fallen asleep on Buck’s couch with one sock on and one sock off. Eddie had sat on the floor beside the couch so he wouldn’t have to wake him, and Buck had watched the line of Eddie’s shoulders under a soft gray t-shirt and understood, with the clarity that only arrived when he was already too far in, that he wanted more than the next borrowed cup of anything.

He didn’t say it. Eddie was careful with people the way he was careful with Christopher’s arm. Buck had learned the shape of that carefulness and did not want to be the thing that made it harden.

On Thursday the sensor failed again. This time Eddie was alone. Buck heard the grind and the quiet Spanish curse that followed, and he took the stairs two at a time. When he got the doors open, Eddie was sitting on the floor of the car with his back against the wall and his hands loose between his knees. There was a thin cut across the back of one knuckle, already darkening.

“Tried the override,” Eddie said. “Caught it on the panel.”

Buck crouched. The cut wasn’t deep. He still wanted to clean it. “Come on. I’ve got peroxide.”

Eddie looked at the cut like he had forgotten it was there. “Chris is at PT. I was going to pick him up.”

“I’ll drive you.”

Eddie didn’t argue. In the truck he sat with his hands on his thighs and watched the traffic. The cut had started to bead. Buck kept both hands on the wheel and did not offer the peroxide again until they were back in the building and Christopher was settled on the couch with a juice box and a documentary about sharks.

Eddie let Buck clean the cut at the kitchen sink. The peroxide stung; Eddie’s breath caught once and then evened. Buck pressed a square of gauze in place and taped it with the small medical tape he kept in the drawer because Christopher sometimes scraped his good knee on the playground. Their fingers brushed when Eddie took the roll to finish the last strip himself. Neither of them moved away.

“You don’t have to keep doing this,” Eddie said. The words were quiet. The faucet dripped. “The elevator. The rides. The rice.”

“I know.”

“I’m not good at—” Eddie stopped. He looked at the gauze instead of at Buck. “I don’t want you to think you have to keep showing up.”

Buck rinsed the sink. The water ran clear. “I’m not showing up because I have to.”

Eddie was silent long enough that Buck turned the faucet off. When he looked up, Eddie was watching him with the careful expression he used when Christopher asked a question Eddie didn’t yet have the answer to.

“Okay,” Eddie said.

They ate the rest of Buck’s pasta standing at the counter. Christopher narrated the shark documentary from the other room. Eddie’s shoulder touched Buck’s once when he reached for the salt. He didn’t move it away.

The slow part was not the waiting. The slow part was the way they kept choosing the smaller, safer version of the thing they both already knew. Buck stopped asking if Eddie needed anything and started leaving the extra portion of whatever he cooked on the mat outside Eddie’s door with a sticky note that said “too much again.” Eddie stopped knocking to borrow things and started bringing them back washed, with Christopher’s drawings folded inside. One of the drawings was of the elevator with three stick figures inside it. The tallest one had a circle of yellow for hair.

On a Saturday the building lost power for four hours. Buck’s apartment stayed warm longer than Eddie’s because it faced the afternoon sun. He texted once. Eddie came over with Christopher and a deck of cards. They played Go Fish by the light of Buck’s phone until the battery died, then they sat on the floor and listened to the building settle. Christopher fell asleep with his head on Eddie’s thigh. Eddie’s free hand rested on the carpet between them. Buck set his own hand down an inch away. The space stayed empty until the lights came back on and Eddie stood to carry Christopher home.

At the door Eddie paused. Christopher’s breathing was even against his shoulder.

“You could come with us,” Eddie said. “If you want. There’s leftover pizza.”

Buck went. The pizza was cold. They ate it anyway. After Christopher was in bed, Eddie walked Buck to the door and didn’t open it. They stood in the narrow entryway with the coats and the single pair of Christopher’s sneakers lined up against the wall. Eddie’s hand found the edge of Buck’s sleeve. He held it the way he held the elevator rail when the car lurched—steady, not tight.

“I keep waiting for the part where this gets complicated,” Eddie said.

“It already is.”

Eddie nodded. His thumb moved once across the seam of Buck’s sleeve. “I know. I just didn’t want to be the one who made it more complicated than you wanted.”

Buck turned his hand so their fingers lined up. Eddie’s were warm. The gauze from Thursday was gone; the cut had closed into a thin red line.

“I want it,” Buck said.

Eddie looked at their hands. Then he looked at Buck. The careful expression was still there, but it had shifted. It looked, for the first time, like something that could be set down.

“Okay,” Eddie said again. This time the word didn’t sit between them. It settled.

They didn’t kiss that night. Eddie walked him the rest of the way to the door and stood in the hallway until the elevator arrived. When the doors opened, the sensor held. Buck stepped in. Eddie stayed where he was, one hand on the frame, and watched the doors close. Buck watched back until the gap disappeared.

In the morning there was a sticky note on Buck’s mat. The handwriting was Christopher’s, the letters large and careful. It said “extra pizza” and underneath, in Eddie’s smaller print, “door’s open.”

Buck crossed the hall. The door was open. Eddie stood at the stove in the same gray t-shirt, stirring something that smelled like coffee and cinnamon. Christopher sat at the table with the shark documentary paused on the television and a marker in his good hand. He looked up when Buck came in.

“You can draw the next star,” Christopher said. He held the marker out. “Dad said you know which ones don’t bleed.”

Buck took the marker. Eddie glanced over his shoulder. The carefulness was still there, but it had room in it now. Buck sat down. The marker was purple. He drew a small star on the edge of the cast, then another, and waited to see if Eddie would tell him to stop. Eddie didn’t. He turned the burner down and set three mugs on the counter, and the morning kept going, ordinary and already different, the way the best things sometimes did.
