The bell over the door of Munson’s Vinyl & Oddities rang the same way it always did: a thin, slightly off-key chime that Eddie had stopped hearing until the third week of October, when Steve Harrington walked in with a cardboard box balanced on one hip.

Eddie was halfway up a stepladder, alphabetizing the new arrivals in the jazz section. He didn’t turn around at first. Most customers announced themselves with either a question or the scrape of a boot. This one just stood there, the box creaking under its own weight.

“Help you?” Eddie said, still sorting. He slid *Kind of Blue* into place and finally glanced down.

Steve’s hair was shorter. Not the carefully styled thing Eddie remembered from the mall or the hospital waiting room, but something practical, still thick enough to fall in his eyes when he tipped his head. He wore a Hawkins Community Center windbreaker that had seen better seasons. The box was labeled, in marker, *records / maybe keep?*

“Hey,” Steve said. His voice was lower than Eddie expected. “Figured you’d still be the guy who knew what was worth anything.”

Eddie climbed down. The ladder wobbled; he caught the edge of the shelf without thinking. “Depends on whether you want money or just somebody to tell you they’re all junk.”

Steve set the box on the counter. The lid was open. Eddie saw the corner of a Led Zeppelin sleeve, a cracked copy of *Rumours*, and something that looked like it had lived in a basement since 1978. He didn’t touch any of it yet.

“How’ve you been?” Steve asked. It wasn’t casual. It was the kind of question people asked when they already knew the answer was complicated.

Eddie shrugged one shoulder. “Alive. Working. Same as most people who got out of Hawkins.” He nodded at the box. “You cleaning out the house?”

“Something like that.” Steve’s fingers rested on the edge of the cardboard. “Mom’s moving. Said if I wanted any of the old stuff, now’s the time. Most of it’s not mine. Just… ended up there.”

They stood on opposite sides of the counter. Outside, a truck idled at the light. Eddie could hear the low rumble through the thin glass. He reached for the Zeppelin and turned it over. The inner sleeve was still there. Someone had written *property of S.H.* in the corner in careful pencil.

“Not bad condition,” Eddie said. “Could get you twenty if the right person comes in. More if you wait for the weekend crowd.”

Steve didn’t look at the record. He was watching Eddie’s hands. “You still play?”

“Sometimes. After close. Keeps the neighbors from complaining if I do it when the street’s empty.” Eddie set the album down. “You still driving that Beemer?”

“Traded it. Got something that doesn’t stall when it rains.” Steve shifted his weight. The windbreaker creaked. “Listen, I don’t actually need the money. I just didn’t know what else to do with them. Figured if anybody would want them, it’d be you.”

Eddie studied him for a second longer than was polite. There was a scar at the edge of Steve’s hairline that hadn’t been there the last time they’d been in the same room. Small. Pale. Eddie didn’t ask.

“I can hold them,” he said. “You can come back if you change your mind.”

“Yeah.” Steve nodded once. “Okay.”

He left the box. The bell rang again when the door closed. Eddie stood with his hands on the counter until the truck at the light finally moved.

---

Steve came back on a Thursday. Not for the records. He bought a coffee from the place two doors down and stood outside Munson’s until Eddie flipped the sign to closed and stepped out to lock up.

“You free?” Steve asked. He held the cup like he wasn’t sure what to do with the second hand.

Eddie had his keys out. The street was quiet except for the buzz of the neon from the laundromat. “Depends.”

“There’s a diner that stays open late. Not great food. But they don’t kick you out if you sit for a while.” Steve glanced at the shop window. “You don’t have to. I just—thought I’d ask.”

They walked three blocks. Steve didn’t fill the silence. Eddie found himself listening to the rhythm of their footsteps on the sidewalk, the way Steve shortened his stride without making it obvious. At the diner, they took a booth by the window. The vinyl was cracked. Steve ordered black coffee and a slice of pie he didn’t touch. Eddie ordered the same and ate half of it while Steve talked, in pieces, about the community center job. Kids who needed somewhere to be after school. A gym that still smelled like the one at Hawkins High. Nothing about the past year. Nothing about the scar.

Eddie told him about the shop. How the owner was an old guy who only came in on Saturdays to count the till and complain about the rent. How Eddie had started keeping a notebook of which records people asked for and never bought. Steve listened with his hands wrapped around the mug, thumbs moving against the ceramic like he was checking for cracks.

When the waitress dropped the check, Steve reached for it. Eddie didn’t argue. Outside, the air had turned sharp. They stood on the sidewalk under the diner’s awning while Steve fished his keys out of his pocket.

“Same time next week?” Steve said. It came out like a question he hadn’t meant to ask out loud.

Eddie looked at the keys in Steve’s hand. The keyring had a plastic fob from a gas station that no longer existed. “Yeah,” he said. “If you’re around.”

---

They met at the diner three more times before Steve asked if Eddie wanted to come by the community center on a Saturday. “We’re painting the locker room,” he said. “Could use another set of hands. Or just company. Whatever.”

Eddie showed up in a flannel with the sleeves already rolled. The center was half-empty. Most of the kids had gone home. Steve was on a stepladder with a roller, paint flecked across one forearm. He didn’t hear Eddie come in until Eddie cleared his throat.

“You always this prepared?” Eddie asked, nodding at the drop cloths.

Steve looked down. There was a streak of pale blue across his cheekbone. “Figured if I made a mess, at least it’d be contained.”

They worked for two hours. Eddie took the low sections. Steve stayed on the ladder. They didn’t talk much. When Steve came down for more paint, he stood close enough that Eddie could smell the detergent on his shirt and the faint chemical edge of the latex. Steve’s hand brushed Eddie’s when he passed the tray. Neither of them moved away immediately.

Later, in the parking lot, Steve sat on the bumper of his car with the door open, drinking water from a plastic bottle. Eddie leaned against the hood. The sun was low. The paint on Steve’s forearm had dried in a cracked line.

“You ever think about leaving?” Eddie asked. “Not Hawkins. Just… going somewhere that doesn’t remember.”

Steve turned the bottle in his hands. “Sometimes. Then I think about the kids who still show up here even when their parents forget to pick them up. And I stay.” He glanced sideways. “You?”

“I left. Sort of. This is as far as I got.” Eddie shrugged. “Turns out distance doesn’t do as much as people say.”

Steve nodded like he understood the part Eddie hadn’t said. They sat until the bottle was empty. When Eddie pushed off the hood, Steve stood too. For a second they were face to face in the narrow space between the open door and the car. Steve’s eyes flicked down, then back up. He didn’t step closer. Eddie didn’t either.

“Drive safe,” Steve said.

“Yeah.” Eddie stepped back. “You too.”

---

The first time Steve stayed after the diner, it was because Eddie’s bike had a flat. They walked it back to the shop. Eddie kept the lights low while he found the patch kit. Steve sat on the stool behind the counter and watched him work, elbows on his knees.

“You don’t have to stay,” Eddie said. The inner tube was already half-fixed. “I can walk it the rest of the way.”

“I know.” Steve didn’t move. “Mind if I do anyway?”

Eddie didn’t answer. He finished the patch, tested it, and rolled the bike toward the door. Steve stood when he did. Outside, the streetlights had come on. Eddie locked the shop. They walked the last two blocks in silence. At Eddie’s building—an old house split into apartments above a closed bakery—Steve stopped at the bottom of the steps.

“You good?” he asked.

Eddie looked at the dark windows. “Yeah. Thanks for the walk.”

Steve nodded. He started to turn, then stopped. “Eddie.”

“Yeah?”

“If you ever need—someone. Not just for a flat tire. You can call.” Steve’s mouth twitched, not quite a smile. “I still have the same number. Or I can give you the center’s. Whatever’s easier.”

Eddie held the bike’s handlebars. The rubber grip was warm from his hand. “I’ll keep that in mind.”

He watched Steve walk back toward the diner where he’d left his car. The street was empty. Eddie stood on the steps until the sound of the engine faded, then carried the bike up and locked the door behind him.

---

It took another month before either of them said anything that couldn’t be walked back.

They were at the center again. The locker room was finished. Steve had started on the storage closet, sorting old equipment. Eddie had come by after closing with two sandwiches from the place that stayed open late. They ate sitting on overturned milk crates, the door propped open for air.

Steve set his sandwich down unfinished. “I keep thinking about that night,” he said. “At the hospital. After. You were already gone by the time they let me in.”

Eddie didn’t pretend not to know which night. “They said you were stable. Figured you’d rather not have an audience.”

“I looked for you.” Steve’s voice was quiet. “Not right away. Later. When I could walk without the nurses yelling. Nobody knew where you’d gone.”

“Didn’t tell many people.” Eddie picked at the crust of his sandwich. “Didn’t want the questions.”

“I get that.” Steve leaned back against the wall. The concrete was cold through his shirt. “I just—wanted you to know. That I looked.”

Eddie was quiet for a long time. Outside, a car passed. The headlights swept across the open doorway and then were gone.

“I thought about calling,” he said finally. “More than once. Never did. Figured if you wanted to find me, you would. And if you didn’t, that was an answer too.”

Steve turned his head. In the low light, the scar at his hairline was almost invisible. “I wanted to.”

“Yeah.” Eddie met his eyes. “Me too.”

They didn’t touch. They finished the sandwiches. When Eddie stood to leave, Steve walked him to the door. On the steps, Steve’s hand settled lightly at the small of Eddie’s back—just enough pressure to guide him past a loose board. Eddie didn’t step away. Steve didn’t pull him closer. They stood there until the automatic light clicked off, then clicked back on when Eddie shifted his weight.

“Next Thursday,” Steve said. “Diner. If you want.”

“I’ll be there.”

Eddie walked to his bike. He didn’t look back until he was at the corner. Steve was still on the steps, hands in his pockets, watching the street. Eddie lifted one hand. Steve lifted his in return. Then Eddie pedaled toward the shop, the night air sharp in his lungs, the streetlights steady overhead.

He didn’t know what would happen next. He knew he would show up on Thursday. That, for now, was enough.
