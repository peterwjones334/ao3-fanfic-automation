Iwaizumi’s hands were steady when he unwrapped the bandage. Oikawa sat on the edge of the treatment table, one leg extended, the other bent, heel hooked on the metal rail. The clinic smelled of menthol and clean cotton. Outside the window, rain streaked the glass over a Tokyo side street that had not existed when they were seventeen.

“Still swelling,” Iwaizumi said. He pressed two fingers just above the kneecap. Oikawa’s breath hitched, short, then evened. “Tell me when it peaks.”

“Now.” Oikawa’s voice was lower than it used to be. The accent had flattened some in the years he had spent between Japan and Argentina, but the edge was still there, the way he turned ordinary words into something that demanded an answer. “It peaks now.”

Iwaizumi nodded and kept the pressure light. He had read the surgical notes three times. Partial tear, clean repair, six weeks of restricted weight-bearing before progressive loading. The surgeon had written that the patient was “highly motivated.” Iwaizumi had not needed the note to know that.

They had not spoken in four years. The last time had been at the airport, Oikawa’s duffel over one shoulder, a ticket in his free hand. Iwaizumi had driven him. They had stood at the curb longer than necessary. Oikawa had said, “Don’t do anything stupid while I’m gone,” and Iwaizumi had answered, “Same to you,” and neither of them had moved until a taxi pulled up behind the car and the driver leaned on the horn.

Oikawa flexed his toes. The motion pulled at the stitches under the dressing. “You can look,” he said. “It’s not going to bite.”

“I’m looking.” Iwaizumi reached for the ultrasound gel. The bottle was cold. He squeezed a line across the joint and spread it with the flat of his palm, careful of the scar. Oikawa’s skin was warm. The muscle of the thigh had thinned on the injured side. Iwaizumi noted the difference without speaking it aloud.

“Physical therapy starts tomorrow,” he said. “We’ll keep it gentle until the graft settles.”

“Gentle is not a word anyone has ever used for me.”

“Get used to it.” Iwaizumi wiped his hands on a towel and stepped back. He wrote the session notes on the tablet while Oikawa sat still, watching the rain. The silence was not uncomfortable. It was the same silence that had lived between them in the club room after practice, when the others had already left and the lights hummed and neither of them had known how to leave first.

The next morning Oikawa arrived ten minutes early. He wore track pants and a hoodie that had been washed until the cuffs frayed. He did the range-of-motion exercises without complaint, even when the flexion reached the point where his mouth tightened. Iwaizumi counted the repetitions. On the twelfth, Oikawa’s hand found the edge of the table and held. Iwaizumi stopped the count.

“Breathe through it,” he said. “Don’t hold.”

Oikawa exhaled. The sound was small. After the set he sat with his head tipped back against the wall, eyes closed. Sweat darkened the collar of the hoodie. Iwaizumi brought him water and a fresh towel. Oikawa took both without looking up.

“They told me the timeline,” Oikawa said. “Six months if everything goes right. Longer if it doesn’t.”

“It will go right.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know you.” Iwaizumi folded the towel and set it on the bench. “You don’t quit things that matter.”

Oikawa opened his eyes. The look he gave Iwaizumi was the same one he had used across the net when they were still on the same side of it: measuring, a little wary, a little hopeful. “You sound sure.”

“I am.”

Outside, a delivery truck idled. The sound came through the glass as a low vibration. Oikawa flexed the knee again, testing the edge of the pain, and Iwaizumi watched the way the muscle engaged under the thin fabric of the track pants. The scar would fade. The habit of caution would not, not for a while.

They worked through the week. Oikawa came every day. On Thursday the rain stopped and the light through the window turned the treatment room the color of weak tea. Iwaizumi adjusted the resistance band and knelt to check the alignment of Oikawa’s foot. Their knees almost touched. Oikawa’s hand rested on his own thigh, fingers curled, not quite relaxed.

“I used to watch your hands,” Oikawa said. The words were quiet. “In high school. When you taped everyone. You were careful even then.”

Iwaizumi kept his attention on the band. “Someone had to be.”

“I didn’t say it then.” Oikawa shifted. The band pulled taut. “I thought if I said it you would hear something else.”

“What else?”

“That I wanted more than the tape.”

The resistance held for three more counts. Iwaizumi released it. He stayed kneeling. The floor was hard under his knee. Oikawa’s sock had a small hole near the toe. Iwaizumi looked at the hole instead of at Oikawa’s face.

“You could have said it,” he said. “I would have heard what you meant.”

“Would you?”

“I was waiting for it.” Iwaizumi stood. His knee cracked. He ignored it. “I waited a long time.”

Oikawa’s laugh was short and not quite steady. “We’re both idiots.”

“Speak for yourself.”

They finished the set. Oikawa walked the length of the room on crutches, then without them, weight shifting carefully onto the healing leg. Iwaizumi walked beside him, close enough that their shoulders brushed when Oikawa swayed. Neither of them moved away.

In the second week the swelling dropped. Oikawa could sit with the knee bent for longer stretches. Between exercises he talked about the team in Argentina, the way the light hit the court at practice, the translator who still mixed up his jokes. He did not talk about the nights after the injury when the hotel room had been too quiet and the phone had stayed dark. Iwaizumi did not ask. He listened to the stories and adjusted the exercises when Oikawa’s form slipped.

On a Friday they stayed late. The other therapists had left. The building settled into the low hum of the HVAC. Oikawa was on the bike, resistance low, pedaling in slow circles. Iwaizumi stood at the control panel and watched the numbers.

“My sister asked about you,” Oikawa said. The chain clicked. “She saw the clinic name on the paperwork. She said you were always the one who made sure I ate.”

“Someone had to.”

“She said I should have called sooner.” Oikawa’s hands rested on the bars. The veins stood out on the backs of them. “I didn’t know what to say. ‘Hi, Iwa-chan, my knee is ruined and I still think about the airport’ didn’t seem like a good opening.”

Iwaizumi turned the resistance down another notch. “It would have been fine.”

“Would it?”

“Yes.”

The bike slowed. Oikawa stopped pedaling. The room was quiet enough that Iwaizumi could hear the small sound of fabric when Oikawa shifted his weight. He looked up. Oikawa was watching him, not the display, not the floor. The look was open in a way it had not been at seventeen.

“I still think about the airport,” Oikawa said. “I think about what I would have said if the taxi hadn’t come.”

Iwaizumi crossed the short distance between them. He rested one hand on the bike’s frame, not touching Oikawa, close enough that the heat of the other man’s arm reached his wrist. “Say it now.”

Oikawa’s mouth curved, small. “I wanted you to come with me. Or I wanted to stay. I didn’t know which one was worse, so I didn’t say either.”

“Both were bad options.”

“Yeah.” Oikawa’s fingers tightened on the bars, then loosened. “I kept the ticket stub. It’s in a book at my place. I look at it sometimes and wonder if you still have the parking receipt.”

“I do.” Iwaizumi’s voice stayed even. “Top drawer of the desk at home. Next to the spare keys.”

They stood like that until the automatic lights dimmed. Oikawa swung his good leg over the bike and stood. The crutches were against the wall. He did not reach for them. Iwaizumi offered an arm. Oikawa took it, palm warm against the inside of Iwaizumi’s elbow, and they walked to the door together.

The recovery stretched. Some days the joint ached in the weather. Some days Oikawa pushed too hard and Iwaizumi made him ice it while he filled out forms at the next table. They ate lunch in the break room when the schedule allowed—rice balls from the convenience store, tea that tasted faintly of the metal thermos. Oikawa complained about the tea. Iwaizumi refilled it anyway.

On the day the surgeon cleared Oikawa for light jogging, they walked the long corridor of the clinic three times. Oikawa’s stride was uneven at first, then smoothed. At the end of the third lap he stopped and leaned against the wall, breathing hard, smiling with his whole face.

“I can do it,” he said.

“I know.”

Oikawa reached out. His hand found Iwaizumi’s wrist, thumb resting over the pulse point. The touch was careful, the same care Iwaizumi had used on the first day with the bandage. “Come over tonight,” he said. “I’ll cook. Or I’ll order something and pretend I cooked. I want you to see the ticket stub.”

Iwaizumi turned his hand so their palms met. The corridor was empty. The light above them buzzed once and steadied.

“I’ll bring the receipt,” he said.

They stood until the buzz faded. Outside, the rain had started again, soft against the glass. Oikawa’s fingers tightened once, then relaxed, and they walked the rest of the way to the exit without letting go.

That night Iwaizumi stood in his own kitchen with the parking receipt in his hand. The paper had yellowed at the edges. The time stamp was still clear. He put it in his pocket and took the train across the city. Oikawa’s apartment was on the third floor of a building that smelled faintly of detergent and old wood. The ticket stub sat on the low table next to two bowls of rice and grilled fish that had clearly come from a shop two blocks away.

They ate on the floor because the table was small. Oikawa’s knee rested on a cushion. When the fish was gone he reached for the stub and turned it over once, then set it down between them.

“I almost threw it away,” he said. “Three times. I kept thinking it was stupid to keep something that small.”

“It’s not small.” Iwaizumi took the receipt from his pocket and laid it beside the stub. The two pieces of paper touched at the corners. “Neither is this.”

Oikawa looked at them for a long moment. Then he leaned sideways until his shoulder rested against Iwaizumi’s. The weight was careful, the same care he had used on the bike when he stopped pedaling. Outside, the rain kept falling. Inside, the receipt and the stub stayed where they were, and neither of them moved to put them away.
