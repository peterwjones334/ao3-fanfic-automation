The hallway outside the locker room still smelled of ice and tape. Shane leaned against the wall with his left ankle elevated on an equipment bag, the wrap already seeping cold through the fabric of his suit pants. The Voyageurs had lost the series in seven. His body had given out in the third period of the last game, a block that went wrong, and the trainers had stopped arguing with him about playing through it once the X-rays came back.

He checked his phone again. Nothing from Hayden. Nothing from his parents beyond the expected “we’re proud of you” texts. The arena was emptying. Security would start clearing the lower level soon.

The door at the far end opened. Ilya stepped through in street clothes, dark jacket over a black shirt, hair still damp. He stopped when he saw Shane, one hand resting on the doorframe like he hadn’t decided whether to keep walking.

“You should not be here,” Shane said.

“I know.” Ilya’s accent sat heavier when he was tired. “Boston flew out two hours ago. I told them I had something to sign.”

Shane looked past him at the empty corridor. “They’ll notice.”

“They notice everything. Does not mean they say it.” Ilya crossed the distance and stopped a careful arm’s length away. His eyes dropped to the ankle. “How bad.”

“Sprain. Bone bruise. Six weeks if I’m lucky.” Shane shifted and the ice pack slipped. Ilya caught it before it hit the floor, then knelt without asking and settled it back in place. His fingers stayed on the wrap a second longer than necessary.

“You played on it.”

“It was game seven.”

“You always say that.” Ilya’s thumb pressed lightly at the edge of the swelling, testing. Shane’s breath caught. “And then you pretend it did not hurt until you cannot walk.”

They had done this for years in hotel rooms and locked bathrooms and once, memorably, in a darkened equipment closet after a charity skate. The sex was easy. The rest of it had never been. Shane had spent most of the last four seasons telling himself the rest of it didn’t exist. Ilya had spent the same years making sure no one could prove otherwise.

Ilya stood. “Come on. Your car is in player lot. I will drive.”

“I can get a car.”

“You can also fall in the parking garage and explain to Yuna why your face is on the concrete.” Ilya held out a hand. Shane took it. The grip was warm and steady and familiar in a way that still made something under his ribs pull tight.

They didn’t talk in the elevator. Ilya kept one palm under Shane’s elbow when the car shifted. Outside, the October air cut across the loading dock. Shane’s driver was still waiting by the SUV. Ilya said something short in Russian to the man—Shane caught only the word for hotel—and the driver nodded and opened the back door.

“Where are we going,” Shane asked once they were moving.

“Your place. Mine is full of teammates who want to drink about losing.” Ilya watched the city slide past the window. “Unless you would rather I leave you at the door.”

Shane looked at the side of Ilya’s face, the line of his jaw, the way his fingers rested loose on his knee. “Stay.”

The apartment was dark when they got in. Shane’s cleaner had been through; the counters were bare, the mail stacked in a precise pile. Ilya kicked his shoes off and went straight to the freezer for a fresh ice pack while Shane lowered himself onto the couch. The ankle throbbed in time with his pulse.

Ilya returned with the pack wrapped in a kitchen towel and a glass of water. He set both on the coffee table, then sat on the floor with his back against the couch so Shane’s leg could rest across his shoulder. The position put Ilya’s head level with Shane’s knee. He adjusted the ice pack without looking up.

“You do not have to do that,” Shane said.

“I know.”

They stayed like that while the ice did its work. The television stayed off. Outside, a siren rose and faded. Shane’s hand found the back of Ilya’s neck without deciding to. Ilya’s hair was still slightly damp. Shane’s thumb moved once, slow, along the hairline.

“I thought about calling you after the second period,” Shane said. “When I knew we were done.”

“I would have answered.”

“I know.” Shane’s voice stayed low. “That’s why I didn’t.”

Ilya turned his head just enough that Shane’s palm rested against his cheek instead of his neck. The stubble there was rough. “You think if you do not say it, it stays small.”

“It is small. We’re careful.”

“We are careful so it can be small.” Ilya’s hand closed around Shane’s ankle, above the wrap, warm and steady. “I am tired of careful.”

Shane’s chest went tight. He had spent years building the version of himself that never slipped in front of cameras, that never let a hand linger too long in a handshake, that answered every question about his personal life with the same polite deflection. Ilya had never asked him to stop. He had only kept showing up in the places where the cameras weren’t.

“Ilya.”

“I watched you go down.” Ilya’s thumb stroked once over the bone of Shane’s ankle. “And I could not go to you. I had to stand there with my mouthguard in and pretend I cared more about the score than whether you got up. I am not good at that anymore.”

Shane closed his eyes. The ice pack had gone soft. Ilya’s shoulder under his calf was solid. “If we stop being careful, people will know.”

“Some people already know.” Ilya’s mouth curved, small and tired. “They just do not say it out loud. Your mother knows. She asked me last year if I was eating enough when I came for dinner. She does not ask strangers that.”

Shane’s hand tightened in Ilya’s hair. “She likes you.”

“She likes that you are less alone when I am around.” Ilya shifted so he could look up. The apartment light caught the faint scar at the edge of his eyebrow from a high stick two seasons ago. “I am not asking you to call a press conference. I am asking you to stop pretending, when it is only us, that this is nothing.”

Shane looked at him for a long moment. The ankle still hurt. The series was over. The apartment was quiet in the way it only ever was when Ilya was in it. He leaned forward until his forehead rested against the top of Ilya’s head.

“Okay,” he said.

Ilya’s breath left him in a short, rough sound. He turned fully, careful of the ankle, and rose onto his knees so they were face to face. His hands settled on Shane’s thighs. “Okay?”

“Okay.” Shane’s mouth found Ilya’s. The kiss was slower than the ones they usually allowed themselves. Ilya’s hands stayed where they were, thumbs moving in small circles against the fabric of Shane’s pants. When they broke apart, Shane kept his forehead against Ilya’s. “Stay tonight.”

“I was going to anyway.”

Later, when the ice pack was back in the freezer and the lights were low, Shane lay on his side with his bad ankle propped on a pillow. Ilya’s arm rested across his waist, heavy and warm. Outside, Montreal kept moving. Inside, neither of them spoke for a long time.

Shane’s fingers traced the ridge of Ilya’s wrist. “Six weeks.”

“I will be here for some of them.” Ilya’s voice was already rough with sleep. “Boston has a stretch of home games. I can drive.”

“They’ll notice that too.”

“Let them.” Ilya’s hand spread wider over Shane’s stomach. “I am tired of driving the other direction.”

Shane closed his eyes. The pain in his ankle had dulled to a steady ache. Ilya’s breathing evened out against the back of his neck. For the first time in four years, Shane let himself stop counting the hours until one of them had to leave.
