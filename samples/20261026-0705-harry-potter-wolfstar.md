The kitchen light stayed on past midnight. Remus left it that way when the full moon was close. He sat at the table with the kettle still warm and a book open to a page he had not turned in twenty minutes. Outside the window the lane was empty. The cottage sat at the end of it, half a mile from the village, and the only sound that reached the table was the wind moving through the hawthorns at the gate.

He heard the motorbike before he saw the headlight. The engine cut. Boots on the path. A knock that was two raps and then silence, as if the person outside had already decided not to wait.

Remus opened the door. Sirius stood on the step with his hands in his jacket pockets and his hair cut shorter than Remus remembered. Rain had darkened the shoulders of the jacket. He looked at Remus’s face, then at the light behind him, then back.

“Still the same kettle,” Sirius said.

“Still the same gate that sticks.”

Sirius nodded. He did not step inside until Remus moved aside. The kitchen smelled of tea and the damp that lived in the walls when the weather turned. Sirius set his bag by the door and stayed near it. His eyes moved over the table, the single mug, the chair Remus had left pulled out.

“I can go to the village if—”

“There’s a room,” Remus said. “It has a bed and a window that doesn’t face the moon.”

Sirius looked at him for a long moment. The rain ticked against the glass. He took off the jacket and hung it on the back of the empty chair. The lining was torn at one cuff. He sat. Remus poured a second mug and set it in front of him. Their fingers did not touch.

They drank. Sirius held the mug with both hands. The steam rose between them and fogged the space above the table. Remus watched the way Sirius’s thumb moved over the chip in the rim and did not ask how long he had been riding.

“They cleared the name,” Sirius said after a while. “Last month. Paperwork.”

“I heard.”

“You didn’t write.”

“I didn’t know where to send it.”

Sirius nodded once. He set the mug down. The chip faced Remus. “The room’s fine.”

He stood. Remus stayed at the table while Sirius carried the bag down the short hall. The door to the spare room opened and closed. The light under it stayed on. Remus turned the page of the book he had not been reading and listened to the pipes settle in the wall.

In the morning the second mug was already on the table when Remus came down. Sirius stood at the sink with his back to the room, sleeves pushed to the elbows, washing the pot Remus had left the night before. The water ran steady. His shoulders were thinner than they had been at seventeen. The scar that crossed the back of his left wrist showed white against the skin.

“You don’t have to—” Remus began.

“I do,” Sirius said. He rinsed the pot and set it in the rack. The water stopped. He dried his hands on the towel and turned. The light from the window cut across his face and left the other half in shadow. “I need something to do with my hands that isn’t a throttle.”

Remus nodded. He poured the tea. They ate toast at the table. Sirius buttered his slice one-handed and left the knife where Remus could reach it without turning. Outside a car passed on the lane and did not slow.

At the end of the week Sirius fixed the gate. He worked with the hinge pin on the ground and the old wood braced against his knee. Remus watched from the step. The rain had stopped. The hawthorns dripped onto the path. Sirius’s shirt stuck to his back where the effort pulled the fabric tight. When the gate swung free he tested it twice, then once more, and left it open.

“It’ll stick again in the wet,” he said.

“It always did.”

Sirius wiped his hands on his trousers. He looked at the open gate, then at Remus. “You used to leave it for me.”

“You used to come through it before dark.”

They stood on either side of the path. The space between them was the width of the gate. Remus’s hands stayed in his pockets. Sirius’s were loose at his sides. A bird landed on the hawthorn and left again. Neither of them moved until the wind picked up and the gate creaked on its new pin.

That night the moon rose full. Remus locked the spare room from the outside and left the key on the kitchen table under the empty mug. He sat on the floor with his back against the door and listened to the sound that was not quite human and not quite wolf. His own hands shook. He counted the minutes the way the healer had taught him after the last transformation that had gone wrong. When the sound stopped he stayed where he was until the light under the door went out.

In the morning Sirius was at the table with two mugs poured and the key beside them. His eyes were red at the rims. He did not look up when Remus came in.

“You didn’t have to sit there,” he said.

“I did.”

Sirius turned the key over once. The metal caught the light. “I heard you counting.”

“Old habit.”

“Mine too.”

They drank. The toast was already buttered. Remus’s hands were steady enough to hold the mug. Sirius watched them and said nothing. Outside the gate stood open. The hawthorns moved in a wind that carried the smell of rain still an hour away.

Three days later Sirius found the scar on Remus’s shoulder. They were moving the bookshelf that had always sat too close to the window. Remus’s shirt rode up when he lifted. The mark ran from the collarbone to the edge of the shoulder blade, pale and raised. Sirius’s hand stopped mid-air. He set his end of the shelf down.

“That one wasn’t there before,” he said.

“No.”

“When.”

“Two years after you left.”

Sirius’s jaw worked. He looked at the scar, then at the floor, then at Remus’s face. “I would have—”

“You weren’t here.”

The words sat between them. Remus pulled the shirt down. The fabric stuck where the sweat had dried. Sirius’s hand stayed half-raised. After a moment he lowered it and took the other end of the shelf again. They moved it the rest of the way in silence. When it was against the wall Sirius stayed crouched with one hand on the wood.

“I’m here now,” he said. The words were quiet. They did not fill the room.

Remus sat on the floor opposite him. His shoulder ached where the scar pulled. “I know.”

They stayed there until the light outside the window went blue. Sirius’s hand rested on the shelf between them, palm down, close enough that Remus could have covered it if he moved. He did not. The radiator knocked once in the wall. Outside the gate creaked and settled.

In the morning Remus woke to the sound of the kettle. Sirius stood at the counter with his back to the room. The second mug was already poured. Steam rose. Remus stopped in the doorway. Sirius turned. The light from the window cut across his face. He looked at Remus’s shoulder, then at his eyes.

“Show me,” he said.

Remus crossed the kitchen. He stopped an arm’s length away. Sirius waited. Remus lifted the hem of his shirt. The scar caught the light. Sirius’s fingers hovered, then settled two inches below the edge of it. The touch was light. It stayed until Remus lowered the fabric of his own accord.

“Again tomorrow,” Remus said.

Sirius nodded. His hand dropped. It did not go far. “Yeah.”

They drank the tea standing. The gate stood open at the end of the path. The hawthorns moved in a wind that no longer carried rain. Inside, the kettle clicked off. Neither of them moved to pour another cup.
