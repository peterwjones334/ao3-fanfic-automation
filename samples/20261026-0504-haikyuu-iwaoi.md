The gym lights hummed overhead, the only sound left after everyone else had gone. Iwaizumi wiped the last of the court with a towel that already smelled like the day, then glanced up. Oikawa was still by the net, bouncing a ball against the floor in a rhythm that had nothing to do with practice.

“You’re going to wear a hole in it,” Iwaizumi said.

Oikawa caught the ball on the next bounce and tucked it under his arm. “It’s still good. Besides, I like the sound.”

They had been doing this for weeks now—staying late under the excuse of extra serves or film review that neither of them actually watched. The others left at the usual time. The managers locked the equipment room. And then it was just the two of them and the empty court, the way it had been since they were twelve and the gym teacher forgot to check if anyone was still inside.

Iwaizumi crossed the court. His knees ached in the specific way that meant tomorrow’s practice would start slower than usual. “Train’s in twenty.”

“I know.” Oikawa spun the ball once between his palms. His hair was still damp at the ends from the showers, curling where it had dried wrong. “You don’t have to wait.”

“I always wait.”

The words came out flatter than Iwaizumi meant them to. Oikawa’s mouth twitched, not quite a smile. He set the ball on the floor and nudged it with his toe until it rolled into the cart. The sound of it hitting the others was soft.

They walked out together. The night air was cold enough that their breath showed. Oikawa’s bag strap kept slipping; he kept hitching it higher with the same shoulder. Iwaizumi watched the motion for three blocks before he reached over and adjusted it himself. His fingers brushed the side of Oikawa’s neck. Neither of them said anything about it.

At the station, the platform was mostly empty. A couple of salarymen stood farther down, checking their phones. The train was late. Oikawa rocked on his heels.

“My knee’s fine,” he said, which was how Iwaizumi knew it wasn’t.

“You iced it?”

“I will.”

“When you get home.”

“Yes, Iwa-chan.”

The train arrived. They boarded the same car out of habit. There were seats. Oikawa took the one by the window and left the one beside him free. Iwaizumi sat. Their knees touched when the train lurched into motion. Neither moved.

Outside, the city blurred. Oikawa’s reflection in the glass looked tired in a way the real one didn’t always show. Iwaizumi watched that instead of the streets.

“You’re quiet,” Oikawa said.

“Thinking.”

“About what.”

Iwaizumi almost said *your knee* or *the match next week* or *how you still serve until your shoulder starts to shake and then pretend it doesn’t*. He said, “Nothing important.”

Oikawa turned his head. The train lights made his eyes look darker. “Liar.”

They got off at the same stop. Their paths split two streets later. Oikawa lived farther from the station. Iwaizumi walked with him anyway, the way he had been doing since the start of the season. When they reached the corner where Iwaizumi usually turned, Oikawa stopped.

“You don’t have to,” he said again.

“I know.”

Oikawa’s hand was in his pocket. Iwaizumi could see the shape of his fist through the fabric. “Then why do you.”

The question sat between them. A car passed. Its headlights swept across Oikawa’s face and then left it in the dark again.

“Because you’d do it alone if I didn’t,” Iwaizumi said. “And you’d stay out longer. And then your knee would be worse and you’d still show up early tomorrow like it was fine.”

Oikawa’s mouth opened, then closed. He looked at the ground. When he spoke, his voice was smaller than usual. “It’s not that bad.”

“It is when you limp on the stairs.”

Silence. Then Oikawa laughed once, short. “You notice everything.”

“I notice you.”

The words were out before Iwaizumi could decide whether they were safe. Oikawa’s head came up. For a second neither of them moved. Then Oikawa stepped closer, just enough that Iwaizumi could smell the soap he used and the cold air on his jacket.

“Iwa-chan,” he said.

Iwaizumi waited. His heart was loud in his ears. He kept his hands in his pockets so he wouldn’t do something stupid with them.

Oikawa’s fingers brushed the sleeve of his jacket, then withdrew. “I’ll ice it. Promise.”

“Okay.”

They stood there another moment. Then Oikawa turned and walked the rest of the way to his building. Iwaizumi watched until the door closed behind him. On the walk home his hands were cold and his chest felt tight in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

The next day Oikawa’s knee was wrapped. He still jumped for the quick sets. Iwaizumi watched the landing and didn’t say anything when Oikawa’s face tightened for half a second before the smile came back. After practice, Oikawa found him by the water fountain.

“It helped,” he said. “The ice.”

“Good.”

“You were right.”

Iwaizumi drank so he wouldn’t have to answer. When he lowered the bottle, Oikawa was still there, closer than he needed to be. The rest of the team was packing up. Someone laughed across the gym. Oikawa’s voice stayed low.

“You can keep noticing,” he said. “If you want.”

Iwaizumi met his eyes. The gym lights were too bright. Oikawa’s face was open in the way it only got when no one else was looking.

“I already do,” Iwaizumi said.

Oikawa’s smile this time was smaller, real. He reached out and fixed the strap of Iwaizumi’s bag the same way Iwaizumi had fixed his the night before. His fingers lingered at the collarbone, warm through the fabric.

“Then keep doing it,” Oikawa said. “I don’t mind.”

They walked out together. The others had already left. Outside, the air was the same cold as the night before. Oikawa’s shoulder bumped Iwaizumi’s on purpose when they turned the corner. Neither of them moved away.

At the station they sat in the same seats. This time when their knees touched, Oikawa left his there. Iwaizumi felt the steady press of it all the way to their stop. When they got off, Oikawa didn’t say Iwaizumi didn’t have to walk him home. He just started walking, and Iwaizumi fell into step beside him like he always had.

Halfway there, Oikawa’s hand brushed his. The second time it happened, Iwaizumi turned his palm up. Oikawa’s fingers slid between his without looking. They stayed that way until the corner.

“See you tomorrow,” Oikawa said. His voice was quiet.

“Yeah.”

Iwaizumi watched him go. His own hand was still warm. He flexed his fingers once, then put them in his pocket and started the walk home. The tightness in his chest was still there, but it felt different now—less like something waiting to break and more like something that might, eventually, be allowed to stay.

The next morning Oikawa was already in the gym when Iwaizumi arrived. He was stretching carefully, the wrap still on his knee. When he saw Iwaizumi, he smiled and held up a small ice pack.

“Brought extra,” he said. “Just in case.”

Iwaizumi crossed the court. He stopped in front of him, close enough that Oikawa had to tilt his head back a little to keep looking at him. The gym was still empty. The lights hadn’t finished warming up.

“You didn’t have to,” Iwaizumi said.

“I wanted to.” Oikawa’s voice was soft. “Is that okay?”

Iwaizumi reached out and took the ice pack. Their fingers brushed. He didn’t pull away.

“Yeah,” he said. “It is.”

Outside, the sky was just starting to lighten. Inside, the court waited. Oikawa shifted his weight, testing the knee, and Iwaizumi stayed where he was, close enough to catch him if he needed it, close enough that neither of them had to pretend anymore that this was only about practice.

They had time. The others would arrive soon. For now, the empty gym was theirs, and the quiet between them felt like the beginning of something neither of them had to name yet. Iwaizumi set the ice pack on the bench and picked up a ball. Oikawa watched him, smile small and real.

“Ready?” Iwaizumi asked.

Oikawa nodded. “Always.”

They started the serves. The sound of the ball hitting the floor was steady. Outside, the city woke up. Inside, they kept going, side by side, the way they always had, only now with the space between them a little smaller, and the promise of the rest of the day still ahead.

Later that week the rain started during third period and didn’t stop. Practice moved inside without discussion. By the time they finished, the streets were dark and the gutters ran full. Most of the team took the covered walkway to the station. Iwaizumi and Oikawa stayed back to lock up.

Oikawa’s knee had been quiet for two days. He still favored it on the stairs. Iwaizumi noticed. He always noticed.

They stood under the overhang while the rain came down hard enough to blur the streetlights. Oikawa’s hair was already damp at the edges from the walk between buildings. He shivered once, small.

“Here,” Iwaizumi said, and shrugged out of his jacket. He held it out.

Oikawa looked at it, then at him. “You’ll be cold.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re always fine.”

“Take it.”

Oikawa took it. He put it on without arguing. The sleeves were a little short on him. He pushed them up and then left them there, wrists bare. The jacket smelled like Iwaizumi’s detergent and the gym. Oikawa pulled the collar closer to his face for a second before he seemed to realize what he was doing and stopped.

They waited for a break in the rain that didn’t come. Finally Iwaizumi said, “We can run.”

“Your knee?” Oikawa asked, automatic.

“Yours.”

Oikawa huffed a laugh. “Fair.”

They ran. The rain soaked through Iwaizumi’s shirt in seconds. Oikawa’s borrowed jacket darkened at the shoulders. By the time they reached the station they were both dripping. The platform was crowded with other people who had made the same calculation. They stood close because there wasn’t room not to. Oikawa’s shoulder pressed against Iwaizumi’s. The jacket was wet through in places; Iwaizumi could feel the cold of it where it touched his arm.

On the train they got seats again. Oikawa sat with the jacket still on. Water dripped from his hair onto the collar. He kept glancing sideways. Iwaizumi kept his eyes on the window so he wouldn’t stare at the way the wet fabric clung at Oikawa’s throat.

When they reached their stop, the rain had eased to a steady drizzle. They walked anyway. Halfway to Oikawa’s building, Iwaizumi’s phone buzzed. He checked it. A message from his mother asking if he wanted dinner kept warm. He typed back that he was on his way, then put the phone away.

Oikawa had stopped walking. “You should go,” he said. “You’re soaked.”

“So are you.”

“I have a dryer.”

“I know.”

They stood under a streetlight. The rain made small sounds on the pavement around them. Oikawa’s hands were in the pockets of the jacket. Iwaizumi could see the shape of them.

“Iwa-chan,” Oikawa said. His voice was careful. “When you said you notice me. Did you mean—”

He stopped. Iwaizumi waited. His heart was doing the same thing it had done on the corner three nights ago.

Oikawa tried again. “I don’t want to mess this up. The team. Us. Any of it.”

“You won’t.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know you.” Iwaizumi stepped closer. The rain was light enough now that he could see the individual drops on Oikawa’s eyelashes. “I know how you get when you think you’re going to lose something. You pull back before anyone can. You smile and you say it’s fine and then you stay late until your knee gives out so you don’t have to think about it.”

Oikawa’s mouth tightened. “That’s not—”

“It is.” Iwaizumi kept his voice low. “And I’m still here. I’ve been here. I’m not going anywhere because you decide to tell me something I already know.”

Oikawa stared at him. The streetlight caught the water on his face. For a moment he looked younger than he was, the way he had when they were kids and he’d skinned his knee and refused to cry until they were alone.

“I like you,” he said. The words came out fast, like if he didn’t say them now he wouldn’t. “Not just—like this. Like I want to keep walking you home even when it’s not raining. Like I want to know if your hands are cold. Like I—” He stopped, breath uneven. “I don’t know how to do this without ruining it.”

Iwaizumi reached out. He took one of Oikawa’s hands out of the jacket pocket. It was cold. He held it between both of his.

“You’re not ruining anything,” he said. “I’ve wanted to do this for months. I just didn’t know if you did.”

Oikawa’s fingers curled around his. “I did. I do.”

They stood there while the rain kept falling, lighter now, almost gentle. Oikawa’s other hand came up and rested against Iwaizumi’s chest, over the wet shirt. Iwaizumi could feel the tremor in it.

“Can I—” Oikawa started.

Iwaizumi leaned in. The kiss was brief, careful, tasting of rain and the mint Oikawa had been chewing after practice. When they pulled back, Oikawa’s eyes were wide. He laughed once, shaky, and pressed his forehead to Iwaizumi’s shoulder.

“Okay,” he said into the fabric. “Okay.”

Iwaizumi’s arms came around him. The jacket was soaked. Oikawa was warm underneath it anyway. They stayed like that until a car turned the corner and its headlights swept over them. Then they stepped apart, but not far. Oikawa kept hold of Iwaizumi’s hand.

“Come up,” he said. “Dry off. I’ll make tea or something. Your mom can wait ten minutes.”

“She’ll wait.”

They walked the rest of the way. At the building door, Oikawa paused with his key in the lock. He looked back. His hair was plastered to his forehead. The jacket hung open. He was smiling, small and real and a little stunned.

“Iwa-chan,” he said. “Thank you. For noticing.”

“Always,” Iwaizumi said.

Inside, the hallway was warm. Oikawa’s apartment was the same as it had been the last time Iwaizumi had been there—volleyball posters, a half-finished assignment on the table, two mugs in the sink. Oikawa found towels. They stood in the small bathroom and dried their hair in silence that didn’t feel heavy. When Iwaizumi’s shirt was too wet to keep wearing, Oikawa lent him one. It was soft and smelled like the same detergent.

They drank tea at the table. Oikawa’s knee was propped on the other chair, ice pack balanced carefully. Iwaizumi watched him adjust it and didn’t offer to help. He didn’t need to. Oikawa knew he was watching. That was enough.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The city lights reflected off the wet pavement. Inside, the kettle clicked as it cooled. Oikawa’s foot nudged Iwaizumi’s under the table. Neither of them moved away.

“Tomorrow,” Oikawa said. “Same time?”

“Yeah,” Iwaizumi said. “Same time.”

They finished the tea. Iwaizumi texted his mother that he was on his way. At the door, Oikawa kissed him again, quicker this time, like he was testing whether it was still allowed. It was. Iwaizumi kissed him back, then stepped into the hallway.

“See you,” he said.

“See you,” Oikawa answered.

Iwaizumi walked home with his hands in his pockets and Oikawa’s shirt under his jacket. The tightness in his chest had settled into something steadier. He flexed his fingers once, remembering the cold of Oikawa’s hand and the way it had warmed. Tomorrow they would practice. Tomorrow the others would be there. Tomorrow they would still be the same two people who stayed late and walked the same streets.

Only now they knew. And that, Iwaizumi thought as he turned the last corner, was enough to start with.
