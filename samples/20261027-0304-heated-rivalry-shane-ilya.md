The ice under his skates was already thinning when Shane Hollander took the hit.

It came late, third period, score tied. Rozanov’s shoulder drove into his chest just outside the crease and the boards answered. Shane’s helmet cracked against the glass hard enough that the sound carried over the crowd. He dropped to one knee, stick skittering away, and for three full seconds the arena noise went thin.

He got up. Of course he got up. The whistle blew, the linesman skated in, and Shane waved off the trainer before the man could finish asking. His left ribs burned where the padding had shifted. He skated to the bench, sat, and kept his mouth shut while the next shift went out.

Across the ice, Ilya Rozanov watched him the entire time. Not the way a rival watched. Longer. Still.

They played the last six minutes without another clean hit between them. Montreal won in overtime on a scramble in front. Shane did not celebrate. He skated straight to the tunnel, unlaced his skates in the visitors’ locker room with careful fingers, and showered until the water ran cold. The bruise was already blooming along his side, purple under the spray.

The hotel was twenty minutes from the rink. Shane took the elevator alone to the fourteenth floor, key card in his teeth while he checked his phone. Three messages from Hayden. One from his mother. Nothing else.

He had just set the deadbolt when someone knocked.

Two short raps. Pause. One more.

Shane opened the door with the chain still on. Rozanov stood in the hallway in a black hoodie and track pants, hair damp, no coat. The corridor light made the scar on his upper lip look deeper.

“You are slow tonight,” Ilya said. His English was careful. “On the boards. I nearly scored.”

“You didn’t.”

“Almost.” Ilya’s gaze dropped to the place where Shane’s hand rested against his ribs, then came back up. “Open the door, Hollander.”

Shane closed it, slid the chain, and opened it again. Ilya stepped inside without waiting. The room was standard: two queens, the far one still made, Shane’s bag open on the near one. He had not turned on more than the entry light.

Ilya looked at the bruise when Shane peeled his shirt off to change. He did not comment. He crossed to the mini-fridge, took out two bottles of water, and set one on the desk beside Shane’s phone.

“Sit,” he said.

Shane sat on the edge of the bed. The mattress dipped when Ilya lowered himself next to him, close enough that their knees nearly touched. For a minute neither of them spoke. The only sound was the low hum of the heating unit and the occasional door closing somewhere down the hall.

“You should have stayed down,” Ilya said at last. “Trainer would have pulled you. You look stupid trying to finish shift like that.”

“You hit me.”

“Yes.” Ilya’s mouth twitched. “I am good at that.”

Shane huffed, half a laugh, then winced. Ilya’s hand moved before either of them seemed to decide it. He pressed two fingers lightly against the edge of the bruise, testing. Shane’s breath caught.

“Sorry,” Ilya said, and did not move his hand away.

They stayed like that. Shane’s skin was warm under Ilya’s fingers. The hotel room smelled like detergent and the faint citrus of Shane’s shampoo. Outside, a car horn cut short. Ilya’s thumb traced a slow arc along the lower edge of the bruise, barely pressure, just contact.

Shane turned his head. Ilya was already looking at him.

“Why are you here?” Shane asked. His voice came out quieter than he meant.

Ilya’s hand stilled. He did not pull it back. “Because you are bad at lying about when you are hurt. And because I am also bad at leaving when I should.”

The admission sat between them. Shane felt the weight of it in his chest, separate from the bruise. He had spent years learning the exact distance Ilya kept in public—the chirps, the shoulder checks, the rare half-smiles that never quite reached his eyes on camera. This was closer. This was the version of Ilya who showed up at hotel doors after games and pretended it was about hockey.

Shane lifted his own hand and covered Ilya’s where it rested against his side. Ilya’s fingers were cold from the water bottle. Shane’s were not.

“I would have left if you told me to,” Ilya said. He was not looking at Shane’s face now. He was watching their hands. “I still will.”

“Don’t.”

Ilya’s shoulders dropped, a small release of tension Shane felt more than saw. He shifted, careful of the bruise, until his forehead rested against Shane’s temple. His breath was warm. Shane closed his eyes and let the contact settle. He could feel the place where Ilya’s pulse beat against his wrist.

They did not kiss. Not yet. Ilya’s free hand came up and settled at the back of Shane’s neck, thumb against the short hair there. Shane turned into the touch the way he turned into a check—on instinct, before he could think better of it. Their mouths met once, brief, then again. Ilya tasted like mint gum and the hotel toothpaste. Shane’s ribs protested when he leaned in; Ilya adjusted without being asked, one arm sliding behind Shane’s back to take the weight.

They ended up on the bed that was still made, Shane on his uninjured side, Ilya facing him. The lights stayed off. Ilya’s hand stayed on Shane’s ribs, protective now instead of testing. Every time Shane shifted, Ilya’s thumb moved in a slow circle, soothing.

“You play tomorrow,” Ilya said into the dark. “Against us.”

“I know.”

“You should ice it.”

“I will.”

Ilya was quiet for so long Shane thought he had fallen asleep. Then: “When you get hit like that and you stay up, I cannot think about the puck. I only think about whether you will get up again.”

Shane’s throat tightened. He reached across the small space between them and found Ilya’s free hand. Their fingers laced. Ilya’s grip was careful, like he was still accounting for the bruise even though he was nowhere near it.

“I’m fine,” Shane said.

“You are not.” Ilya’s voice was low. “But you will be. And I will still be here when you are.”

Outside, the city kept moving. Inside the room, Shane let himself believe it for the length of one night. Ilya’s breathing evened out first. Shane stayed awake longer, counting the places their bodies touched: hands, knees, the careful press of Ilya’s palm over the bruise. When he finally slept, he did not dream about the hit. He dreamed about the knock on the door and the way Ilya had said his name without the usual edge.

In the morning, Ilya was gone before the alarm. A second bottle of water sat on the nightstand, condensation already forming. Shane’s ribs ached when he stood. He iced them in the bathroom sink while he brushed his teeth, and when he looked in the mirror he did not look like a man who had spent the night alone.

The game that night was hard. Shane took another hit, smaller, and got up faster. Across the ice, Ilya watched him the same way he had the night before. When the final buzzer sounded, Montreal up by one, Ilya skated past the bench on his way to the tunnel. He did not stop. He did not speak. But his glove brushed Shane’s as he went by, light enough that no one else would have noticed, and Shane felt the contact all the way down to the bruise that was already starting to fade.

He did not look for Ilya in the hotel hallway that night. He did not have to. When he opened his door at half past eleven, the chain was already off and the second bed was no longer made. Ilya sat on the edge of it, still in his post-game clothes, two water bottles waiting on the desk.

“You were slower on the second shift,” Ilya said. “I nearly scored.”

Shane closed the door behind him. “You didn’t.”

“Almost.” Ilya’s mouth curved, small and real. “Come here, Hollander. Let me see.”

Shane went. The lights stayed low. Ilya’s hands were careful on the new bruise and the old one both. Outside, the city kept its distance. Inside, neither of them asked the other to leave.
